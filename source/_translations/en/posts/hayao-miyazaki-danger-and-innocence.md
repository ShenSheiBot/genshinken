[作者简介] ENOUGH IS ENOUGH!

Hayao Miyazaki is often cast as animation’s kindly patron saint of childhood wonder. The image fits a good deal of his work, but not all of it. *Spirited Away*, perhaps his best-known film outside Japan, won both the Golden Bear—the Berlin International Film Festival’s top prize—and the Academy Award for Best Animated Feature. Yet no single film can contain his range: the science fiction of *Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind* and *Castle in the Sky*; the fantasy of *Princess Mononoke* and *Kiki’s Delivery Service*; the domestic fairy tale of *My Neighbor Totoro*; the history and dream visions of *The Wind Rises*. Then the pig walks in.

*Porco Rosso* sits awkwardly in the familiar portrait. Its hero is a weary middle-aged man whose face has somehow become a pig’s. The film is romantic, melancholy, comic, and surreal, but it is not about a child discovering the world. History has already bruised its pleasures. That makes it a useful counterweight to *My Neighbor Totoro*, the film in which childhood seems most capable of turning ordinary life into enchantment.

The difference is not simply one of age. The two films imagine different ways of living with limits and loss. In *Porco Rosso*, guilt, memory, rivalry, and the knowledge that the past cannot be repaired all give desire its shape. In *Totoro*, illness and fear are real, but the story does not allow loss to become final. A child’s openness to wonder still brings help when help is needed. One world is built around obstacles that cannot be wished away. The other imagines, however briefly, a life before those obstacles have hardened.

Throughout this essay, *innocence* means neither moral purity nor political virtue; nor is it simply ignorance. It is a way of looking that loosens the world from the habits and explanations already attached to it. In *Totoro*, that look belongs first to children whom the story shelters from irreversible loss. In Miyazaki’s visual art, it becomes the power to make familiar things vivid again. The danger appears when history is among the things stripped away: a machine may then seem freshly made, detached from the power that produced and employed it. Narrative protection, visual renewal, and political neutralization do not cause one another. They are distinct uses of a shared imaginative habit, and their consequences depend on what that habit asks us to see—or lets us overlook.

## The pig and the child

Porco lives in a fictionalized aftermath of the First World War. Once an ace pilot, he still takes his red seaplane into the blue above the Adriatic. He shares an intimate but undefined bond with Gina, the widow of a dead comrade, and through a string of mishaps wins the devotion of Fio, a teenage engineer. From the outside, his life is hardly empty. His misery lies in his inability to accept what it offers.

Gina tells Porco’s American rival, Donald Curtis, that she will marry Porco if he comes to see her in daylight. Porco knows how she feels, yet he appears only at night, and only now and then. Their conversations keep returning to the pilots who died in the war, including Gina’s husband. The dead remain between them.

![Porco at the controls of his seaplane, with Gina leaning over the cockpit behind him](attachments/roof-archive/cv2919959/02-porco-rosso-gina.jpg)

This is what makes the film’s romantic life feel adult. It is not a matter of explicit sex, or simply of putting a grown man at the center. Desire has already been shaped by social rules, guilt, and prohibition. In Lacanian psychoanalysis, *symbolic castration* names the subject’s entry into a world of language and social law, where complete satisfaction is impossible and no relationship can restore an imagined original wholeness. The term has a history in psychoanalytic accounts of sexual development, but it does not refer here to a literal bodily threat. It names the limit around which desire takes shape.

Porco has internalized that limit as an accusing voice. Psychoanalysis calls such a voice the superego: the demand that speaks from within even when no external authority is present. The memory of his dead friend works on Porco much as the father’s ghost works on Hamlet. It turns love into a debt. Moving toward Gina would feel like betraying the man who can no longer object, so Porco holds their relationship between intimacy and refusal.

Gina’s daylight condition adds another barrier. The film gives no reason to think she has consciously devised a game, but her condition preserves their distance: the meeting that might settle everything is always one visit away. Lacan’s account of courtly love is useful here. Courtly love exalts the beloved by placing her beyond reach; the obstacle does not merely frustrate desire but helps to create it. Cervantes parodies the structure in *Don Quixote*. Don Quixote turns an ordinary woman, Aldonza Lorenzo, into the exalted Dulcinea, then organizes his adventures around reaching someone who exists for him chiefly at a distance. The joke reveals the mechanism: longing can turn a person into an ideal so long as the actual person remains out of reach.

The film sends desire down several blocked paths. Curtis wants Fio; Fio wants Porco; Porco refuses her. Her age makes the arrangement uncomfortable, as it should: a teenage girl declares her love for a much older man. His refusal neither causes her love nor disguises a flirtation. It is the ethical boundary that prevents his affection from becoming a romance. Unattainability nevertheless intensifies every attachment. Curtis pursues a girl whose attention is elsewhere, Fio idealizes a man who will not play the part she has assigned him, and Porco protects her by refusing to turn her devotion into his reward.

*Neon Genesis Evangelion*, made several years later, stages a revealing variation on this arrangement. Its withdrawn teenage pilot, Shinji, is flanked by the quiet, inaccessible Rei and the combative, demanding Asuka. Both works press sharply opposed forms of intimacy on a male protagonist who would rather retreat, but *Porco Rosso* turns that pressure into adult melancholy rather than adolescent crisis. Gina waits; Fio advances; Porco cannot accept either the settled love one offers or the fantasy of rescue the other projects onto him. Each attachment draws sweetness from the fact that it cannot quite be completed. The characters turn impediment into the very form of love.

Porco’s repeated retreat is not merely prudence. Lacan’s *death drive*—not a wish to die, but a compulsion that persists beyond ordinary calculations of pleasure, safety, and successful adjustment—offers a name for its stubbornness. The term does not ennoble him: his refusals hurt Gina and himself. But the same inability to settle will later help him reject the roles of national hero, obedient citizen, and healed romantic lead.

*My Neighbor Totoro* offers almost the reverse. Satsuki and Mei move to the country with their father while their mother is in the hospital. There they meet Totoro, a forest spirit. When Mei sets out alone to visit her mother and becomes lost, Totoro helps Satsuki find her and brings the sisters close enough to see that their mother is safe. The illness remains, but catastrophe is held at bay.

The film does not deny fear or separation. It does, however, withhold irreversible loss. Its children meet a world in which openness receives an answer: soot sprites, a Catbus, a vast creature asleep under a tree. Rather than sending Satsuki and Mei off to a separate Neverland, Miyazaki brings Peter Pan’s fantasy of childhood freedom into a creaking house, a rainy bus stop, and a family’s anxious wait for news from the hospital. Ordinary life itself becomes the enchanted country.

Psychoanalysis usually describes desire as circling whatever remains out of reach. *Totoro* is remarkable because the marvel answers back. Totoro lets Satsuki ride on him, summons the Catbus, and makes rescue possible. The film gives miraculous aid a warm, furry shape and places it inside the children’s world. Here innocence is first of all narrative protection: fear is allowed in, but final loss is kept outside.

The move from story to visual form is crucial. By *spectacle*, I mean a sequence or image in which looking becomes an event in its own right: the story pauses while a landscape, machine, crowd, or room absorbs our attention. Miyazaki’s science-fiction films supply obvious marvels, but his domestic films work the same magic on kitchens, gardens, old houses, food, and weather. They ask us to inspect the familiar with a child’s avid concentration. The 2010 *Arrietty × Yōhei Taneda Exhibition* at the Museum of Contemporary Art Tokyo followed the same impulse. Taneda, a production designer, rebuilt the tiny heroine’s home at human scale so visitors could enter it. Domestic detail became an inhabitable wonder.

![Totoro, the smaller forest spirits, Satsuki, and Mei sharing a branch above the water](attachments/roof-archive/cv2919959/03-my-neighbor-totoro.jpg)

Mamoru Oshii’s animated spectacles clarify what is distinctive about Miyazaki’s. Oshii, too, lets the plot fall quiet while we look. In *Ghost in the Shell*, the camera drifts through the cramped, Hong Kong-inspired streets of New Port City. In *Ghost in the Shell 2: Innocence*, a festival procession fills the northern city of Etorofu with giant dolls, lanterns, carved façades, and Chinese-inspired temple ornament. The production design compresses crowd, architecture, and decoration into a gorgeous, oppressive mass. In *Patlabor 2: The Movie*, military vehicles enter a wintry Tokyo and seem to vanish into the city they are supposed to secure.

Oshii’s city passages do not make everyday life newly lovable. They make it uncanny. *Ghost in the Shell* asks whether the cyborg is only the person with a mechanical body, or whether the city itself has become a machine that produces order from apparent chaos. *Innocence* keeps reversing the places of human subject and manufactured thing. *Patlabor 2* makes peacetime Tokyo look as if war had always been latent within it: “War? It started a long time ago!”

These sequences approach what Lacan calls the Real: not reality as a whole, but whatever resists the stories and categories that make reality feel coherent. Oshii lets that resistance remain unresolved; the city never settles into a reassuring whole. Miyazaki more often turns strangeness into an invitation, giving the viewer a world to enter and love. Oshii obstructs identification; Miyazaki welcomes it.

Porco’s damaged romances and Oshii’s estranging cities work on different scales, but they apply the same pressure. One makes desire confront a past it cannot repair; the other makes perception confront contradictions it cannot smooth away. Neither allows looking or loving to make a broken world complete. Gloom is not inherently mature. What matters is the refusal of easy wholeness.

## The shadow cast by innocence

The allusion to Peter Pan also brings the limit of protected childhood into view. A child who never grows old is also a child who cannot die. Endless youth sounds delightful until it begins to resemble entrapment. If nothing can end, nothing can acquire the shape that an ending gives it.

The Christian story of the Incarnation and Crucifixion offers one language for the difference. Christianity also promises eternal life, so the relevant contrast is not between salvation and immortality. It is between invulnerability and a salvation that passes through suffering and death. In this account, mortal vulnerability is not merely a defect to be repaired. Finitude is part of what allows a life to matter.

The persistent dark reading of *My Neighbor Totoro* is revealing for the same reason, even though the film does not support it. According to this fan theory, the countryside is a land of the dead; Mei is never rescued; Satsuki dies while searching for her; and the child at the end is only Mei’s spirit. Its proponents point to the sisters’ supposed lack of shadows in the final shots. The details do not make the case.

Yet the theory does something telling to the film. It places a barrier between the viewer and a story of apparently unconditional rescue. Its darkness domesticates what may be stranger than death: a world in which wonder answers need without demanding payment. Lacan calls the disturbing surplus within pleasure *jouissance*—enjoyment pushed beyond comfort. The theory makes that surplus manageable by restoring a familiar bargain in which every gift conceals a cost. Like the obstacle in courtly love, it prevents fulfillment from ever arriving whole. Innocence is allowed to survive only after the viewer has supplied its shadow.

Miyazaki’s words at Isao Takahata’s 2018 memorial offer a less defensive way to hold wonder and loss together. “We lived as fully as we could back then,” he said. The past tense concedes that their shared time is over; the memory of its fullness briefly brings that time to life. One ceremonial sentence cannot explain Miyazaki’s private grief, nor need it. Its relevance is public and precise: remembrance can make a lost world vivid without pretending that the loss never happened.

Several Miyazaki films inhabit this conflict between restoration and separation. *Kiki’s Delivery Service* handles it with unusual grace. Kiki regains her ability to fly, but she can no longer converse with Jiji. Their earlier speech suggests a perfect attunement between them; once Kiki develops a life of her own, that unity cannot simply return.

This is symbolic castration in its least forbidding form: accepting the limit that makes perfect attunement impossible without treating that limit as the end of intimacy. Kiki’s loss also makes room for her new relationships, her work, and her place in town. Growing up means learning that no companion, family, or community can abolish every distance between one person and another.

![Kiki and Jiji flying together above the sea and a coastal town](attachments/roof-archive/cv2919959/05-kikis-delivery-service.jpg)

*Spirited Away* leans toward restoration instead. Both it and *Porco Rosso* use pigs to mark estrangement, but the image works differently in each film. Porco’s face gives visible form to his distance from other people, from his former self, and from the nation that would claim him. The curse hurts, yet it also sets him apart from the society around him. Chihiro’s parents, by contrast, become pigs because adult appetite has swallowed their judgment. Their transformation is a degradation, and their child’s courage reverses it.

Chihiro changes through her adventure; she does not simply return to where she began. Structurally, however, the film restores the family with which it opened. Her parents recover their human form, and the threatened household leaves together. *Kiki’s Delivery Service* builds a new life from a separation that cannot be undone. *Spirited Away* lets childhood courage repair the adult family: its heroine grows, and the family survives intact.

Makoto Shinkai’s *Children Who Chase Lost Voices* carries the other possibility further. Shinkai’s debt to Ghibli is easy to see in the film’s creatures, landscapes, and descent into another world. But where *Spirited Away* restores the family, Shinkai’s adventure refuses resurrection. The dead do not return. Near the end, Asuna admits, “So that was it: I was just lonely.” The line can feel embarrassingly blunt because the scene has not earned its full emotional weight. The thought nevertheless matters. Her journey cannot abolish the emptiness that sent her on it.

Lacan’s notorious formula that “there is no sexual relationship” does not mean relationships are impossible. It means that no partner can complete a subject or close the gap within the self. To “traverse the fantasy” is to give up the belief that some perfect person, object, or restored past will do so. Asuna’s admission reaches for that recognition. The execution falters; the insight does not.

The film’s refusal of resurrection also recalls the Japanese myth of Izanagi, who enters Yomi in search of the dead Izanami and flees after seeing what she has become there. The parallel matters because the myth, too, guards the border between the living and the dead. Like the Christian account above, it reaches by a different route toward the same limit: love grants no exemption from death.

Heidegger’s “being-toward-death” gives that limit a philosophical name. Mortality is not a morbid fixation added to life; it is what gives choices and losses their stakes. Eternity cannot mean finite life merely stretched out forever, because significance emerges from acts that cannot be repeated indefinitely. *Children Who Chase Lost Voices* gives this limit unusual force for a Shinkai romance. Instead of filling Asuna’s loneliness with another unreachable beloved, it brings her close to admitting that no beloved could remove it for her.

![Asuna kneeling beneath a vast, radiant sky in *Children Who Chase Lost Voices*](attachments/roof-archive/cv2919959/06-children-who-chase-lost-voices.jpg)

The question running through these examples is not whether Miyazaki simply accepts or rejects mortality. *Kiki* accepts an irreversible separation; *Porco Rosso* is built from irreparable loss. But a restorative wish recurs elsewhere: childhood wonder repairs the family, renews the everyday, or holds catastrophe at bay. The wish is often beautiful. It becomes dangerous when the innocent eye turns from private loss toward public history and treats political violence as one more wound that a beautiful image can soothe.

## When innocence meets history

*The Wind Rises* puts that danger to its hardest test. The film begins with Jiro Horikoshi as a child, and the adult Jiro retains the same rapt love of airplanes. His desire appears innocent because it springs neither from romantic rivalry nor from a lust for power. He wants to make beautiful things fly. The film asks whether that childhood dream can remain pure inside the military industry of imperial Japan.

Its visual answer is troubling. In one sequence, Jiro watches naval aircraft take off and land while warships, including a Nagato-class battleship, pass behind them. The machinery becomes a cinematic marvel: mass, speed, steel, water, and human ingenuity arranged for the eye. The quieter still below invites a slightly different reading. Jiro is not watching the battleship; he faces ahead as it passes behind him. Yet the composition places engineer and war machine in the same glowing evening landscape. Birds cross the frame, the water shines, and proximity itself becomes serene. Both scenes invite awe before judgment.

![Jiro in the foreground as a battleship passes behind him at sunset in *The Wind Rises*](attachments/roof-archive/cv2919959/07-the-wind-rises-warships.jpg)

This is the operation I will call *neutralization*. It does not make an object genuinely neutral. It brackets the relations that produced the object and the purposes it serves. A warship becomes an exquisite arrangement of material, labor, and intelligence before it registers as an instrument of imperial force, military spending, and organized killing. The intelligence embodied in the machine is real. So is the system that directed it toward war. The image asks us to savor the first while keeping the second beyond the edge of attention.

Hegel’s account of the Understanding names the critical counter-move. The Understanding pulls apart what a harmonious image presents as naturally joined. Here it insists that the aircraft is both a beautiful craft and a product of a militarized world; that skilled labor is both genuinely admirable and directed by an industrial system; that a nation is a historical institution, not a natural home. Neutralization suppresses these distinctions. Criticism restores the relations that the ravishing image lets us forget.

The plot performs a related abstraction. Jiro remains faithful to his childhood dream, but the dream becomes a career inside the imperial military-industrial system. The film’s clearest victim of state persecution is Castorp, the German guest at Jiro’s mountain hotel who openly condemns Hitler. Japanese authorities threaten to deport him to Nazi Germany, and he goes into hiding. The danger he faces is real. Yet this choice relocates dissent: the Japanese victims of Japan’s own police state remain out of view.

The imperial state’s Special Higher Police, or Tokkō, arrested communists and other leftists in the nationwide March 15 crackdown of 1928. By 1935, further arrests had destroyed the Communist Party’s remaining underground organization. Detainees were imprisoned and tortured; some were killed. Castorp need not stand in for these victims. The point is that the film acknowledges repression through a foreign anti-Nazi while leaving the imperial state’s domestic targets unnamed. Political brutality remains legible, but its displacement keeps Japan’s domestic repression at a safe distance from Jiro’s story.

Walter Benjamin’s phrase “the aestheticization of politics” helps explain the risk. Power is offered first as a sensory experience, allowing us to admire its machinery and choreography apart from the domination they organize. The political danger begins when visual unity turns collective force into seamless spectacle and screens out the conflicts, commands, and victims that made the spectacle possible. An “innocent” gaze then does political work precisely by seeming to stand outside politics.

In an unequal struggle over who rules, suspending judgment does not suspend power. It leaves existing power in place as the default. This does not mean that a beautiful warship orders the viewer to support empire. The effect is smaller and more plausible. The composition directs attention toward design and engineering while withholding command, use, and consequence. Nothing within the frame recalls the people subjected to that power, so empire recedes into scenery—as if it were weather rather than a contested human order.

Isao Takahata’s *Grave of the Fireflies* offers a limited but useful comparison. Its focus on two Japanese children makes wartime suffering intimate and devastating. That child-centered view is not the visual neutralization at work in *The Wind Rises*, but it creates a related absence: war descends on the children almost like a natural disaster. The decisions of Japan’s political and military leaders, the history of Japanese occupation, and the suffering Japan inflicted elsewhere in Asia remain outside the story’s frame. The children’s pain is no less real. The comparison matters precisely at this narrow point: one film abstracts military machinery from its uses, while the other isolates civilian suffering from much of its political cause.

*Triumph of the Will* makes the formal danger stark. Leni Riefenstahl’s film openly glorifies a Nazi rally; *The Wind Rises* is an ambivalent fictional drama about compromised dreams. They are not equivalent in purpose or guilt. The point of the limit case is visual: Riefenstahl turns massed bodies and monumental architecture into thrilling geometry, making organized power appear as beautiful motion. Undisguised propaganda reveals a capacity of visual form that remains harder to see in gentler work—the capacity to make domination feel like harmony.

The meanings of innocence can now be separated. In *Totoro*, innocence shelters a child from final loss. In Miyazaki’s domestic spectacles, it frees familiar things from habit and restores them to attention. In *The Wind Rises*, a similar suspension of familiar meaning can also suspend historical cause. These are neither synonyms nor stages in a progression. What links them is Miyazaki’s habit of clearing away what seems settled so that we can look again. That habit shelters, renews, or neutralizes according to its object and context; a kitchen and a warship do not become innocent in the same way, or with the same stakes.

Miyazaki’s innocent eye is therefore neither fraudulent nor harmless. Its gift is concrete. It makes a meal look nourishing, wind feel palpable, and the labor of hands absorb our attention; it restores the thrill of flight. But when the same eye rests on a war machine, its power to lift an object out of habit can also lift it out of history. Innocence stops being a refuge from damaged adult life and becomes a way of letting the existing order pass as background.

## Better a pig than a fascist

Neutralization works by detaching beautiful objects from the relations that made them. That is why labor, rather than a verdict on Miyazaki’s politics, is the right place to continue. The work his films bring close—and the work they leave at a distance—shows how the detachment happens.

His films teem with labor at a human scale: delivery, cooking, cleaning, repair, and craft performed by small groups whose members remain visible as individuals. They rarely follow those intimate tasks into the mass workforce created and divided by large-scale industry, with its classes, chains of command, and separation of workers from the purposes their work serves. Even the aircraft industry in *The Wind Rises* often resembles an enlarged workshop. *Porco Rosso* goes further, placing sophisticated aircraft production in a charming Milanese shop that feels artisanal beside the industrial world around it.

The shop’s charm proves nothing by itself about Miyazaki’s politics, but it does reveal an aesthetic preference. Miyazaki makes a complex system legible by showing recognizable people using their hands; the impersonal organization of capital and military production slips out of focus. The work is real, and so is the pleasure of watching it. What becomes harder to see is the system that joins the workshop to the war machine.

That limitation makes *Porco Rosso* all the more valuable. Porco’s pig face is not simply a punishment for greed. It figures his estrangement from his former self, from other people, and from the nation that would claim him. Yet estrangement gives him political clarity. “Better a pig than a fascist” is not the slogan of an innocent who knows nothing of politics. It is the judgment of a cynic who understands the available loyalties and refuses them.

So far, *alienation* has mostly meant Porco’s emotional and social distance. In a broader sense, it is the fact that a person never fits perfectly into either nature or the social identities on offer. The loss of perfect belonging hurts. It also prevents culture, nation, and historical circumstance from telling us completely who we are. Because we never fit seamlessly into the order that formed us, we can refuse the place it assigns. Freedom does not require perfect belonging. It begins with the mismatch that makes such belonging impossible.

The same thought clarifies Heidegger’s idea of *thrownness*: no one chooses the world, history, or language into which they arrive. But being formed by a world does not mean owing that world allegiance. Heidegger’s own praise of “the inner truth and greatness of this movement”—Nazism—is the warning case. He mistook historical destiny for a command. Porco takes the opposite course. The world has made him a pilot, a veteran, and an Italian, but none of those facts can compel his loyalty to fascism.

*Porco Rosso* is not a master key to every film Miyazaki made, and it cannot explain earlier works as though they were reactions to a film that did not yet exist. It is better understood as a fault line running through his cinema. On one side lies the wish to heal alienation through childhood wonder, restored families, harmonious little communities, and loving attention to things. On the other lies the knowledge that estrangement can be ethically necessary.

Miyazaki’s gift is his ability to make the everyday visible again. His danger is that visibility can become absolution: once a thing has been made beautiful, the history inside it may disappear. Porco never settles cleanly into the sky, the nation, the workshop, or a fantasy of romantic repair. His guilt is not automatically noble, and the stubborn repetition that Lacan calls the death drive wounds him as much as it sustains his refusal. But it keeps him from mistaking reconciliation for innocence. He rejects the fascist state; he also refuses to treat Fio’s teenage devotion as his salvation. Gina’s love is real, but it cannot undo the dead.

No single obstacle gives Porco his freedom. What matters is the persistent mismatch between him and every role that promises to make him whole. That small, painful distance lets him say no. Freedom begins there—not in innocence without a shadow, but in the refusal to let beauty erase contradiction.
