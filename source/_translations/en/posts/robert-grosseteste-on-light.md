## About the Author

> Robert Grosseteste (ca. 1168–1253), Bishop of Lincoln from 1235 to 1253, was one of the most prominent and remarkable figures in thirteenth-century English intellectual life. He was a man of many talents: commentator and translator of Aristotle and Greek patristic thinkers, philosopher, theologian, and student of nature. He was heavily influenced by Augustine, […] from whom he drew a Neoplatonic outlook, but he was also one of the first to make extensive use of the thought of Aristotle, Avicenna and Averroes. He developed a highly original and imaginative account of the generation and fundamental nature of the physical world in terms of the action of light, and composed a number of short works regarding optics and other natural phenomena, as well as works of philosophy and theology. […] He made a powerful impression on his contemporaries and subsequent thinkers at Oxford, and has been hailed as an inspiration to scientific developments in fourteenth-century Oxford.
>
> — Neil Lewis, [“Robert Grosseteste,” *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, Fall 2020 archive](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2020/entries/grosseteste/), excerpted to follow the Chinese edition’s selection.

**Introductory discussion from the Chinese edition:** Grosseteste may be said to exemplify a distinctive feature of English scholasticism: its emphasis on scientific and mathematical knowledge, as this treatise shows. From this angle, the metaphysics of light in *On Light* is undoubtedly closer to Aristotle’s account of the heavens and physics. Conversely, it can also be seen as an attempt to prove Plato’s theory of Forms. For example, the proposition that “the infinite multiplication of a simple being must produce a finite quantity”—an idea resembling the mathematical theory of limits—is highly valuable for resolving problems in the theory of Forms.

## Robert Grosseteste’s *On Light, or the Beginning of Forms*

*Newly translated from the Latin of [Ludwig Baur’s 1912 edition, pp. 51–59](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/lweb/digital/collections/cul/texts/ldpd_7395671_000/), with the readings specified in “Notes on the English Text” below. The six notes inherited from the Chinese edition retain their separate attributions.*

I take the first corporeal form, which some call corporeity, to be light (*lux*). For light by its own nature diffuses itself in every direction, so that a sphere of light, however large, is generated instantaneously from a point of light, unless something opaque obstructs it. Corporeity necessarily entails the extension of matter in three dimensions, although both corporeity and matter are in themselves simple substances, lacking any dimension. A form that is itself simple and dimensionless could not introduce dimension in every direction into matter that is likewise simple and dimensionless unless it multiplied itself, diffused itself instantaneously in every direction, and extended matter in its own diffusion. For form cannot abandon matter, since it is inseparable from it, nor can matter be deprived of form. Yet I have proposed that this operation—multiplying itself and diffusing itself instantaneously in every direction—belongs to light by its own nature. Whatever performs this operation, then, either is light itself or performs it through participation in light, to which the operation intrinsically belongs. Corporeity, therefore, either is light itself or performs the operation described, introducing dimensions into matter by participating in light and acting through its power. But the first form cannot introduce dimensions into matter through the power of a form subsequent to itself. Light, then, is not a form subsequent to corporeity: it is corporeity itself.

Furthermore, the wise hold that the first corporeal form is more exalted, more excellent and noble in essence, and more like separately existing forms than all the forms that follow it. Light, however, is more exalted, noble, and excellent in essence than all corporeal things, and more closely resembles separately existing forms—the intelligences—than all bodies do. Light is therefore the first corporeal form.

Thus light, the first form created in prime matter, multiplied itself by its own nature an infinite number of times on every side and spread equally in every direction. At the beginning of time it extended matter, which it could not leave behind, drawing it out along with itself into a bulk as great as the fabric of the world. This extension of matter could not have occurred through a finite multiplication of light, because something simple, repeated a finite number of times, does not generate a quantity, as Aristotle shows in *On the Heavens*.[^on-light-1] Multiplied infinitely, however, it must generate a finite quantity, because the product of the infinite multiplication of something exceeds infinitely that from whose multiplication it is produced. Yet one simple thing does not infinitely exceed another; only a finite quantity infinitely exceeds something simple. For an infinite quantity exceeds something simple infinitely, an infinite number of times. Light, therefore, which is itself simple, must, when multiplied infinitely, extend matter, likewise simple, into dimensions of finite magnitude.[^on-light-2]

An infinite aggregate of numbers can stand to another infinite aggregate in every numerical ratio, and also in every non-numerical ratio. Some infinities are greater than other infinities, and some smaller. The aggregate of all numbers, both even and odd, is infinite, and it is greater than the aggregate of all even numbers, which is nevertheless infinite: it exceeds that aggregate by the aggregate of all odd numbers. The aggregate of numbers successively doubled from unity is also infinite; likewise, the aggregate of all the halves corresponding to those doubles is infinite. The aggregate of these halves must be half the aggregate of their doubles. Similarly, the aggregate of all numbers successively tripled from unity is three times the aggregate of all their corresponding thirds. The same is plainly true of every kind of numerical ratio: one infinite aggregate can stand to another in any of these ratios.

Suppose, however, that we take an infinite aggregate of all the successive doubles from unity and an infinite aggregate of all their corresponding halves, and remove unity or any finite number from the aggregate of halves. After this subtraction, the first aggregate will no longer stand in a two-to-one ratio to what remains of the second. Nor will any numerical ratio remain. For if subtracting from the smaller term of a numerical ratio leaves another numerical ratio, what is subtracted must be an aliquot part, or several aliquot parts, of that from which it is subtracted. But a finite number cannot be an aliquot part, or several aliquot parts, of an infinite number. Thus, when a number is subtracted from the infinite aggregate of halves, no numerical ratio remains between the infinite aggregate of doubles and the remainder of the infinite aggregate of halves.

Given this, it is clear that light, through its infinite multiplication, extends matter into smaller and larger finite dimensions standing to one another in any ratio, numerical or non-numerical. For if light, through its infinite self-multiplication, extends matter to a dimension of two cubits, doubling that infinite multiplication will extend it to four cubits, and halving it will extend it to one cubit. The same holds for the other numerical and non-numerical ratios.

This, I think, was what the philosophers meant who held that everything is composed of atoms, saying that bodies are composed of surfaces, surfaces of lines, and lines of points. This view does not contradict the view that magnitude is composed only of magnitudes, because “whole” has as many senses as “part.” A half is called a part of a whole in one sense, since taken twice it gives the whole. A side is part of a diagonal in another sense: no whole-number multiple of the side equals the diagonal, though the side taken a certain number of times falls short of it. Again, an angle of contingence is called a part of a right angle in another sense: it is contained in the right angle infinitely many times, yet subtracting it a finite number of times diminishes the right angle. A point is part of a line in yet another sense: it is contained in the line infinitely many times, but subtracting it a finite number of times does not diminish the line.

Returning, then, to my argument, I say that light, by multiplying itself infinitely and equally in every direction, extends matter equally on all sides into a spherical shape. This extension necessarily entails that the outermost parts of matter are extended and rarefied more than the innermost parts near the center. When the outermost parts have been rarefied to the utmost, the inner parts will still be capable of further rarefaction.

By extending prime matter into a spherical shape in this way and rarefying its outermost parts to the utmost, light brought matter’s potentiality to completion in the outermost sphere, leaving it incapable of receiving any further impression. Thus the first body, called the firmament, was perfected at the sphere’s outer boundary, with nothing in its composition except prime matter and first form. It is therefore the simplest body with respect to the parts constituting its essence, and the greatest in quantity. It differs from body considered as a genus only in that its matter is completed through first form alone. Body as a genus, present in this and other bodies and having prime matter and first form in its essence, abstracts both from matter’s completion through first form and from its diminution through first form.

Once the first body, the firmament, had been completed in this way, it spread its light (*lumen*) from every part of itself toward the center of the whole. For light (*lux*) is the perfection of the first body and naturally multiplies itself from that body; it therefore necessarily diffuses toward the center of the whole. Since this light is a form wholly inseparable from matter, in diffusing from the first body it extends along with itself the spirituality of that body’s matter. Thus from the first body proceeds light (*lumen*), which is a spiritual body—or, if you prefer, a corporeal spirit. In passing, this light does not divide the body through which it passes, and so it passes instantaneously from the body of the first heaven to the center. Its passage is not to be understood as one and the same individual passing instantaneously from heaven to the center—for that is perhaps impossible—but occurs through its self-multiplication and the infinite generation of light (*lumen*).[^on-light-3] This light, spread from the first body and concentrated toward the center, gathered together the mass of matter beneath the first body.[^on-light-4] Since the first body, being complete and unchangeable, could no longer shrink, and no empty place could arise, this gathering necessarily extended and dispersed the outermost parts of the mass. The inner parts consequently became denser, while the outermost parts became more rarefied. The power of the light that gathered and, in gathering, separated was so great that it made the outermost parts of the mass contained beneath the first body tenuous and rarefied to the utmost. Thus, in these outermost parts, a second sphere came into being, complete and incapable of receiving any further impression. Such is the completion and perfection of the second sphere: light (*lumen*) is generated from the first sphere, and light (*lux*), simple in the first sphere, is doubled in the second.

Just as the light (*lumen*) generated from the first body completed the second sphere and left a denser mass beneath it, so the light generated from the second sphere perfects the third and, by gathering matter together, leaves a still denser mass beneath the third sphere. This gathering that disperses proceeded in the same order until nine celestial spheres were completed and a dense mass was gathered beneath the ninth and lowest sphere to serve as the matter of the four elements. The lowest sphere, the sphere of the moon, also generated light (*lumen*) from itself. Through this light it gathered the mass contained beneath it and, in gathering it, made its outermost parts tenuous and dispersed them. But this light lacked the power to disperse those outermost parts to the utmost through its gathering. Every part of this mass therefore remained imperfect and capable of undergoing gathering and dispersal. The uppermost part of the mass, dispersed but not to the utmost, became fire through its dispersal, while still remaining the matter of the elements. This element generated light from itself and gathered the mass beneath it, dispersing that mass’s outermost parts, though less than fire itself had been dispersed; in this way it produced air. Air, too, generated from itself a spiritual body or corporeal spirit, gathered what it contained, and, by gathering it and dispersing its outer parts, produced water and earth. But because more of the gathering power than of the dispersing power remained in water, water, like earth, remained heavy.

In this way the thirteen spheres of this sensible world were brought into being: nine celestial spheres, incapable of alteration, increase, generation, or corruption because they are complete; and four with the opposite properties, capable of alteration, increase, generation, and corruption because they are incomplete. It is evident that every higher body, through the light (*lumen*) generated from it, is the form (*species*) and perfection of the body that follows. And just as unity is potentially every subsequent number, so the first body, through the multiplication of its light, is every subsequent body.

Earth, however, is all the higher bodies through the gathering of their lights within it. Hence the poets call it Pan, meaning “all.” It is also named Cybele,[^on-light-5] as though *cubile*, from *cubus*, a cube, signifying solidity, because earth is the most compressed of all bodies. This is Cybele, mother of all the gods: although the higher lights are gathered within earth, they have not manifested themselves there through their operations. Yet the light of whichever sphere you choose can be drawn out of earth into act and operation; thus any of the gods will be brought forth from it as from a mother. The intermediate bodies stand in two relations. To the lower bodies they stand as the first heaven does to all the rest; to the higher bodies, as earth does to all the others. Thus, in certain respects, each contains all the rest.

Light is the form (*species*) and perfection of all bodies, but in the higher bodies it is more spiritual and simple, and in the lower bodies more corporeal and multiplied. Nor are all bodies of the same kind (*species*), although they have proceeded from light, single or multiplied—just as numbers are not all of the same kind, although they are constituted by greater or lesser multiplication of unity.

Perhaps this account makes clear what is meant by those who say that “all things are one through the perfection of one light,” and by those who say that “the things that are many are many through the different multiplication of light itself.”

Since lower bodies participate in the form of higher bodies, a lower body, through sharing the same form as a higher body, can receive motion from the same incorporeal motive power that moves the higher one. Thus the incorporeal power of the intelligence or soul that moves the first and highest sphere with a daily motion moves all the lower celestial spheres with that same daily motion. But the lower they are, the more weakly they receive this motion, because the lower a sphere is, the less pure and the weaker the first corporeal light within it.

Although the elements participate in the form of the first heaven, they are not moved with a daily motion by its mover. Although they participate in that first light, they do not obey the first motive power, for their light is impure and weak, far removed from its purity in the first body. They also have the density of matter, which is the principle of resistance and disobedience. Some nevertheless think that the sphere of fire revolves with a daily motion, taking the revolution of comets as evidence. They also say that this motion extends even to the waters of the sea, causing the tides. Yet all who philosophize rightly hold that earth is exempt from this motion.

In the same way, all the spheres following the second sphere—which, when counted upward, is generally called the eighth—participate in its form and therefore share its motion, the motion proper to them besides the daily motion.

Because the celestial spheres are complete and incapable of rarefaction or condensation, the light (*lux*) within them does not incline the parts of their matter away from the center to rarefy them, or toward the center to condense them. Thus the celestial spheres cannot receive upward or downward motion, but only circular motion from an intellectual motive power that, reflecting its gaze bodily back upon itself, turns the spheres in bodily revolution. The elements, however, are incomplete and capable of rarefaction and condensation. The light (*lumen*) within them therefore inclines them either away from the center to rarefy them or toward the center to condense them. Hence they are naturally capable of upward or downward motion.

In the highest body, the simplest of bodies, four things can be found: form, matter, composition, and the composite.[^on-light-6] Form, being utterly simple, occupies the place of unity. Matter rightly receives the nature of two because of its twofold capacity—susceptibility to impressions and receptivity to them—and also because of density, which is rooted in matter and belongs first and principally to two. Composition contains three within itself, for in it we find matter endowed with form, form embodied in matter, and the distinctive character of composition itself, which in every composite is found as a third thing, different from matter and form. The composite as such, over and above these three, falls under the number four. Thus four is present in the first body, in which all other bodies are present through its power, and consequently the number of the other bodies, considered at its root, does not exceed ten. For the unity of form, the two of matter, the three of composition, and the four of the composite together make ten. Hence ten is the number of the bodies of the world’s spheres: although the sphere of the elements is divided into four, it is one through participation in a corruptible earthly nature.

From this it is clear that ten is the perfect number of the universe. Everything whole and perfect contains something corresponding to form and unity, something to matter and two, something to composition and three, and something to the composite and four. No fifth constituent can be added beyond these four. Therefore everything whole and perfect is ten.

It is clear from this that only the five ratios found among these four numbers—one, two, three, and four—are suited to composition and to the concord that gives stability to every composite. Hence these five ratios alone are harmonious in musical measures, gestures, and rhythmic durations.

*Here ends the Bishop of Lincoln’s treatise on light.*

### Notes on the English Text

The following notes are those of the present English edition.

- **Latin basis.** The translation follows Baur’s historical edition, with his correction at 55.17, *subtiliaret et rarefaceret*, and the departures specified below. It is not based on Cecilia Panti’s [2013 critical edition](https://art.torvergata.it/handle/2108/74341). Manuscript sigla follow Baur.

- **Infinite aggregates.** At 53.12–13, “infinite to infinite” follows *infinitum ad infinitum* in R, D, P, and V, rather than Baur’s printed *finitum ad infinitum*, “finite to infinite.” The preceding comparisons of infinite aggregates support this reading. [Baur’s apparatus, p. 675](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/lweb/digital/collections/cul/texts/ldpd_7395671_000/gallery/images/ldpd_7395671_000_00000873.jpg).

- **Spheres and elements.** At 55.25, “beneath” follows *infra* in R, F, and D rather than *intra*, “within”; at 55.29 it follows F’s *infra* rather than *super*, “above.” At 56.5–8, the translation follows V in omitting the passage from *et sic produxit* through the subsequent *disgregatione*, which otherwise generates fire a second time before air. These are also the readings inherited through Riedl by the Chinese edition. The contrast between the nine complete celestial spheres and four incomplete elemental spheres, omitted in Chinese, is retained from the Latin. [Baur’s apparatus, pp. 676](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/lweb/digital/collections/cul/texts/ldpd_7395671_000/gallery/images/ldpd_7395671_000_00000874.jpg)–[677](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/lweb/digital/collections/cul/texts/ldpd_7395671_000/gallery/images/ldpd_7395671_000_00000875.jpg).

- **Parts and wholes.** “Atoms” here are geometrical indivisibles. An *aliquot part* measures its whole an integral number of times. “Diagonal” renders Latin *diameter*; Baur reports the reading *quadrati*, “of a square,” in D and P. An *angle of contingence* is the curvilinear angle between a circle and its tangent, not an angle between two straight lines. [Baur’s apparatus, p. 675](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/lweb/digital/collections/cul/texts/ldpd_7395671_000/gallery/images/ldpd_7395671_000_00000873.jpg); [Euclid, VII, definitions 3–5](https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookVII/defVII3.html), and [III.16](https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookIII/propIII16.html).

- **The returning gaze and matter’s twofold character.** “Reflecting its gaze bodily back upon itself” retains Baur’s *corporaliter* at 58.2; Ma reads *incorporaliter*, “incorporeally.” “Density” retains Baur’s *densitatem* at 58.13; Ma and D read *divisibilitatem*, “divisibility.” [Baur’s apparatus, p. 678](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/lweb/digital/collections/cul/texts/ldpd_7395671_000/gallery/images/ldpd_7395671_000_00000876.jpg).

- **Light and composition.** *Lux* generally names light as first corporeal form; *lumen*, light generated and propagated from a constituted body. Neither “reflection” nor “refraction” is a uniform equivalent for *lumen*. The completion of a celestial sphere does not prevent it from generating light or receiving circular motion. *Composition* and *the composite* distinguish composition from what is composed; the Chinese translator’s sixth note gives a separate interpretation.
