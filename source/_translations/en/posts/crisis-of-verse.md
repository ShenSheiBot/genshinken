> **Chinese editor’s introduction:** “Crisis of Verse”[^1] is selected from *Stéphane Mallarmé: De la lettre au livre* (*From the Letter to the Book*), compiled by Pierre-Henry Frangne (Le mot et le reste, 2010), and originally appeared in Mallarmé’s *Divagations*. The Chinese text was translated from French, with reference also to the English versions by Barbara Johnson and Robert Greer Cohn. Like the storm he saw outside his window, Mallarmé discerned turmoil on the eve of change in fin-de-siècle French literature: Hugo’s death had finally reduced the alexandrine to a historical relic, while emerging free verse and Symbolism arriving from across the Atlantic were gathering irresistible momentum. Mallarmé placed great hopes in both. For him, this wave was not merely a weapon for sweeping away the old metres and Naturalism: he also sought to build, at the century’s end, a new “Poetry” that would unite words and music, sight and hearing, yet have no centre.

> **Chinese edition’s selection notice:** This is an abridged Chinese translation. It omits the sentence about Verlaine following the Hugo paragraph in the 1897 *Divagations* edition of “Crise de vers,” and the original essay’s final paragraph.

**English edition’s source note:** Mallarmé’s prose is translated directly from [*Divagations* (Paris: Bibliothèque-Charpentier, Eugène Fasquelle, 1897), pp. 235–251](https://archive.org/details/divagations00mall), retaining the Chinese edition’s selection and arrangement. This is not the 2010 Frangne edition cited above. The introduction, captions and twenty-one numbered annotations are translated from Chinese; the illustrations are those of the Chinese edition.

Besides the two announced exclusions, the selection omits the comparison with fugues and sonatas on p. 245, the sentence extending the Work’s organisation beyond a single volume on p. 247, and part of that page’s qualification about complementary forms. These cuts are retained. The self-quotation from *Music and Letters* and its author’s note are identified as in 1897. The Chinese commentary’s interpretations remain its own; new clarifications are labelled separately.

A moment ago, with a slackening gesture, weary from bad weather that makes one afternoon after another hopeless, I let fall again the slender strands of multicoloured beads that overlay the booklets’ shimmer in the bookcase with yet more rain—not with any curiosity, but as though I had read everything twenty years ago. Many a work, beneath the curtain’s glass beads, will line up its own scintillation: I like to follow flashes of storm-light there, as I do through the window in the ripe sky.

Our recent phase, if it is not closing, pauses—or perhaps becomes conscious of itself: a certain attentiveness releases the creative and relatively assured will.

Even the press, which takes twenty years to get its information, suddenly attends to the subject precisely on time.

Literature here undergoes an exquisite crisis—fundamental.

Anyone who grants this activity a place, or the foremost place, recognises the event of the moment: at a century’s close we witness, not upheavals as at the close of the last, but, away from the public square, a disturbance of the temple veil, with significant folds and something of a tear.

A French reader, his habits interrupted by Victor Hugo’s death, cannot but be disconcerted. Hugo, in his mysterious task, brought all prose—philosophy, eloquence, history—under verse; and since he was verse in person, he all but confiscated the right to speak from anyone who thinks, discourses or narrates. A monument in that desert, with silence stretching far around; thus, in a crypt, the divinity of a majestic, unconscious idea: that the form called verse is simply literature itself; that there is verse as soon as diction acquires emphasis, rhythm as soon as there is style. Verse, I believe, respectfully waited for the giant who made it one with his tenacious, ever firmer blacksmith’s hand[^2] to be gone, before itself breaking apart. The whole language, fitted to metre and recovering its living articulations there, escapes through a free disjunction into a thousand simple elements; and, as I shall indicate, not without resemblance to the many cries of an orchestration that remains verbal.

A witness to this adventure, in which others wanted me to play a more active role, though such a role befits no one, I gave it, at least, my fervent interest; and the time has come to speak of it, preferably at a distance, just as it took place almost anonymously.

Grant that French poetry, because of the primacy it gives rhyme in enchantment, has proved intermittent throughout its evolution down to us: it shines for a while, exhausts that interval, and waits. Extinction—or rather, wear that lays bare the weave, repetitions. The need to make poetry, in opposition to changing circumstances, now means that after one of those periodic orgiastic excesses lasting almost a century, comparable only to the Renaissance, when shadow and cooling should take their turn—not at all!—the brilliance changes, continues: the retempering, ordinarily hidden, takes place in public, through recourse to delightful approximations.

I believe I can distinguish three degrees in the treatment of verse’s hieratic canon.

This prosody, its rules so brief, is all the more intractable: it prescribes this or that precaution, the hemistich among them, and lays down the least effort needed to simulate versification, like codes according to which refraining from theft is, for example, the condition of uprightness. Precisely what need not be learned; not to have guessed it for oneself, from the outset, proves the futility of submitting to it.

Those faithful to the alexandrine, our hexameter, loosen from within the rigid, childish mechanism of its measure; the ear, freed from an artificial counter, delights in discerning for itself every possible combination of twelve timbres.

Consider how very modern the taste is.

An intermediate case, by no means the least curious, is the following.

The poet of acute touch who still regards this alexandrine as the definitive jewel, but one to be brought out—sword, flower[^3]—only rarely and for some premeditated reason, touches it almost shyly or plays about it. He grants it neighbouring chords before presenting it, magnificent and bare: often letting his fingering falter at the eleventh syllable or extend to a thirteenth. M. Henri de Régnier[^4] excels at these accompaniments—his own invention, I know, discreet and proud as the genius he has brought into being, and revealing the performers’ passing unease before the inherited instrument. Something else, or simply the opposite: a deliberate mutiny shows itself while the old, tired mould lies unused, as when Jules Laforgue[^5] first initiated us into the unmistakable charm of faulty verse.

So far, in either of the examples cited, there is nothing but restraint and letting go, owing to weariness from overuse of the national cadence, whose use, like that of the flag, must remain exceptional. With this amusing peculiarity, however: deliberate infractions or learned dissonances appeal to our delicacy, whereas scarcely fifteen years ago they would have infuriated the pedants we still were, as though faced with some ignorant sacrilege! I will say that the memory of strict verse haunts these games played beside it and lends them an advantage.

All the novelty lies in free verse—not what the seventeenth century assigned to fable or opera, which was merely an arrangement, without the stanza, of various recognised metres, but what we should properly call “polymorphous.” Let us consider the official count now dissolving into whatever one wishes, without limit, provided a pleasure recurs within it. Sometimes a euphony fragmented with the assent of the intuitive reader, with an ingenuous and precious rightness—M. Moréas[^6] lately; or a gesture, languid with reverie, starting up with passion, that marks the rhythm—M. Vielé-Griffin;[^7] before them, M. Kahn,[^8] with a highly accomplished notation of the tonal value of words. I give names—there are other representative ones, MM. Charles Morice,[^9] Verhaeren,[^10] Dujardin,[^11] Mockel[^12] and all the rest—only as evidence for what I say, so that readers may turn to the publications.

What is remarkable is that, for the first time in any people’s literary history, alongside the great communal pipe organs of the centuries, on whose latent keyboard orthodoxy exalts itself, anyone, with individual playing and hearing, can compose an instrument, once he blows into it, brushes or strikes it with skill; use it on his own, and dedicate it also to Language.

An exalted freedom has been won, the newest: I see no erasure of anything beautiful in the past, and this remains my intense conviction. I remain persuaded that on grand occasions we shall always obey the solemn tradition whose pre-eminence belongs to the classical genius; only, where there is no call to disturb the venerable echoes for a sentimental outburst or a narrative, we shall think twice before doing so. Every soul is a melody whose thread must be taken up again; and for this each has a flute or viol.[^en-viol]

![The Afternoon of a Faun](/attachments/roof-archive/cv19846696/faun-afternoon.png)

In my view, a true condition emerges late—or the possibility not merely of expressing oneself, but of modulating oneself at will.

Languages are imperfect in being several; the supreme one is missing.[^13] Since thinking is writing without accessories, without even a whisper, yet still the immortal word left unspoken, the diversity of idioms on earth prevents anyone from uttering the words that otherwise would be, at a single stroke, materially truth itself. This prohibition holds expressly in nature—we come up against it with a smile—leaving no grounds for considering oneself God. But, turning for the moment to aesthetics, my sensibility regrets that discourse fails to express objects through touches answering to their colouring or bearing, touches that exist in the instrument of the voice, among languages and sometimes within a single one. Beside opaque *ombre* (“shadow”), *ténèbres* (“darkness”) grows little darker; what a disappointment, this perversity that gives *jour* (“day”) a dark timbre and *nuit* (“night”) a bright one, contrary to their meanings. The wish for a term denoting splendour to shine, or for the reverse, for it to go out: simple alternations of light—*only*, let us understand, *verse would not exist*: verse, philosophically, compensates for the defect of languages, a superior complement.[^14]

A strange mystery; and with intentions no less great, metre sprang forth in the days of incubation.

Let a moderate span of words, within the eye’s grasp, arrange itself in definitive strokes, with silence alongside.

If, in the French case, private invention does not surpass the prosodic inheritance, displeasure would nevertheless erupt if a singer could not wander apart and, as his steps lead him through infinity, pluck little flowers wherever his voice finds a notation… The attempt has just been made; apart from learned investigations still announced in this direction—accentuation, and so on—I know of nothing but a seductive game played with recognisable fragments of the old verse, now eluding it, now disclosing it, rather than a sudden discovery wholly alien to it. Time to loosen the constraints and curb the zeal that led the school astray. Precious indeed: but to reckon on more from this liberation, or seriously to suppose that every individual brings a new prosody partaking of his breath—and, of course, some new orthography—the joke laughs out loud—or inspires the theatrics of preface-writers. Resemblance between lines, and the old proportions:[^15] a regularity will endure, because the poetic act consists in suddenly seeing an idea divide into a number of motifs equal in value, and in grouping them. They rhyme: their common measure, made manifest by the final stroke, serves as an outward seal.[^16]

The crisis lies less in this most interesting treatment of versification, in its rest and interregnum, than in our untried conditions of thought.

To hear the indisputable ray—as shafts gild and tear a meander of melodies: or Music joins Verse to form, since Wagner, Poetry.[^17]

![Portrait of Richard Wagner](/attachments/roof-archive/cv19846696/wagner.png)

Not that either element cannot, to its advantage, move apart towards an integrity triumphant in its own right: the concert mute insofar as it articulates no words, the poem giving utterance. Their communion and retempering bring instrumentation into the light, evident beneath the veil, as utterance descends into the evening of sonorities. The modern meteor, the symphony, approaches thought, at the musician’s will or unbeknown to him; thought no longer lays claim solely to ordinary expression.

Some explosion of Mystery[^18] through all the heavens of its impersonal magnificence, in which the orchestra could not but influence the ancient endeavour that had long claimed to render it through the race’s voice alone.

A consequent double sign—

Decadent, Mystic—the Schools, whether self-proclaimed or hastily labelled by our information press, find their meeting-point in an Idealism that rejects natural materials and, as brutal, an exact thought arranging them, retaining of anything only suggestion. To establish an exact relation between images,[^19] and let a third aspect detach itself, fusible and clear, offered to divination… Abolished, the pretension—an aesthetic error, though it governed masterpieces—to include in a volume’s delicate paper anything other than, for example, the horror of the forest, or the mute thunder scattered through the foliage: not the intrinsic, dense wood of the trees. A few outbursts of inward pride, truthfully trumpeted, awaken the architecture of the only habitable palace, free of any stone over which the pages would not close properly.

“Monuments, the sea, the human face, in their native fullness, retain a power of attraction no veiling description can match. Evocation, you say; *allusion*, I know; *suggestion*: this somewhat haphazard terminology attests a tendency, perhaps among the most decisive literary art has undergone; it bounds that art and releases it. What is its own enchantment, if not to free, from a handful of dust or reality, without enclosing it in the book, even as text, the volatile dispersal that is spirit, which has no use for anything beyond the musicality of everything?”[^mallarme]

Speaking has to do with the reality of things only commercially: in literature, it is content to allude to that reality, or to draw away a quality of things that some idea will embody.

On this condition song takes flight, nothing but a lightened joy.

This aim I call Transposition—Structure, another.[^20]

The pure work implies that the poet disappears from utterance, yielding the initiative to words, set in motion by the collision of their inequality. They light one another with reciprocal reflections, like a virtual trail of fire across precious stones, replacing the perceptible breathing of the old lyrical breath or the enthusiastic personal direction of the sentence.

[fig] Chimera of Notre-Dame: Nikolai Tarkhov, 1902.

![Chimera of Notre-Dame](/attachments/roof-archive/cv19846696/notre-dame-chimera.png)

An ordering of the book of verse, neither innate nor found everywhere, eliminates chance; such an ordering is still needed if the author is to be omitted. Now a subject, inevitable, requires a particular accord with its corresponding place among the pieces assembled in the volume. Responsiveness, because a cry has an echo—motifs of the same set will balance, poised at a distance: neither the sublime incoherence of Romantic page-layout nor that artificial unity once allotted wholesale to a book. Everything becomes suspension: a fragmentary arrangement with alternation and facing parts, contributing to the total rhythm, which would be the poem kept silent in the white spaces, rendered, in a way, only by each pendentive.[^en-pendentive] An instinct, I mean, glimpsed in certain publications; and this time, in poetry, where a shattering and harmonious fullness asserts itself, the young stammered the magical concept of the Work.[^21]

A chimera: to have thought of it testifies, in the reflection from its scales, to how powerfully the present cycle, or last quarter-century, is struck by an absolute flash—whose streaming disturbance the dishevelled shower at my windowpanes wipes away, until it illuminates this: that, more or less, all books contain a fusion of a limited number of repetitions; indeed, there might be only one book—the world’s law—a bible such as nations simulate. The difference between one work and another offers so many readings proposed in an immense contest for the true text, among the ages called civilised—or literate.

Certainly I never sit on the concert benches without perceiving, amid the obscure sublimity, some sketch of one of the poems immanent in humanity, or their original state, all the more comprehensible for being unspoken and because, in determining the vast outline, the composer found it easy to suspend even the temptation to explain himself. I imagine, with a writer’s prejudice doubtless ineradicable, that nothing will remain unuttered; that we have reached precisely this point: confronted with the breaking of the great literary rhythms, discussed above, and their dispersal into articulated shivers close to instrumentation, we seek an art that will complete the symphony’s transposition into the Book, or simply reclaim what is ours. For it is not from elementary sonorities produced by brass, strings and woodwind, but unmistakably from intellectual speech at its height, that Music must emerge in fullness and clarity, as the totality of relations existing in everything.


An undeniable desire of my time is to separate, as though assigning them different functions, the two states of speech: raw or immediate here, essential there.

To narrate, to teach, even to describe, is all very well; although perhaps, to exchange human thought, it would suffice for each of us silently to take a coin from another’s hand or place one in it. The elementary use of discourse serves the universal *reportage* in which, literature excepted, every genre of contemporary writing participates.

Yet what use is the marvel of transposing a fact of nature, through the play of speech, into its almost-disappearance in vibration, if not so that the pure notion may emanate from it, unhampered by a nearby or concrete reminder?

I say: a flower! And beyond the oblivion to which my voice consigns every contour, as something other than the known calyces, there rises musically—idea itself, and sweet—the flower absent from all bouquets.

Rather than serving as ready, representative currency, as the crowd first uses it, saying—above all, dream and song—recovers in the Poet, through the constitutive necessity of an art devoted to fictions, its virtuality.[^en-virtuality]

### Notes

The twenty-one annotations of the Chinese edition are translated below. Mallarmé’s source note and the English translator’s notes and additions are explicitly identified.

[^1]: Mallarmé distinguishes three concepts in this essay: “verse” (*vers*), “poem” (*poème*) and “Poetry” (*Poésie*). In most instances, “verse” here refers specifically to the alexandrine, the traditional French form with twelve syllables to a line, divided into two equal half-lines, or hemistichs. During the Pléiade period, the alexandrine acquired a “double-pause” phrasing that gave verse greater fluidity; Hugo later developed this practice further. The “crisis of verse” discussed here occurred precisely after Hugo’s death: French readers accustomed to counting syllables as they read suddenly had to confront an entirely new form—free verse.

    **English translator’s clarification:** French alexandrines count syllables, not stresses. Mallarmé’s “our hexameter” gives the alexandrine a standing analogous to the classical six-foot line; it is not a technical description of French verse as English-style hexameter. His use of *vers* also extends beyond the alexandrine.

[^2]: This adapts an image from Rimbaud’s poem “The Blacksmith” (*Le Forgeron*); the “blacksmith” is the poet.

    **English translator’s clarification:** The identification of a Rimbaud allusion is the Chinese annotator’s interpretation, not an explicit citation by Mallarmé.

[^3]: “Flower” and “sword” are a recurrent pair of images in Mallarmé, whose use of them generates greater tension than Baudelaire’s. For Mallarmé, “sword” (*glaive*) is no different from “flower” (*fleur*): the etymological source of *glaive* is actually “flower”—the Latin for a wild iris is *gladius*. In “The Tomb of Edgar Poe,” the poet describes Poe awakening in his tomb with a “naked sword” (*un glaive nu*), an expression that gives Poe both the sword’s destructive beauty and the flower’s feminine beauty.

    **English translator’s clarification:** The etymology runs otherwise: Latin *gladius* means “sword”; its diminutive *gladiolus* supplies the plant-name. The wording in this essay is *épée, fleur*, not *glaive, fleur*. The sword–flower interpretation remains the annotator’s. See the [Académie française’s account of the plant-name](https://www.academie-francaise.fr/le-glaieul-et-lespadon). In [“The Tomb of Edgar Poe”](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Les_Po%C3%A8mes_d%E2%80%99Edgar_Poe/Le_Tombeau_d%E2%80%99Edgar_Poe), Poe rouses his century with the sword; the poem does not describe him waking in his tomb.

[^4]: Henri de Régnier (1864–1936), a poet of late French Symbolism, elected to the Académie française in 1912.

    **English translator’s clarification:** Régnier was elected on 9 February 1911; his reception took place on 18 January 1912. [Académie française biography](https://www.academie-francaise.fr/les-immortels/henri-de-regnier).

[^5]: Jules Laforgue (1860–1887), a French poet born in Uruguay. His poetry combines Symbolist and Impressionist features.

[^6]: Jean Moréas (1856–1910), a Greek Symbolist poet writing in French. In 1886 he published the “Symbolist Manifesto” in *Le Figaro*, singling out Baudelaire, Verlaine and Mallarmé and their replacement of naturalistic description with subjective perception.

[^7]: Francis Vielé-Griffin (1864–1937), originally named Egbert Ludovicus Viele, a French poet born in the United States. A Decadent poet who made innovative use of free verse.

[^8]: Gustave Kahn (1859–1936). Kahn was the first poet to explain free verse theoretically, and also claimed to have invented it. Although its inventor is difficult to establish, Kahn unquestionably deserves to be counted among its pioneers. He first set out a theoretical account of free verse in the preface to his collection *Early Poems* (*Premiers poèmes*, 1897). His poetics sought to break the constraints of traditional metre and find rhythm in the movement of poetic thought.

    **English translator’s clarification:** The 1897 preface explicitly revisits earlier explanations, so it was not his first theoretical statement. Kahn also rejects “polymorphous verse,” the term Mallarmé proposes here, in favour of “free verse.” Their positions should not be treated as identical. [Kahn’s preface](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Premiers_po%C3%A8mes/Pr%C3%A9face).

[^9]: Charles Morice (1860–1919), a French Symbolist poet.

[^10]: Émile Verhaeren (1855–1916), a Belgian poet writing in French. His first collection, *Flemish Women* (*Les Flamandes*), had a fresh naturalistic character, but this soon gave way to a dark, obscure Symbolist style. Verhaeren was also among the earliest French-language poets to use free verse.

[^11]: Édouard Dujardin (1861–1949), a French Symbolist poet, a disciple of Mallarmé and one of the pioneers of stream of consciousness.

[^12]: Albert Mockel (1866–1945), a Belgian Symbolist poet writing in French; a member of the Royal Academy of French Language and Literature of Belgium.

[^13]: Mallarmé acutely recognised the distinctions between language, writing and speech sounds: in his view, poetry has no speech sounds, and rhythm and music must be found outside traditional metre.

[^14]: For Mallarmé, rhythm serves to compensate (*complément supérieur*) for writing’s defect—its lack of speech sounds.

    **English translator’s clarification on notes 13–14:** These notes offer the Chinese annotator’s account. The French passage begins with the plurality of languages, not an absence of sound, and makes verse—not rhythm alone—the agent that compensates for their defect. Its examples explicitly concern vocal timbre.

[^15]: The “old proportions” (*vieilles proportions*) are the alexandrine.

[^16]: The original has *sceau extérieur*. The translator takes this as a vivid image: the shared metre binds words together in the poem, as sealing wax joins an envelope’s flap to the envelope.

[^17]: In Mallarmé’s theory, writing and music have a common source, “la Poésie” / “la Musique.” At this source, sound and writing, poetry and music, are one, as in Wagner’s music. In the translator’s view, Mallarmé’s term has ambitions far beyond the literature of the late nineteenth century: he wants to construct a total art synthesised from multiple, parallel senses. In other words, no single form or sense occupies an absolute centre in “la Poésie,” despite Mallarmé’s repeated emphasis on “Music.” This can be seen in the synaesthesia of this passage: “One hears light when its traces tear through a meander of melodies and gild it.”

    **English translator’s clarification:** This centreless synthesis is the annotator’s interpretation. Mallarmé’s following paragraph also allows music and verse to achieve separate integrity; later he explicitly acknowledges a writer’s prejudice in claiming Music for intellectual speech. The quotation above translates the Chinese annotator’s rendering.

[^18]: That is, *explosion du Mystère*, a kind of originating Big Bang. Robert Greer Cohn points out that Mallarmé also mentions this “Big Bang” in *A Throw of the Dice* (*Un coup de dés*), but my French is so lousy that I really cannot make sense of this extraordinary poem; readers will have to consult it for themselves.

[^19]: This is Mallarmé’s proposal to “eliminate uncertainty/chance” (*hasard*). “Chance” is a chaotic phenomenal world, filled with fleeting matter and language decaying in everyday life. The poet’s task—at the end of the nineteenth century—is to build a mysterious, transcendent world of authenticity and return to the unified, harmonious source.

[^20]: “Transposition” means freeing spirit from the material fetters of Naturalism. The subsequent task is “Structure”: words illuminate one another, forming a transcendent world. This world is anti-metaphysical, although it sounds very much like Plato’s *logos*.

[^21]: The “Work” / “Book” (*l’Œuvre*) is another of Mallarmé’s original concepts. He never completed this book that would contain all these differences.

[^mallarme]: Extract from *Music and Letters* (*La Musique et les Lettres*). — **Mallarmé’s note, restored from the 1897 edition, p. 246.**

[^en-viol]: **English translator:** The French *viole* can name a viol or, in nineteenth-century usage, a viola. See the [Académie française’s 1878 entry](https://www.dictionnaire-academie.fr/article/A7V0617).

[^en-pendentive]: **English translator:** A pendentive is an architectural element supporting a dome and mediating between it and the structure below. See [Viollet-le-Duc’s entry on *pendentif*](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_raisonn%C3%A9_de_l%E2%80%99architecture_fran%C3%A7aise_du_XIe_au_XVIe_si%C3%A8cle/Pendentif).

[^en-virtuality]: **English translator:** “Virtuality” here concerns the powers speech can realise beyond its practical use as representative currency—not digital existence or mere unreality. “Fictions” likewise extends beyond invented stories to artistic making.
