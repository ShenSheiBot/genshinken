*English edition’s note:* This edition translates will’s Chinese adaptation, not a verbatim Japanese transcript. Lin Qinnan is better known as Lin Shu; invoking him signals literary rewriting as well as excerpting. The idioms, allusions, and changes of register belong to this adaptation and should not be assumed to reproduce Karatani’s Japanese wording. Quotations are translated from the Chinese unless otherwise noted. The underlying lecture dates to [January 9, 2015](https://www.kinokuniya.co.jp/c/label/20141204162950.html). The excerpt begins partway through it, and its references to “today” and to earlier discussion belong to that setting.

And so, although I did not want to reflect on myself, I have ended up looking back over my work. Simply reconsidering that one book, *Origins of Modern Japanese Literature*, brings a great deal into view. But I no longer feel like actively thinking about literature. I do not even have today’s half-dead sort of interest in it. Although *Origins of Modern Japanese Literature* was a critique—an examination—of literature, it was, after all, written when literature possessed a special value. Today, however you criticize it, there is no point. Lately, literature has become worthless. Faculties of letters, for example, have been gradually disappearing from Japanese universities. There is no question that literature’s standing has plummeted. Today, only writers whose books sell command respect. Those whose books do not sell—heh, nobody even looks at them. Is this literary judgment? It is more like the economic logic of the capitalist market.

Things used to be different. Literature had few readers, but even with so few, there was nothing to fear. “Even if nobody reads it now, one day it will find its way into the world.” People who made literature their vocation once held this faith: literature would not die; tenuous but unbroken, it would endure. Kyojin Ōnishi, whom I mentioned earlier, is one example. He kept revising his work until he died. Yutaka Haniya was another. He never revised his work, but he wrote *Dead Spirits* from the 1930s through the 1980s in the same unwavering style. Their attitudes were the same. Though their works were bounded by a particular time and place, a close reading shows that their concerns were still universal. And they were utterly indifferent to their contemporaries’ judgments. You could say they were thinking about their reputations centuries into the future.

![Photograph of Yutaka Haniya](attachments/roof-archive/cv13590618/figure-1.jpg)

For them, literature had become something very close to religious faith. I do not think this was a good attitude, but that faith certainly persisted until the 1990s. It was not confined to those two writers: everyone with a connection to literature believed in it. Even writers of popular fiction believed in “pure literature.”[^pure-literature] That faith is gone now. In its place is the economic logic of the capitalist market. In this atmosphere, I gradually lost the inclination to practice literary criticism.

But I have no desire to restore faith in literature, either. A younger friend who teaches English literature at the University of Tokyo told me that his students neither read novels nor knew how to read them. So he first had them read *Harry Potter*, then coaxed them toward literature. Hearing this, I felt I could not have been more right to stop living off the academic bureaucracy and strike out on my own.

Actually, I had felt something similar before. When I went to teach in America in the 1970s, I discovered that more than half my students read literature only in university classrooms. I was dumbfounded. Literature is not something you can learn at school. So I thought Americans did not understand literature at all. But on reflection, perhaps America once had a literary heyday of its own. Quite apart from the classics, modern American literature probably did, at some point, have an independent readership outside the academy. Then, somewhere along the way, nobody read literature anymore. That was why students had to be forced to read it at school. And then came these things called creative-writing classes. Seen this way, Japan is merely following in America’s footsteps.

But when I say I quit literature, it is not simply because literature declined. I never practiced literary criticism in the narrow sense to begin with. If literary criticism means merely discussing literary works, then I have never done it. Literary criticism can take up subjects other than literature. Philosophy, religion, economics, history—all can be approached through its methods. Literary criticism can criticize anything, provided it is a written text. People working in philosophy, economics, or history can probably do little beyond their own discipline; entering other fields is difficult for them. Literary criticism, by contrast, can discuss anything. I chose it because of my intellectual ambitions.

So when I say I quit literature, I do not mean literature in the narrow sense. It is not a matter of quitting literature to take up philosophy or literary theory. I had already done philosophy and theory, and I was tired of them too. When I say I quit literature, I mean I quit philosophy and theory along with it.

I quit literature. That was what I thought at the end of the twentieth century. I had just finished *Transcritique: On Kant and Marx*. The “critique” in the title really means “(literary) criticism.” Reading Kant and Marx and drawing new meanings out of them—that is “criticism.” It is almost exactly the same thing as literary criticism.

![Cover of the Japanese edition of Kojin Karatani’s Transcritique](attachments/wechat/zl6RQfs3ZMAQPbqgIGeCfw/body-001.jpg)

Yet this book was different from my earlier work. *Transcritique* brought out the intellectual position I had held all along. That is what the title literally means. *Transcritique* is a concept I invented; you will not find it in an English dictionary. The *trans-* means both “transcendental” and “transversal.” The former is Kant’s method, a vertical inquiry into the a priori and the empirical; I put greater emphasis on the latter. It means connecting horizontally—in other words, movement, a shift or displacement. The Chinese translation of the title is rather interesting: 跨越性批判. I cannot pronounce Chinese, but when I read the characters with their Japanese pronunciations, 跨 suggests a horizontal crossing, and 越 going beyond. The image of a cross comes to mind.

In this book, I argue that Kant and Marx gained their critical force through their “movements.” I did not first discover this while writing the book. I had already discussed it in *Marx: Towards the Centre of Possibility*, which I wrote in the 1970s. Scholars generally regard Marx’s thought as a synthesis of German classical philosophy, French political theory, and English economics. But Marx’s thought is not an all-purpose, grand unified theory “applicable” to all three fields. His theory is actually a form of criticism. We can see this only by attending to changes in the space of his discourse—his logic. The move from Germany to France and then to England was not merely geographical. Marx did not decide for himself to make these “moves.” Harsh political circumstances forced him. He had to flee for his life.

In the second half of the 1990s, I too began to feel that I was moving. Certainly not because I enjoyed it. With the dissolution of the Soviet Union and the end of the Cold War, I gradually felt that I had moved somewhere different. It was during this period that I wrote *Transcritique*. As I said earlier, the book reads Kant and Marx, but in the end I wrote something neither Kant nor Marx would ever have written. It was writing by “seizing the embryo and changing the bones.”[^remaking] This was the theory of modes of exchange. I replaced Marx’s history of social formations organized by modes of production with a history organized by modes of exchange. This was not yet obvious in *Transcritique*, but it became clear in *The Structure of World History*, written ten years later.

Still, the idea came to me while I was writing *Transcritique*. This is where you can see my “movement.” It was not merely theoretical. In fact, as soon as I finished the book, I started NAM, the New Associationist Movement. My attitude had therefore changed fundamentally. That is what I mean by quitting literature. Why at precisely this moment? Before explaining that, I want to talk about what literary criticism actually is.

I said earlier that literature once had a special value and subsequently lost it. What does that mean? We ought to reverse the question itself. When, and in what way, did literature acquire that special value? This is what we must understand first.

Broadly speaking, this change began in Europe in the second half of the eighteenth century. Both religion and philosophy had generally denied the value of sensibility—sensation and feeling. But as commerce and industry flourished and the natural sciences developed, modern philosophy began to value sensation. Before this, knowledge founded on sensation had been regarded as deceptive illusion. In the eighteenth century, people began to value feeling too. Feeling, too, had always been devalued.

For example, a character in the Korean television drama *My Beloved Sister* (*누나*, Japanese title *姉さん*), which I mentioned earlier, says, “As long as human beings have feelings, literature will not end.” But in earlier times, probably no country valued feeling. Only in the modern age did it come to be valued. The principal reason, put simply, was the modern market economy.

In Britain in the second half of the eighteenth century, the Industrial Revolution was in full swing. A new perspective emerged alongside it. Adam Smith’s sympathy is one example. Although Smith discussed free competition—laissez-faire—in economic life, he also worried about inequality between classes. This sets him apart from today’s neoliberals. Smith is generally regarded as an economist, but he was always a moral philosopher. At the heart of the new ethics he advocated was “sympathy.” Its resemblance to the pity and compassion of older religions is deceptive. Smith’s sympathy is the “imagination” through which we put ourselves in another person’s place. In contrast to an ethical community, this is actually an affirmation of individualism. “Imagination” in fact presupposes atomized individuals, separated from one another, a condition rooted in the modern market economy. Sympathy arises through the destruction of premodern community—which is also the elevation of individualism. It is therefore different from religious compassion and pity.[^sympathy]

Sympathy was an entirely new feeling. As its value was exalted, feeling itself began to be reassessed. In eighteenth-century English literature, for example, sentimentalism was a new attitude, and the term was used approvingly. Laurence Sterne wrote *A Sentimental Journey*. It does not describe a “sentimental” journey in the everyday sense. *A Sentimental Journey* is perhaps the sort of book that Natsume Sōseki’s *Kusamakura* is.

As I have said, people gradually began to value feeling and imagination. At the same time, literature, which was rooted in feeling and imagination, also gained in importance. It gradually took the place of religion and philosophy. As literature flourished, nationalism was born. The nation is, in fact, a new community formed through sympathy—imagination—an “imagined community.” Germany is one example. Until Prussia unified it in the second half of the nineteenth century, it was a place of warring states and contending religious denominations. Amid this multiplicity, only literature could unite the German nation. Or rather, the German nation was German literature.

This change was not confined to Europe. Something similar happened in Japan. In the second half of the eighteenth century, Motoori Norinaga began to value feeling. The Zhu Xi school of Neo-Confucianism, espoused by the Tokugawa shogunate, exalted intellect and morality; Norinaga criticized it from the standpoint of feeling. He held that its intellect and morality were artificial concepts divorced from natural feeling. Against them, he advocated the sense of *mono no aware*—a responsiveness to things—awakened by literature.

This was not simply a feeling. It meant that there was another kind of knowledge outside philosophy and religion. He believed that *The Tale of Genji*, condemned by Confucianism as a corruption of public morals, represented true morality. His *mono no aware* was, in fact, Smith’s “sympathy.” Through “sympathy,” people of different estates under feudalism—warriors, farmers, artisans, and merchants—and people from different domains could come together in a new community. This was Japanese nationalism.

This was an invention independent of Europe. Nor was Japan the only place where it happened. The reason was that the flourishing merchant class in the second half of the eighteenth century brought about an affirmation of sensibility. Norinaga’s criticism of Confucianism soon led to the later Japanese *kokugaku*, or “National Learning.” Japanese nationalism gradually emerged from this. We could say that nationalism is rooted in literature and literary criticism.

In modern society, it is natural that sensation, feeling, and literature should be valued. But as far as I know, Kant alone examined this from a rigorous theoretical standpoint. He thoroughly investigated the relationship between literature and art, philosophical understanding, and religious morality. His aim was not to assign literature and art a place, but to mark out the limits of our possible knowledge.

For Kant, reason without sensibility is empty. That is what the metaphysics he criticized amounts to. Conversely, sensibility without reason is blind. Mere sensations or feelings, in other words, do not become knowledge. Kant saw that it was precisely imagination that joined sensibility to reason.[^kant] Before this, imagination was generally regarded as delusion or fancy. Like sensation and sensibility, it was held in low esteem. Literature, founded on imagination, was of course also disparaged. Only through Kant did imagination, and literature and art, acquire an important position. Imagination could do what neither sensibility nor reason could do. It could accomplish what physics and metaphysics could not.

Until then, the church had continually attacked literature as immoral. In the eighteenth-century West, a great many pieces with titles such as “A Defense of Poetry” or “A Defense of Literature” were written. They opposed the church and defended literature. After Kant, literature was finally spared the accusation of “immorality.” Kant gave it a splendid reason to exist.

Yet although literature escaped religious criticism, it did not become free. On the contrary, it acquired a responsibility. For imagination had been assigned the role of leading reason onward.[^leading-reason] The English poet Coleridge, for example, distinguished imagination from fancy on the basis of Kant. Although these words are often used interchangeably in ordinary speech, what Kant called imagination was not fancy but the indispensable link between reason and sensibility.

More concretely, the distinction between imagination and fancy closely resembles the distinction between modern literature and tales. Tales have existed since ancient times. They are based on fancy. People may think fancy roams freely wherever it likes, but it is actually determined by structure. Tales have simply repeated the same patterns since antiquity. Take the “noble-exile tale”—the story of a prince fallen on hard times. Someone who ought to occupy an exalted position, someone of noble birth, is banished for some reason, undergoes the trials of suffering, and returns to his original position. That is the story. We can see, then, that fancy is anything but free and unrestrained. Quite the reverse.

The modern novel is built on tales, but it is also a criticism of them. The classic example is *Don Quixote*: it is based on tales of chivalry yet takes a critical stance toward them. If this self-reflexive critical attitude is lost, the novel turns back into a tale. Such sorry failures of novels are popular fiction. Popular fiction is nothing but structure. Beyond structure: zero. Nothing at all. Perhaps an eternal zero.

There is another way to explain how modern literature connects sensibility and reason. Roughly speaking, we might say that the particular self corresponds to sensibility and the self considered in general to reason. Fields of knowledge outside literature abstract away the particular “I.” What belongs to reason must transcend personal feeling: that is all there is to it. Modern literature, however, begins by affirming the particular “I.” Of course, it does not stop there. Modern literature cannot help pursuing something universal. If someone merely writes about a singular experience of their own, without finding a resonance among thousands upon thousands of readers, it hardly seems possible to call it literature.

So there is probably something wrong with seeing literature as a matter of sensibility taking precedence over reason. It is less a matter of valuing sensibility than of valuing the relationship between sensibility and reason. Literature as tales and poetry has existed since antiquity. “As long as human beings have feelings,” literature of this kind will certainly exist. But philosophy and religion regarded it as inferior. Why did literature become important in the modern age? Because modern literature was connected to universal truth or morality. In that age, literature was eternal.

Why was literature eternal? Because, and only because, it bore the burden of reason. In the past, we could still distinguish pure literature from popular literature, imagination from fancy. Popular literature shared the structure of tales. Pure literature grew out of them but, by criticizing them, earned its claim to an independent existence. That was imagination; that was pure literature. Today, however, this distinction no longer holds. We need to reconsider the relationship between literature—imagination—and reason.

In Kant’s terms, imagination can exist only within the relationship between reason and sensibility. In other words, modern literature had to take over religion’s burden and its moral task. But the Romantics after Kant took the priority of imagination—of literature—for granted, as something requiring no reflection. Realist literature criticized this from the side of sensibility. Yet both Romanticism and realism actually lacked “reason”: they lacked a moral dimension.

As I said earlier, nationalism arose from Romanticism. Literature is the foundation of the state—the imagined community. Kant would certainly never have agreed with this route to inventing a nation. For Kant, nationalism was nothing but a delusion. He would probably also have despised literature tied to nationalism. He recognized no individual state, no city-state or *polis*, but sought a world city, a *cosmopolis*—“all under heaven.” In literature, Goethe, with his advocacy of world literature, was perhaps the figure who echoed Kant.

In short, modern literature bore religion’s moral task. Even after its emancipation from religion, it could not escape this intellectual burden. Literature assumed religious responsibility in another form. Socialism was one aspect of that responsibility. Politics, meanwhile, replaced religion as a constraint on literature.

Politics too was a burden for literature. There is no question that literature subordinated to politics is not literature at all. But literature that loses every political dimension becomes entertainment. From the 1990s onward, literature was “liberated” from all its burdens. At the same time, however, it ended. Literature as entertainment of course survives—indeed, it flourishes—but it has lost the imagination that connects reason and sensibility. In other words, it has lost the ideal called socialism.

That was when I washed my hands of literary criticism. I do not mean literary criticism in the narrow sense. As I said, I had already withdrawn from the literary world and taken up philosophy. So “quitting literature” at this point meant giving both literature and philosophy the axe.

I chose literary criticism early in 1960. I thought it could discuss anything. This view was directly influenced by the critic Takaaki Yoshimoto. The larger background was the exceptional trust placed in literary criticism in postwar Japan. The reason, perhaps, was that before and during the war, philosophy and the social sciences had disgracefully exposed their fatal flaws. After the war, only literary criticism remained. Literature did not abstract away the dimension of individual sensation and feeling, yet it could grasp social structures that transcended the individual. In short, it preserved the self while grasping the world. One need only think for a moment about the experience before and during the war—militarism’s destruction of the individual and so on—to feel the importance of literary criticism in this respect.

Even before the Second World War, literary criticism was an independent form of knowledge that stood against philosophy and the social sciences. Please note the following point. What Hideo Kobayashi called literary criticism was actually French philosophy. In France, there is no clear division between philosophy and literature. Outside France, therefore, French philosophy is studied not in philosophy departments but in departments of French literature. This was true in Japan both before and after the war, and in America after the war as well: philosophy departments centered on analytic philosophy, with German philosophy accounting for the rest.

Previously, wherever people discussed philosophy, they were doing German philosophy. It gradually declined only after the Second World War. In a sense, however, the French inherited the lineage of German philosophy. Sartre, for example, brought together Hegel, Husserl, Heidegger, and Marx. French in substance, German in application.[^substance] He wrote novels and plays, and late in life he was still writing literary criticism such as *The Family Idiot*, his study of Flaubert. There is nothing incongruous about a French philosopher doing this. I doubt anyone has ever bothered with the tedious question of whether Rousseau was a philosopher or a literary writer. So when I chose literary criticism rather than philosophy directly, what I actually chose was the French kind of philosophy–literature.

Postwar Japanese intellectuals no longer trusted the utterly spineless disciplines of philosophy and social science. Literary criticism, allied with French philosophy, won out. French philosophy, represented by Sartre, replaced German philosophy and the Kyoto School. This intellectual movement and the development of literary criticism were two sides of the same thing. Even within the social sciences, a political scientist such as Masao Maruyama was really more like a literary critic. His students Shōzō Fujita and Bunzō Hashikawa actually were literary critics.

The reason French philosophy acquired such an important position in the postwar world was, in fact, political: France’s distinctive strategic position in the Cold War.

After the Second World War came the Cold War between the Soviet Union and the United States. It is generally understood as a confrontation between socialism and capitalism. But please note that people soon became disillusioned with Soviet-style socialism. At the same time, they wanted to reject capitalist America. Neither America nor the Soviet Union would do: people felt the need to consider a “third way.” Of course, this was not realistic. But the effort to pursue this “third way” was precisely “imagination,” precisely “literature.” Not literature in the narrow sense, of course, but literature as political and philosophical “imagination” in pursuit of a third way.

One attempt to realize the political dimension I have just described—the Cold War search for a “third way”—was the “Third World” movement, quite literally so called. The “Third World” is usually taken to mean backward countries and regions. But in essence, it was originally a project opposed to both the First World, the Euro-American sphere, and the Second World, the Soviet sphere. After 1990, therefore, when the Second World disappeared, the Third World naturally could no longer sustain itself.

Another attempt at a “third way” was France. President de Gaulle tried to construct a third force outside the United States and the Soviet Union. This was “Europe.” Like the Third World project, it lost its meaning after 1990 and was eventually absorbed into the German-led European Union. De Gaulle’s political strategy certainly suffered defeat, but the “third way” as an intellectual project was realized in postwar French philosophy. This gave French philosophy in that period a distinctive reason to exist.

Sartre, whom I mentioned earlier, is representative. His early philosophical work concerned imagination. His “imagination” was the power to reduce what exists to nothingness—*néantisation*, or nihilation—and bring what does not exist into being. Although Sartre did not mention Kant during this period, he actually restored the meaning of “imagination” in the Kantian sense. “Imagination” was not merely a philosophical problem. His own Suonan-like “History of the Heart” pointed toward a “third way” beyond the opposition between capitalism, represented by America, and socialism, represented by the Soviet Union.[^heart]

The same was true in Japan. Kan’ichi Kuroda’s advocacy of “anti-imperialism and anti-Stalinism” was literally a third way. Takaaki Yoshimoto’s “autonomy” meant the same thing. Autonomy meant breaking free of two external forces and standing independently. In France, “autonomy” meant political independence. Although there was no direct connection, Sartre was precisely the figure seeking independence in philosophy. He founded this philosophical lineage, and philosophers pursuing the same intellectual task began to emerge.

Of course, these French thinkers always had to take Sartre to task and give his concept of imagination a good thrashing. Derrida, for example, proposed deconstructing metaphysical binary oppositions. He tried to show something outside the two terms: this was really a pursuit of a “third way.” So when Derrida criticized metaphysical binary oppositions, he was not so much criticizing metaphysics from antiquity onward as alluding to the political opposition of the Cold War—the opposition between the Soviet Union and America. Moreover, his philosophy centered on the reading of texts. Judged by a traditional conception of philosophy, Derrida’s philosophy comes infinitely close to literary criticism.

A biography of Derrida was recently published in Japan. It is quite a thick book, and I only pick it up now and then, but I found something unexpected in it. It says that the young Derrida had been expected to become a philosopher, but he went into literature instead. So he did not gradually approach literature; he had belonged to literature all along. I used to think that I had gone from literature to philosophy, while Derrida had gone from philosophy to literature, and that this was a decisive difference between us. But after reading his biography, it dawned on me that we were actually similar types.

Derrida proposed the deconstruction of binary oppositions, and Deleuze said something similar. He too sought, through “imagination,” a third way outside both the Soviet Union and America. He too therefore moved from philosophy toward literature and art. But I am not here to sing the praises of French philosophers. Then came 1990—and the big upheaval.

In 1990, after the upheavals in the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe, the Cold War binary opposition came to an end. The Second World, the Soviet sphere, vanished into thin air, and global capitalism reigned supreme. This was the age of neoliberalism. My own view is that modern French thought could not resist this new situation. More than that, it helped neoliberalism along. As far as I know, both Derrida and Deleuze were acutely sensitive to this. They began, for example, openly and explicitly declaring themselves Marxists. But such changes were soon submerged by the postmodern current of the age. Not long afterward, Deleuze killed himself.

Once the second way, the Soviet Union, had collapsed, a third way was no longer viable. This judgment applies equally to the New Left. It is generally thought that the Soviet dissolution dealt an enormous blow to the Old Left. I think the opposite. It was the New Left, rather, that took the blow. Previously, the New Left had only to criticize the Old Left to brim with vitality. Once the Old Left disappeared, the New Left faced a crisis. But this crisis went unnoticed. Nor did people notice that philosophy—literary criticism—had also lost the basis for its existence. There was no “third way” anymore.

Nietzsche once said, “God is dead.”[^nietzsche] But what he really meant was: religion is dead, while art is coming into its own. He believed that poetry—literature—such as *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* would endure forever. In fact, he wrote *Zarathustra* to oppose the Gospels of the New Testament. Art became God; seen this way, God had not died after all. The same was true of Heidegger and Deleuze: they raised the banner of art against religion and philosophy. But what if literature and art had died too? This was the question I encountered at the end of the twentieth century.

I began writing *Transcritique* in the 1990s. I wanted to reread Kant and Marx from the ground up. By the end of the decade, however, I was gradually becoming aware of the limits of this kind of criticism. As I said earlier, *Transcritique* is the same as literary criticism, but by the end it becomes something different. The true art of literary criticism is to discern a “third way” within a text. By the time I reached the end, however, I had given up this method. I gave up turning Kant’s iron into gold, and gave up “seizing the embryo and changing the bones” of Marx’s thought. I wanted neither lineage anymore. I introduced the theory of modes of exchange directly.[^japanese-passage] In the end, I got involved in social movements.

This is “movement.” But I did not move of my own accord; I was simply thrown into the post–Cold War age. It was not that I wanted to change. Things themselves began to change. Only in this new situation did I want to leave my earlier thinking behind and think things through again. This was my transcritique of myself.

But I do not think that this French style of thought, my own included, was wrong or false. No discourse represents the truth at all times and in all places. The texts French thinkers wrote during the Cold War had critical force in that time; from the 1990s onward, they no longer did. There is nothing unusual about this. Of course, perhaps one day, as circumstances change, we will be able to read meaning in them again.

> Sun Sheng’s annals passed overseas;  
> Suonan’s *History of the Heart* survived intact within a well.[^chen]

It is too early to speak of that today.

After finishing *Transcritique*, I spent about ten years thinking. Throughout those ten years, I continued to develop the theory of modes of exchange proposed in that book. The results were *The Structure of World History*, *Isonomia and the Origins of Philosophy*, and *The Structure of Empire*. Perhaps I will continue this work in the future. At the same time, I have also been reflecting on my past work.

At the beginning, I said that I did not want to reflect on myself unless compelled by an external force. Yet there have been times when I have done so. In 2003, I began preparing the five-volume definitive edition of my collected works for Iwanami Shoten. I thoroughly rewrote what I had written before. Perhaps because I was also giving up smoking, I did no other work during this period. Nothing new. I was also rereading my writings on literature.

In fact, I am considering revisiting and revising my work once again—for example, collecting my literary criticism and philosophical writings. Although thirty years ago I said in the afterword to *Introspection and Retrospection* that I did not engage in self-reflection, I am now considering finishing “Language, Number, Money,” the unfinished essay included in that book. But whether I will meet with another setback and give it up again, I cannot say.

### English translator’s notes

[^pure-literature]: “Pure literature” (*junbungaku*) is a Japanese literary category conventionally contrasted with popular or mass literature. “Pure” here does not mean morally unblemished or detached from politics.

[^remaking]: “Seizing the embryo and changing the bones” is a Chinese poetic idiom associated with Huang Tingjian and Song poetics: inherited language and conceptions are transformed into new writing. It returns below alongside “turning iron into gold.” These are features of the Chinese adaptation’s literary language. See [Jonathan Stalling on the idiom and its English renderings](https://www.asiancha.com/content/view/1621/421/).

[^sympathy]: The Chinese adaptation uses “individualism” and develops the image of atomized individuals. The [Japanese passage quoted in 2015](https://martbm.hatenablog.com/entry/20150307/1425656002) instead uses “egoism” and describes sympathy as emerging after older communities have broken down and egoism has spread.

[^kant]: This is a paraphrase, not Kant’s exact formulation. At A51/B75 of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, Kant writes: “Thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind” (Paul Guyer and Allen W. Wood’s translation; see their [introduction, p. 50](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781107266209_A23693433/preview-9781107266209_A23693433.pdf)). The reason–sensibility pairing is already present in the [quoted Japanese lecture passage](https://martbm.hatenablog.com/entry/20150307/1425656002); it is not solely a Chinese substitution.

[^leading-reason]: The Chinese adaptation says that imagination leads reason onward. In the corresponding [Japanese passage quoted in 2015](https://martbm.hatenablog.com/entry/20150307/1425656002), imagination instead assumes the burden of reason. The English retains the Chinese formulation.

[^substance]: This compact formula draws on the Chinese distinction between *ti*, substance, and *yong*, function or application, familiar from “Chinese learning as substance, Western learning for application.” It is the Chinese adaptation’s way of describing Sartre, not a technical formula of his.

[^heart]: *History of the Heart* (*Xin shi*) is traditionally attributed to Zheng Sixiao, known as Suonan, a loyalist of the fallen Song dynasty. Here the Chinese adaptation invokes it figuratively for Sartre’s inner intellectual commitments; it is not a title by Sartre. The allusion returns in the couplet near the end.

[^nietzsche]: “God is dead” appears in Nietzsche’s *The Gay Science*, including §§108 and 125. The wording is also that of Walter Kaufmann’s translation, reproduced in [German History in Documents and Images](https://germanhistorydocs.org/en/forging-an-empire-bismarckian-germany-1866-1890/friedrich-nietzsche-pronounces-god-is-dead-the-gay-science-1882). What follows here about art succeeding religion is the article’s interpretation, not a continuation of the quotation.

[^japanese-passage]: The Japanese passage reproduced in [Zhao Yishan’s handout, pp. 119–120](https://www.tufs.ac.jp/blog/icjs/events/pdf/summerschool_shiryosyu.pdf), citing *Intellectual Earthquakes*, p. 279, describes abandoning the search for a third way within texts, introducing modes of exchange not derivable from Kant or Marx, and turning to social movements. The iron-and-gold, embryo-and-bones, and lineage rhetoric is an expansion in the Chinese adaptation.

[^chen]: These lines open the second of Chen Yinke’s 1953 poems “Bidding Farewell to Jiang Bingnan in Guangzhou.” They invoke accounts of writings preserved beyond immediate political pressures: Sun Sheng’s history transmitted overseas and Suonan’s *History of the Heart* said to have survived concealed in a well. The verse is translated here from the Chinese adaptation. For the poem and its context, see [Guan Aihe, “Chen Yinke’s Scholarly Orientation and Literary Research,” p. 50](https://dflt.qdu.edu.cn/__local/4/F3/CC/2A83DFB68712340542EC6D95538_871F842E_1735E2.pdf).
