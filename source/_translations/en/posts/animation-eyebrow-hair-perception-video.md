Moving between animation history and the phenomenology of perception, this video essay asks what happens when two-dimensional animation lets a character’s eyebrows and eyes show through the hair—or makes the hair hide them. The author presents it as one of the earlier Chinese-language videos to focus closely on this question. It was selected for the 2022 Eve Festival of Rags Drum, Lab on Roof’s annual open-call program.

### 00:00 — The Problem of Perception

**00:00.** Animation is often called “the art of line.” That description makes immediate sense at the drawing stage. Apart from areas marked for shadow, the space bounded by the lines is still blank. We nevertheless see a face, a lock of hair, or a sleeve before any color has been added. Here, *intuition* does not mean a hunch. It means the immediate perceptual act by which separate marks become a recognizable whole.

The finished image asks more of us. Color turns those blank spaces into positive shapes. The area between two contours is no longer merely a gap: it may be a flesh-colored cheek, a dark mass of hair, or a blue background visible between them. I will call such a bounded span an *interval*. Its color can make one shape seem to sit in front of another even when their outlines lie on the same flat screen. We therefore have to look not only at lines but at how lines, colored intervals, and apparent layers work together.

**00:38.** Consider a black line on a white field. Before we name it as the rim of a face or the edge of a hand, perception has already joined its points into a contour and separated a figure from its ground. This is the *pre-objective* (*préobjectif*) level of experience: the organization of what we see before we decide what the thing is.

A photograph begins from an object that stood before a camera. The old comparison between photography and mummification describes the camera as preserving a physical trace of that once-present subject; this trace is why a photograph can summon the memory of a particular person or moment. Most drawn animation has no such photographed body behind each figure. We do not have to work backward from a patch of cel paint to a unique object that once occupied the same position in front of a lens.

That difference gives us two orders of abstraction. A photograph is a first abstraction: a three-dimensional subject becomes a flat image while retaining a material link to what the camera recorded. A drawing can be called a second abstraction: an animator has already reduced observed forms and movements to lines and colored areas, and the viewer must assemble those signs into a body and a world. Ordinary abstraction often starts with a whole object and breaks it into parts. Animation reverses that movement. It starts with detached marks, asks us to group them into a coherent *Gestalt*, and then lets that perceptual whole move.

**01:40.** The figure-and-ground example in *Phenomenology of Perception* offers a simple model. Imagine a white patch on a field of uniform blue. We do not experience it as an equal distribution of white and blue points. The white holds together as a figure. Its border seems to belong to it, while the blue reads as a continuous field extending behind it. The patch therefore appears to lie on a plane in front of the blue even though both colors occupy the same physical surface.

The crucial fact is not that the white is intrinsically brighter or stronger. It is that our attention assigns it a different role. We inspect the patch; the blue recedes into context. When I later speak of the “light” of perception, I mean this selective attention, not a ray projected by the eye. It predicts something we can check in an image: whichever shape our attention organizes as the figure will seem more complete, more legible, and usually nearer than the shapes it treats as ground.

**02:39.** So where does attention settle in an animated face? If we stop at the key drawing, we see only contours and the closed shapes they describe. The audience, however, sees finished footage. Color and compositing may reinforce those contours, weaken them, or make two lines that cross on the page appear to belong to different depths.

Eyebrows, eyes, and hair provide an unusually clear test. In what I will call the *opaque treatment*, the hair shape actually occludes some or all of the brow or eye lines. In the *show-through treatment*, those features remain drawn across the hair region as if the hair were transparent. These are graphic conventions, not claims about literal optical transparency. Each convention tells the viewer what to prioritize: the mass of hair in front, the expressive features beneath it, or a negotiated balance between the two.

### 03:25 — Variation in Production Practice

**03:25.** The character designer usually establishes the default treatment, but an animation director or episode director can alter it. A design sheet may hide a character’s brows and eyes behind the hair while a particular shot reveals them. The reverse can convey embarrassment, withdrawal, menace, or another momentary emotion: features that normally show through can disappear behind the fringe. It is one of the choices made in directing an episode. Even characters who share a fictional world need not follow the same rule.

**04:03.** The third season of *Dropkick on My Devil!* offers a convenient example. Medusa’s brows and eyes show through her hair. The protagonist Yurine has a blunt fringe, and her eyebrows remain behind it, while Hatsune Miku returns to the show-through convention. These differences help distinguish the three designs: Medusa and Miku keep a readily legible facial surface, whereas Yurine’s fringe forms a stronger shape in front of the face. Within a typical twenty-minute television episode, however, each character will usually retain her own default across most shots, and most of the cast will be governed by a broadly consistent design logic.

### 04:43 — The Opaque Treatment: From Naturalist Occlusion to Layered Faces

**04:43.** A prominent recent cluster begins with Masayoshi Tanaka’s Chichibu trilogy—*anohana: The Flower We Saw That Day*, *The Anthem of the Heart*, and *Her Blue Sky*—and extends through *DARLING in the FRANXX* and the films from *Your Name.* to *Suzume*. Related approaches appear in Saki Takahashi’s designs for *Wonder Egg Priority* and Kazutaka Sugiyama’s for *Mushoku Tensei: Jobless Reincarnation*, both shaped by the character-design and directorial practices associated with Tanaka and Noriko Takao. Across these works, hair is allowed to hide the brows rather than turning transparent whenever it crosses them.

The opaque convention has an obvious naturalistic rationale: solid hair sits in front of the face, so it should conceal whatever lies behind it. Isao Takahata and Yasuo Otsuka’s *Horus, Prince of the Sun* (1968) stands as one early landmark in a naturalist-realist lineage that used such occlusion. This is not a claim that every Japanese production of the following two decades used opaque hair. It marks a contrast with the show-through convention that became especially established during the 1980s.

Production technology helped that later convention spread. A transfer machine could copy key-drawing lines directly onto cels before paint was applied, making an overlapping brow line easier to preserve consistently across many drawings than it had been in a workflow dependent on selectively tracing lines by hand. Digital scanning later extended the same practical advantage: artists could retain, recolor, or remove the brow and hair lines independently before the finished shot was composited. Neither technology invented show-through, but each made a deliberate exception to physical occlusion easier to reproduce at television and OVA scale.

**05:46.** The opaque designs seen between 2011’s *anohana* and 2021’s *Wonder Egg Priority* and *Mushoku Tensei: Jobless Reincarnation* belong to a different moment from early Toei naturalism. They participate in a tendency, influenced by Hironori Tanaka, toward drawings that are flatter in their modeling yet more sharply stratified into planes. Hair becomes a strong upper shape; fragments of brow and eye sit lower, as though recessed into an eye socket. The face gains depth not through painstaking three-dimensional shading but through the tension between those stacked graphic layers.

This is why color design and digital compositing matter. Compositing is the final process that combines character drawings, color, backgrounds, lighting, and effects into a finished shot. It is not simply editing. It can make the same lines feel soft or hard, near or distant, fused with the background or cut sharply away from it.

An on-screen note makes a bilingual joke out of *一揆*. Read as a playful Chinese decomposition, the characters suggest measuring every cut by “one principle,” so that a production shares the same logic and look; read in Japanese as *ikki*, the word means an uprising. The pun catches compositing’s double role. It imposes visual continuity across cuts, yet a change at this late stage can also overturn the depth, color, and emphasis created by every earlier department.

Digital compositing did not by itself cause the opaque treatment to return. It made the return more expressive. Shadows, graded color, softened edges, and more carefully modeled lips and cheeks could now preserve facial feeling even when a brow line vanished behind the hair. Work that show-through once had to perform almost alone—keeping the face readable and lively—could be distributed across facial drawing, color, and the final composite. Opaque hair could therefore contribute weight and depth without simply erasing expression.

**07:14.** In Masayoshi Tanaka’s designs, concealment makes the face feel denser. Visible fragments of eyebrow peek out where the fringe parts or ends; the hidden portions are inferred rather than somehow seen through solid hair. The soft but weighty mass above them makes the face seem layered and tactile. These designs often use heavy hair, softened features, and only partly available brows to evoke an adolescent body still taking shape. No eyebrow is literally a secondary sexual characteristic. The effect comes from how the ensemble makes physical maturation feel tentative and not yet fully disclosed.

This is not simply a continuation of early Toei realism or of realist tendencies in the 1980s and 1990s. Its realism lies in *layered attention*. When we concentrate on one feature, another becomes its ground. The hair is graphically in front, yet viewers who are reading the expression continue to search the gaps and edges for the brows and eyes. The resulting tension is specific to highly stratified *bishōjo*, or “pretty-girl,” animation: the drawing withholds part of the very face it has trained us to examine.

**08:34.** Human attention is strongly drawn to eyes and eye-like shapes, though not every viewer will respond with equal force. Viewers accustomed to *bishōjo* animation since the mid-1980s may be particularly ready to treat large, frontalized eyes as a form of address. A character’s diegetic eyeline can point elsewhere, yet the graphic prominence of the eyes can still make the face feel presented to us.

This relation is an example of *intentionality*: perception is always directed toward something, and here the animated face and the viewer’s attention seem to reach toward one another. The stage is a useful metaphor as long as we keep it concrete. Brows and eyes carry the performance; the fringe frames, interrupts, or briefly conceals them like scenery. The viewer supplies no literal spotlight. Our attention simply selects the facial movement as the main event, and the hair’s job changes according to whether it assists or obstructs that selection.

### 09:20 — The Emergence of the Show-Through Treatment

**09:20.** If opaque hair can be defended as naturalistic, it is tempting to dismiss show-through as a rejection of the real body. That conclusion rests on an overly narrow empiricism: the assumption that a drawing is realistic only when every visible surface obeys literal physical occlusion. On that account, *Horus, Prince of the Sun* represents realism in 1968, while the show-through convention that flourished in the 1980s and 1990s must represent a turn away from bodily experience.

But perception is not a simple inventory of whatever remains optically unobstructed. Fine or parted hair can reveal pieces of the features beneath it; lighting can alter how permeable it appears; and, even when a feature is completely covered, our knowledge of the face lets us locate and mentally complete it. That does not mean we literally see an eyebrow through opaque hair. It means we continue to group the hidden brow with the eyes and the rest of the expression. Show-through makes that perceptual grouping visible on the drawing. Far from ignoring the body, it can translate our active experience of looking at a face into a graphic convention.

**10:38.** This brings us to the *affection-image*. By that term I mean an image, often organized around a face or close-up, that presents a felt quality before it advances an action: apprehension in the eyes, a hesitation in the brow, a mood held in the features. Show-through supports such an image by keeping the expressive parts of the face available to the viewer.

In life, brows and lashes do not occupy a literal pictorial plane. Perceptually, however, we tend to group them with the other facial features because they work together to make an expression. Hair may be physically nearer, but attention can treat it as a foreground frame around that expressive unit. The closest production analogy is a *BOOK* layer: in traditional cel compositing, a separate foreground overlay placed in front of the character. Like that overlay, the hair remains visibly in front without necessarily taking over as the image’s subject.

The rapid growth of television anime and the OVA market in the mid-to-late 1980s expanded both the number and the variety of *bishōjo* designs. Elaborate hairstyles and ornaments presented a practical expressive problem: if every lock obeyed physical occlusion, it could bury the brows and sometimes the eyes, reducing the face’s readable movement. Meanwhile, direct line transfer—and later digital scanning and compositing—made overlapping lines easier to reproduce and adjust. Content and technique therefore pushed in the same direction. Show-through kept brows and eyes legible while allowing hairstyles to become larger and more ornate.

The result is not that hair and face literally occupy one layer. Rather, the finished image lets attention move between them without losing the expression. Brows and eyes remain the primary carriers of mood; hair and ornaments frame or accent that mood. The affection-image becomes more outward-facing and immediately readable because the character’s emotional cues remain on the surface presented to us.

**12:40.** Early and influential examples include Tsukasa Dokite’s character designs and animation direction for the television series *Dirty Pair*; Kazuaki Mōri’s work on the OVA *Dream Hunter Rem*; Akemi Takada’s designs for the television version of *Kimagure Orange Road*; and Mutsumi Inomata’s character design and animation direction for the OVA *Leda: The Fantastic Adventure of Yohko*.

An on-screen note observes that *Dirty Pair* even treats its two leads differently according to their hairstyles. This does more than decorate the designs: the lead whose features show through keeps an open, continuous facial surface, while the lead whose hair interrupts them gains a stronger sense of fringe in front of face. *Dream Hunter Rem* and *Leda* offer a sharper comparison. They are near-contemporary “bikini armor” fantasies created within overlapping professional networks, but *Rem* uses show-through while *Leda* uses opaque hair. In *Rem*, expression remains immediately available despite the ornate hairstyle; in *Leda*, the gaze feels more recessed and the hair gives the head more bodily volume. The comparison cannot prove a single historical line of influence, but it isolates what the graphic choice changes for the viewer.

### 13:16 — A Morphology of Show-Through Treatments

**13:16.** Show-through is not a single technique with a single effect. Its forms can be classified more precisely.

**13:29.** A model-sheet instruction such as “show the brows through” is not enough. We have to begin with the finished image, after coloring and compositing have determined how every outlined area appears. The question is not only whether a brow line survives, but whether it seems to sit on the hair, behind the hair, or on the same expressive surface as the eye.

**13:49.** One common strategy follows the illustrated character sprites of romance games. Works such as *ToHeart* color the eyebrows darker, often with the hair’s shadow color or an even deeper second-shadow tone. The eyebrow line and the solid outline of the hair remain intact where they meet rather than erasing one another. On the drawing they are simply juxtaposed, but the darker brow reads as if it lies behind the lighter hair. Color, rather than occlusion, supplies the depth cue. The viewer can read the full expression without losing the sense of hair in front.

Notice the eyelashes. The boundary between sclera and skin may be drawn in *local color*—an outline close to the color of the adjacent area—rather than a single solid dark line. Iris, pupil, and lashes then provide the eye’s most forceful contour. This is common in *bishōjo* animation: the hair can cross the skin and cast a shadow there, but its fill and outline stop at the eye’s contour. The eye remains the clearest point of focus.

**15:07.** A second strategy appears throughout *Revolutionary Girl Utena*. The drawing groups the eyebrow with the hair surface, then handles the eye differently. Where a lock crosses the iris, that portion of hair takes on the iris’s color. It covers the sclera, but the upper and lower lashes remain crisp. The lock therefore seems to pass through the eye’s color without covering the eye’s defining edges. Hair appears to flow around the pupil, making the pupil—and the gaze it carries—seem luminous and impossible to block.

The *ToHeart* strategy keeps hair outside the eye. *Utena* allows it inside but changes its color to preserve the eye. A hybrid of the two has become common in more recent animation: the brow may remain visible through the hair, but the hair’s solid outline stops before the eye rather than crossing it as it does in *Utena*. The convention still overrides literal physical layering, but it protects the eye’s internal design. This matters because many twenty-first-century character designs divide the iris into several colors, highlights, and colored lines. Letting a heavy hair contour cross that already dense structure would make the gaze harder to parse. The newer hybrid keeps both the elaborate eye and the elaborate hairstyle legible.

**16:38.** A third method uses color-trace lines: outlines colored to harmonize with the surrounding fill. In *Kase-san and Morning Glories*, the section of eyebrow beneath the hair is drawn entirely with such colored outlines, while the exposed section retains a solid dark edge. Under the hair, the brow is slightly darker than the hair’s first shadow tone but lighter than the brow where no hair covers it. The viewer sees a subdued trace rather than a full-strength line. The feature is neither absent nor pasted implausibly onto the hair’s surface; it hovers just behind it, softening the expression without making it unreadable.

**17:21.** *VLADLOVE* takes another route. It lets the hair hide part of the eye and groups the eyebrow graphically with the hair, while the visible portions of the eye remain clearly recessed behind that foreground shape. Because the eye emerges only where the hair permits it, the flat drawing suggests the turn of the face and the hollow of the eye socket. Here occlusion does not merely suppress expression; it supplies facial curvature and depth.

### 17:45 — A Thought Experiment in Direction

**17:45.** Several shots from episode four of *Casshern Sins* make a useful final experiment. Yoshihiko Umakoshi personally handled most of the animation corrections in the episode’s B-part—the second half, after the commercial break—yet opaque and show-through treatments alternate there. No single rule governs an entire scene or sequence. The choice can be made shot by shot, and within one continuous shot the character’s movement can change how much of the brow or eye remains visible.

**18:20.** Seeing the brows and eyes is therefore not merely a matter of meeting a character’s gaze from near or far. It changes the direction of the affection-image—how the image delivers feeling to us. To test that claim, make two versions of the same shot, one show-through and one opaque, and splice each back into the original footage. Pose, timing, camera distance, and performance remain constant; only the relation among hair, brows, and eyes changes.

In the show-through version, the face holds together as one expressive surface. The brow movement registers sooner, the eyes feel more available, and the emotion seems to travel outward toward the viewer. In the opaque version, the hair asserts itself as a foreground mass. The eyes recede, the viewer has to infer more of the expression, and the same emotion can feel more private, weighted, or withheld. Neither result is inherently more realistic, and neither convention has one fixed emotional meaning. Context can reverse or complicate the effect.

The experiment nevertheless gives us a testable principle. A brow-and-eye treatment guides perception by assigning visual priority. It can ask us to read the face before the hair, to feel the hair before reaching the face, or to oscillate between the two. What I have called the “light” of perception is simply that mobile attention. It is everywhere in the image because every contour, color, and layer helps direct it—and because the viewer, not the drawing alone, completes the final arrangement.
