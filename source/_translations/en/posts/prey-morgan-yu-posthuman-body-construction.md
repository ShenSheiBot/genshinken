## I. Introduction

*Prey* unfolds in the near future of an alternate history. After the Soviet Union encounters the extraterrestrial Typhon in orbit, the United States and the USSR build Kletka, a prison and research station in lunar orbit, to contain the aliens. The facility and its research change hands several times. In 2025, TranStar Industries acquires the derelict station; by 2030, it has rebuilt Kletka as Talos I, where experiments on the Typhon underpin a new neuroscience industry.

The protagonist, Morgan Yu, is the child of TranStar founders Catherine and William Yu. Morgan joins the company in 2027, helps develop the technology that will become the Neuromod, and moves to Talos I in 2032. Over the next three years, Morgan volunteers as the principal test subject in experiments that use Neuromods to give a human being Typhon abilities. The device is more than a convenient game mechanic. It is the hinge between the game’s world, its plot, and its account of the self. How does *Prey* use the Neuromod to construct a posthuman subject?

## II. Real-World Analogues of the Neuromod

The Neuromod—short for Neural Modifier—is a gun-shaped applicator pressed against the eye. Two needles slide around the eyeball and inject a serum that rewires the brain. TranStar first maps the neural structure associated with a given skill, then makes the user’s brain reproduce that synaptic pattern.[[1]](#ref-1)

Once the brain can be mapped and remodeled in this way, skill no longer looks intangible. It becomes information that can be captured, manufactured, and installed. TranStar’s sales pitch supplies a suitably extravagant example: make a Neuromod from a concert pianist, install it in someone else, and that person can play with the expertise the pianist spent a lifetime acquiring. The same process could preserve a person’s abilities if aging, illness, or injury left them unable to exercise those abilities themselves. Knowledge has been detached from one body and made available to another.

The Neuromod bundles several familiar technological fantasies into one device, along with their ethical problems. It can make Morgan stronger and tougher, or confer specialized combat and scientific skills. Because it enhances the body by reconstructing its neural architecture, it invites comparison with genetic engineering. Both technologies trouble the notion of a given “natural order.”[[2]](#ref-2) Darwinian evolution had already displaced Aristotle’s vision of eternal, unchanging species, but it put no guiding purpose in its place: species are contingent products of variation and selection. Once the results of that unguided process are declared natural and therefore proper, however, deliberate change can look like an attempt to usurp evolution’s authority. The dispute is political as well as philosophical. Who gets to direct human transformation? How far may human beings remake themselves? Like genetic engineering, the Neuromod poses questions that a simple story of progress cannot settle.

The device also recalls neuropharmacology. No real drug corresponds exactly to the Neuromod’s fictional injection; the resemblance lies in the promise of rapid self-transformation through direct intervention in the nervous system. The game speaks of “removing” Neuromods, but nothing implanted is pulled back out of the skull. TranStar instead reverses the neural alterations produced by previous injections. That reset returns the brain to the state recorded before the first modification and erases every memory formed since. Morgan’s repeated cycle of modification, testing, and reset therefore produces amnesia and “personality drift.” The Neuromod literalizes a familiar anxiety about pharmacological enhancement: a shortcut to greater capacity may also alter the person who decides what counts as improvement.[[3]](#ref-3) Its effects are neural, bodily, and personal. They are political, too, because TranStar controls both the modification and the amnesia-producing reset.

The Neuromod cannot answer the questions it raises. It does, however, force us to ask what happens to the body it changes. To understand how *Prey* constructs a posthuman body, we need a more precise account of embodiment.

## III. Dividing and Reconstructing the Body

One strand of modern thought about the body asks how technology enters bodily experience. In *Bodies in Technology*,[[4]](#ref-4) Don Ihde approaches the question by distinguishing “body one” from “body two.” These are not separate bodies. They are two aspects of embodiment.

Body one is the Merleau-Pontian body: active, perceptive, emotional, and situated in the world. It is the body as lived in first-person experience. Body two is the Foucauldian body made legible from the outside: represented, interpreted, and marked by psychological, political, and cultural forces. Institutions classify it; records describe it; other people assign it identities and meanings. A person can also externalize an account of their own body, which then becomes something they or others encounter as an object. Technology does not simply act upon these two aspects from outside. It helps shape the relation between them.

*Prey* puts this distinction to ingenious use, but its nested experiments require some orientation. The original Morgan Yu was a human being who underwent TranStar’s Neuromod trials before the disaster on Talos I. Each neural reset returned that same body to an earlier brain state, producing a succession of amnesiac Morgans with different recent experiences and, eventually, different plans. The Morgan the player inhabits is something else: a reconstruction of Morgan’s identity inside a later empathy test, experienced by a physically present Typhon subject. The game initially conceals this last fact.

The opening apartment and test sequence reconstructs one day from the original human Morgan’s trials. That earlier experiment was not a virtual world. TranStar repeatedly woke Morgan in a physically staged apartment, sent the same living human body through adjoining sets and test chambers, and reset Morgan’s memory before running the day again with a new configuration of Typhon Neuromods. The later empathy test, by contrast, reconstructs Morgan and the Talos I crisis as a scenario for the Typhon subject. Thus the player sees a simulation of an event that, for the original human Morgan, took place in real rooms and a real body.

In the opening iteration, TranStar’s latest reset has erased Morgan’s recent memories, while January has replaced the Typhon Neuromods prepared for the test with blank applicators. Morgan must therefore solve each room as an unmodified human: move some boxes, crouch behind a chair, cross the room, press a button. At the level of the historical event, this is body one in action—the original Morgan perceiving a physical room and responding with an ordinary human body. At the level of the later empathy test, the physical Typhon subject experiences that action from within the reconstructed Morgan identity.

Body two is already present in the researchers’ expectations. When Morgan crouches behind the chair, one observer is startled that the subject is literally hiding there. The test expected Morgan to use Mimic Matter and become the chair. Morgan has obeyed the instruction but failed the institutional test, which was designed to measure a technologically altered human body. The mismatch brings lived action and outside classification into the same small, absurd scene.

January and December then make the original Morgan’s externalized judgments impossible to ignore. They are custom Operators—mobile service robots with physical bodies—that different post-reset versions of the human Morgan secretly programmed during the trials. Each carries the voice, persona, and instructions supplied by the Morgan who built it. They embody the judgments of earlier Morgans; they are neither disembodied minds nor virtual copies of the person.

Those earlier Morgans had begun to recognize and resist the repeated-day experiment, but the resets kept dividing what one state of Morgan knew and wanted from the next. They left incompatible contingency plans behind. December tries to help the playable Morgan identity escape aboard the private pod belonging to Alex—Morgan’s brother and the director of the Talos I experiments. January insists that the station and all its Typhon research must be destroyed.

The game never shows exactly how the human Morgan built the two Operators, but it establishes that each preserves its maker’s moral outlook at a particular moment. They are not neutral memory backups. They are time-stamped, physically embodied projections of one person’s conflicting post-reset states, and their dispute can become violent: January destroys December rather than let December’s escape plan override January’s destruction directive.

The Operators matter to body two because they let earlier Morgans’ judgments confront the playable identity as external objects. Recordings, videos, emails, documents, and the crew’s memories do something similar without acquiring robot bodies. Together, these traces represent the original Morgan as employee, test subject, sibling, colleague, and threat. Some were created by Morgan; others record how TranStar and its staff interpreted and classified Morgan. Faced with competing accounts of who Morgan was and what ought to happen next, the Typhon subject acting as Morgan—and, through that subject, the player—must decide which instructions to accept, reject, or revise.

## IV. Disembodiment, Embodiment, and “Body Three”

Ihde uses his distinction to ask how technology and social forces shape embodiment and disembodiment. As David R. Koukal observes, Ihde contrasts the lived body with the body converted into an image or virtual object.[[5]](#ref-5) In the first case, a technology may extend perception and action until the device itself recedes into bodily experience. In the second, technology places the body at a distance, where an observer can inspect and interpret it. The distinction is useful for thinking about physical and virtual bodies, but if applied too rigidly it risks rebuilding the Cartesian divide between subject and object in new terms.

Melissa Clarke calls the technological dimension of Ihde’s account “body three.”[[6]](#ref-6) This essay follows Clarke’s term but uses it narrowly: not for a third body or a third aspect alongside body one and body two, but for the technologically mediated relation between them. Andrew Feenberg’s “extended body” likewise acts through technical mediation and acquires meaning through that mediation.[[7]](#ref-7) A blind person’s cane, for example, can become part of how its user feels and moves through the world. At the same time, it identifies the user to observers as someone who navigates by touch and may change how those observers respond. The technical extension is lived from within and interpreted from outside. Here, “body three” is shorthand for that conjunction of embodied action, technological mediation, and social legibility.

N. Katherine Hayles gives us a broader reason not to imagine technology as an accessory attached to an otherwise complete, natural body. In *How We Became Posthuman*, she describes the body as an “original prosthesis.”[[8]](#ref-8) She does not mean that the body is literally an artificial replacement. Rather, embodiment has never been a finished essence. From before birth onward, a person develops capacities through a changing body that mediates perception and action. Later extensions and substitutions continue this process of acquiring and incorporating ways to act. Hayles therefore connects technical mediation to posthumanism without requiring the mind to abandon the body.

The opening tests let *Prey* stage this conjunction at two levels. Historically, the physical chambers were built for the original human Morgan to use actual Typhon abilities: Kinetic Blast to clear the boxes in Room A, Mimic Matter to become the chair in Room B, and Remote Manipulation to press the distant button in Room C. Later, inside the empathy simulation, the Typhon subject can return to the reconstructed chambers through the Morgan identity and perform those intended solutions. The subject experiences each power as a new way for Morgan to perceive and act. The reconstructed test, meanwhile, treats those actions as evidence of the technological alteration it was designed to measure. The same goals are reached through Neuromod-granted capacities rather than Morgan’s unmodified body.

Talos I’s security turrets turn this relation into a threat classification. Within the reconstructed crisis, installing enough Typhon abilities causes a turret to count the Morgan identity as alien and open fire. This is not the physical Typhon subject outside the simulation sprouting new neural material with each click. It is the empathy test reproducing a rule from Talos I: the station’s machines judged a human body by the amount of Typhon material in its brain. At the historical level, “human” had become a technologically calculated threshold rather than a self-evident species boundary. At the level of the empathy test, the physical Typhon subject experiences the consequences of that rule while acting through Morgan. In both cases, technology connects what a subject can do from within to the identity a machine assigns from outside. That mediated relation is what this essay calls body three.

## V. Constructing Morgan Yu

Many familiar cyborg narratives culminate in the fantasy of mind uploading: consciousness escapes into a machine or virtual world, leaving the physical body behind. In this version of transhumanism, flesh is a burden, an obsolete container for the self. Replace it with a more efficient machine, and consciousness can supposedly live forever in cyberspace.

*Prey* refuses that clean escape. In the scenario initially presented to the player, an amnesiac Morgan wakes on Talos I with no usable knowledge of the preceding years. The physically embodied Operators January and December issue incompatible instructions; recordings, messages, and videos supply still more accounts of the original human Morgan. Meanwhile, the player installs Neuromods and discovers what their capacities make possible by moving through the station. Installing an ability is only the beginning. Posthuman embodiment emerges as the player puts that ability to use, the environment responds, and other bodies and machines pass judgment.

Body one and body two therefore do not divide the story into one real Morgan and a collection of unreal copies. The original human Morgan had both a lived body and that same body as represented, classified, and interpreted from outside. The Operators, recordings, files, and memories preserve historical representations of the second aspect; they confront the playable Morgan identity as distinct objects within the story. Technology changes the relation between Morgan’s action and those outside accounts: it alters what Morgan can perceive and do, how the environment responds, and how institutions and machines classify the body. Cyberpunk, Veronica Hollinger writes, “radically decenters the human body, the sacred icon of the essential self.”[[9]](#ref-9) *Prey* performs precisely this decentering, then goes further.

The post-credits epilogue reveals the second experiment. Long after the original human Morgan endured the physically staged apartment loop, a physical Typhon subject lies in a laboratory and undergoes an empathy test. Human neural material has been implanted into that organism, while Morgan’s recorded memories supply the basis for the scenario. The test gives the subject a reconstructed Morgan identity and a simulated Talos I crisis assembled from those memories. The first “simulation” was a real human repeatedly moving through a deceptive but physical set; this later one is a reconstructed world experienced by an alien body.

Alex observes the Typhon from outside the simulation along with four Operator robots modeled on Talos I crew members Sarah Elazar, Mikhaila Ilyushin, Dayo Igwe, and Danielle Sho. The human crew members themselves are not standing in the laboratory. The Operators reproduce their voices and personae and report how the subject treated the corresponding characters inside the test. Like January and December, they appear here as embodied programs rather than as the human crew members themselves. Unlike January and December, they do not preserve reset states of Morgan: they form a panel that interprets the Typhon’s conduct and votes on whether it has shown human empathy.

The reveal reorganizes body one and body two without assigning them to different beings. For the Typhon subject, body one is the living alien organism as it perceives and acts through its human neural graft and the simulated Morgan perspective. Its experience of choosing, moving, and suffering as Morgan is virtual, but the embodied subject having that experience is physically present in Alex’s laboratory. Body two is that same Typhon body as Alex and the four Operator observers measure, interpret, and label it: empathic or monstrous, a possible ally or a failed experiment. Those social judgments are imposed on the Typhon itself.

The reconstructed Morgan identity belongs to another layer. It is built from historical representations of the original human Morgan—memories, records, roles, and the remembered station—but it is not by itself the Typhon’s body two, nor does it turn the original Morgan into the Typhon. It is the technological form through which the Typhon acts and through which the observers elicit conduct to judge. The simulation, neural graft, reconstructed identity, and observers’ instruments mediate between the Typhon’s lived experience and its socially classified body. What first looked like one human protagonist is thus a layered construction: a physical Typhon organism, human neural material, a represented Morgan identity, lived experience within a simulated world, and institutional judgments made outside it.

The playable subject—the Typhon organism acting through a simulated Morgan identity—therefore escapes any simple opposition between embodiment and disembodiment, lived experience and representation, the human and the technological. Susanne Lettow uses “somatechnologies” for practices that deliberately modify bodies and may even use bodily substances as technical means. Such practices are always embedded in social relations and structures of power.[[10]](#ref-10) The Neuromod is a literal somatechnology, as is the experiment performed on the Typhon subject.

The game labels each installed ability a “skill.” We can read that word through the idea of *technē*, or practical know-how, but this is an interpretive step, not the game’s own terminology. A Neuromod bypasses the ordinary process of learning and supplies a neural capacity instantly. It does not, by itself, supply the whole practice of using that capacity in a world. The Typhon subject, living through the Morgan identity, must still learn to use each power in practice, discover what it affords, and face the judgments that follow. Technology here is neither an external tool nor immaterial code that leaves the body behind. It becomes a bodily capacity while exposing the body to new forms of measurement, control, and judgment. *Prey* locates the posthuman body in the continuing interaction among lived action, technological mediation, and the institutions that decide what kind of body has acted.
