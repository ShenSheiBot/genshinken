### I. Introduction

Walter Benjamin witnessed the profound changes that industrialization brought to culture and art in the early twentieth century. In “The Work of Art in the Age of Its Technological Reproducibility,” he argued that mass reproduction eroded the artwork’s “aura”: the authority of its unique presence. Art became entangled with industrial cultural production, entered ever more deeply into social life, and acquired an overtly political function.

Benjamin was writing about printed matter, photography, and film. As reproductions circulated through these media, they ceased merely to copy a prior reality and began to form self-reproducing systems of their own. The virtual domain expanded without limit; through mass entertainment, the logic of capital appeared to exercise what Benjamin called a covert control over the public. Capitalist industry, fascist spectacle, and communist agitation could all make art an instrument for acting upon the masses. Benjamin’s call to politicize art was specifically a response to fascism’s aestheticization of politics.

The information age introduced another turn: digital cameras, the internet, software, and electronic screens became the media of art. Digital reproduction loosened the copy’s remaining bond to an original or referent, turning it into a simulacrum that proliferates as code. At the same time, increasingly accessible digital tools gave ordinary users new means of cultural production and transformed consumption once again. The question, then, is not only how technology changes artistic media, but how those changes reorganize cultural production, cultural consumption, and ultimately the form of society itself.

### II. Reproduction as Code

#### 1. The Myth of Integral Realism

Benjamin examined how reproducibility transformed artistic creation, perception, and politics. Bernard Stiegler’s philosophy of technics carries that inquiry forward by treating technical reproduction as an expanding field of fiction:

> “Techno-science-fiction is a reproduction-as-fiction industry.”[^reproduction-simulacra-1]

Stiegler approaches technical reproduction through the desires of its recipients—through the question of “what we want.” Industrial technoscience does not simply reveal an already existing reality. It invents possibilities and then realizes them; in that sense, it produces reality. The result is an endlessly proliferating fiction that draws society into a symbolic space governed by cultural production. Stiegler describes this as reproducibility—or, more precisely in the digital context, hyper-reproducibility: production conceived from the outset as serial reproduction, without an indispensable original.[^reproduction-simulacra-2]

Yet photography initially appeared to fulfill the opposite dream: a perfectly truthful copy of nature. The shock of the photograph seemed to arise from its instantaneous, apparently automatic inscription of the world. André Bazin grounded photography’s special claim to realism in precisely that automation:

> “For the first time an image of the world is formed automatically, without the creative intervention of man.”[^reproduction-simulacra-3]

For Bazin, photography’s aesthetic power lay in its capacity to disclose reality.[^reproduction-simulacra-4] In “The Myth of Total Cinema,” he gave the larger dream a name: a cinema capable of reproducing the external world in its entirety.[^reproduction-simulacra-5] On this account, cinema had failed to achieve integral realism only because its technology remained incomplete.

This gives us two senses of “reproduction.” In the first, transitive sense, an image reproduces something else. In the second, intransitive sense, reproductions proliferate through a system of their own. The early photographs discussed in Benjamin’s “Little History of Photography,” where traces of aura still linger, belong chiefly to the first sense. The mass-circulating works in his essay on technological reproducibility, freed from unique ownership and location, belong to the second. The two are not separate: self-proliferation still begins from some referent—reality, a photographic negative, a release print, or an original literary or artistic work.

The logic of reproduction, however, is never wholly faithful to that referent. Photography seems to guarantee fidelity through an automatic process, yet Benjamin argues that it also reveals an “optical unconscious”: details and patterns that intentional vision never chose to see. The photograph can therefore bring viewers into contact with impulses outside conscious attention, producing the very experience of shock. It does not merely record reality; it constructs another one and organizes our perception within it. As Benjamin puts it, photography reveals “image worlds, which dwell in the smallest things.”[^reproduction-simulacra-6] In the waking dream of the image, technology turns back into magic. The ideal of perfect reproduction thus becomes the fabrication of reality. However closely an image resembles the world, it first establishes a self-contained visual field; resemblance to an external reality comes afterward.

#### 2. The Database of Simulacra

In *Symbolic Exchange and Death*, Jean Baudrillard distinguishes three orders of simulacra: counterfeit, the dominant schema from the Renaissance to the Industrial Revolution; production, the schema of the industrial era; and simulation, the code-governed schema of the present.[^reproduction-simulacra-7] He associates them respectively with natural, market, and structural laws of value.

The Industrial Revolution brought the second order: the industrial simulacrum of mass production. A universal equivalence among reproducible objects becomes possible only when reference to an original ceases to matter. Production therefore tends to eliminate its own endpoint; as Baudrillard writes, “Simulacra prevail over history.”[^reproduction-simulacra-8]

Once industry repeats not a particular original but interchangeable copies, just as capital comes to reproduce its own value, we enter the third order. Here the production of simulacra resembles a word-chain game: one sign attaches to the next, and the continuation of the chain becomes an end in itself. Forms no longer proceed toward a final purpose; they issue from models as modulated differences. Baudrillard locates the completed form of this logic in genetic code and in the digital, programmatic sign, whose value lies in its tactical relation to other signals.[^reproduction-simulacra-9]

The essay’s deliberately schematic examples place marble sculpture and Baroque or Impressionist painting in the first order, propaganda and film posters in the second, and recombinant signs in the third. Otaku consumption supplies a particularly clear example of the last. *Hyperdimension Neptunia* builds its world around personifications of game consoles and companies: characters were conceived as the project’s central attraction, with story and mechanics constructed around them. Whatever one thinks of its plot or gameplay, the franchise found a substantial otaku audience. Its protagonist Neptune[^reproduction-simulacra-10] displays the consumption of codes at a glance: she personifies a console; her purple palette, youthful “loli” styling, game-controller ornaments, and relaxed moe clothing all point outward to gaming culture or to recurring conventions of anime character design. These “moe-elements” have acquired the force of tradition through repetition. What is consumed is not only the game’s content but its extension into other textual chains. Simulacra, in this account, are generated by the continuing translation and reproduction of codes.

[fig] Neptune’s design decomposed into recurring “moe-elements.” The annotations are the essay’s analysis of the character art.

![English diagram decomposing Neptune’s design into a youthful age design, purple palette, exaggerated proportions, game-controller ornaments, and her name incorporated into her clothing](attachments/roof-archive/cv11752140/translations/en/figure-4.png)

In *Otaku: Japan’s Database Animals*, Hiroki Azuma argues that Baudrillard describes the disappearance of the original but treats the resulting simulacra as a disorderly proliferation. Once the old tree structure organized around an original collapses, Azuma proposes, a database takes its place and establishes a different order—a new kind of “tradition.”[^reproduction-simulacra-11]

The database[^reproduction-simulacra-12] is an apt model for the source of simulacra. Like Neptune, almost any contemporary cultural product can be broken down into recurring features or codes. As those fragments reappear across works, they become conventional and are cited again in later works. A cultural sphere gradually acquires a set of recognizable signs—the image of Che Guevara on a T-shirt, for example—which can be recombined into new products at any time. In this limited structural sense, the *Avengers* films emerge from the database of American comics and Marvel much as *Hyperdimension Neptunia* emerges from the database of games and moe character design.

[fig] Azuma’s database model, localized from the diagram reproduced in the edition cited by the original essay.

![English reconstruction of Azuma’s database model: a grand nonnarrative database feeds shared modes of expression into combinations that produce simulacra, while an outlined simulacrum of another kind points toward another database](attachments/roof-archive/cv11752140/translations/en/figure-5.png)

Database consumption means continually reading information out of this structure: clicking from one related page, article, or video to the next. Azuma calls the resulting form “hyperflat.”[^reproduction-simulacra-13] A hyperlink does not carry us from one plane to a higher level; it jumps to another plane beside it. The database—the “grand nonnarrative” in the published English translation—generates small narratives, but reading one small narrative does not let us reconstruct the whole. The database is not a stable master story. It is an ever-changing mesh of planes and links.

In Bergsonian terms, we might call this grand nonnarrative virtual: it flows through what is written and produced, much as a recommendation feed or “You may also like” panel keeps opening new paths. To make a work is to actualize part of that flow, reconnecting loose elements into a readable plane. Derivative works and meme-making create further planes. But “readable” does not necessarily mean “meaningful.” As in a word-chain game, readability may guarantee only that one text can connect to another. Screen-bound users can therefore fall into a compulsive form of reading for its own sake, passing strings of signs into further strings. This is the experience of art in the age of digital reproducibility.

That formal account remains incomplete without the larger transformation of social life. The following two films offer contrasting images of the modern subject. From there, we can ask what cultural ecology contemporary consumers have built around the forms described above.

### III. The Situation of the Cultural Consumer

#### 1. Two Images of the “Masses”

As reproduction expanded, it displaced the authority of the original; the decline of aura also weakened a Platonic reverence for an underlying “real.” Changes in the form of the artwork brought changes in cultural production. Once the artwork’s ritual authority receded, its social basis shifted into the political and commercial domains. In the essay’s broad historical sketch, the original was reconstituted as property protected by copyright, while production moved from private commission toward a culture industry guided by anticipated demand and therefore by the logic of capital.

These changes joined art and the masses more closely than ever. “The mass is a matrix,” Benjamin writes, from which established ways of responding to art emerge in altered form.[^reproduction-simulacra-14] Yet mass participation did not automatically give the public a means of self-expression. Benjamin first saw the covert control that entertainment could exercise over audiences, and only from there considered their possible political mobilization.

In this account, the masses enter cultural production less often as autonomous subjects than as objects represented and shaped. Two films make that divided image especially clear. Sergei Eisenstein’s *Battleship Potemkin* (1925), a Soviet dramatization of the 1905 Revolution, builds its revolutionary force around representations of the masses. In Part III, “The Dead Man Calls for Justice,” three shots show people passing beneath a bridge on their way to the harbor to mourn the sailor killed during the mutiny. The bridge, flanking stairs, and passage below let Eisenstein turn layered streams of people into an image of firm, unified purpose.

[fig] The procession to the harbor in *Battleship Potemkin* (1925).

![Three black-and-white frames from Battleship Potemkin showing ordered streams of people moving above, beside, and beneath a bridge](attachments/roof-archive/cv11752140/figure-6.jpg)

At the sailor’s bier, grief becomes resistance to tsarist tyranny. An orator stands above the crowd and speaks toward the right; the next shot answers with faces turned left. A close-up of a hand gripping cloth concentrates the people’s anger, and raised arms in the following shot release it.

[fig] Eisenstein’s montage turns mourning into a collective call to resist tyranny.

![Four black-and-white frames from Battleship Potemkin: an orator addressing the crowd, attentive faces, a hand gripping cloth, and arms raised against tyranny](attachments/roof-archive/cv11752140/figure-7.jpg)

The crowd is organized as marching, still, shouting, and upraised bodies: a forceful, unitary mass capable of action. Montage gives order to a disorderly aggregate and joins shots into a visual grammar capable of expressing abstract concepts. The audience may have overlapped with the people represented on screen, making this a kind of self-display, but the displayed collective had already been arranged by cinematic form and subordinated to the idea the montage was designed to express. In this sense, film could both control and mobilize the masses. The people in the theater could recognize themselves as a collective only after cinema had constructed that collective for them. The possibility of self-expression remained, but identification could just as readily allow the screen to shape its viewers.

Vittorio De Sica’s Italian neorealist film *Bicycle Thieves* (1948) presents the opposite image. Crowds appear throughout as disorderly flows, and Antonio, the protagonist, is reduced to one figure among them. Gilles Deleuze describes the new relation between character and world:

> “The character has become a kind of viewer. . . . He records rather than reacts.”[^reproduction-simulacra-15]

Antonio’s actions are no longer guaranteed by classical narrative. The familiar schema—character acts, situation reacts—breaks down because the situation exceeds his power to answer it. Caught in what Deleuze calls a “pure optical and sound situation,” he can only watch. In one sequence, Antonio searches for his stolen bicycle in the rain. A point-of-view shot gives us the disorderly crowd; in the next external shot he almost disappears into it. Only the man pausing in the downpour resolves into the protagonist. A final high angle intensifies his powerlessness: his frantic search has made him indistinguishable from everyone around him.

[fig] Antonio searches for his stolen bicycle and nearly vanishes into the crowd in *Bicycle Thieves* (1948).

![Four black-and-white frames from Bicycle Thieves showing a rain-soaked crowd, Antonio looking into it, Antonio nearly lost among umbrellas, and a high-angle view of the street](attachments/roof-archive/cv11752140/figure-8.jpg)

Crowds occupy even more of *Bicycle Thieves* than of *Battleship Potemkin*, but they convey neither order nor unity. The film offers no stable “image of the masses,” only the inertia of everyday life. Its extended takes dwell on Antonio walking down a street or on fragments of routine activity. Instead of guiding the audience toward a concept through montage, neorealist duration suspends that obligation and leaves viewers to share Antonio’s uncertainty. Film grammar no longer encloses the protagonist and gives every movement a meaning. He watches and records but cannot respond effectively. In this reading, neorealism’s optical and sound situations disenchant the magic of montage.

The individual in the age of technological reproduction thus carries a divided image of the masses. The social or political collective promises sovereignty by absorbing each citizen into a powerful, unitary myth. Yet the same individual also finds themself lost in a strange and disorderly crowd, able to observe but unable to act. When cultural production is conscripted by political power, the problem is not merely that an individual lacks a voice. Cultural production captures the individual, and identification with its first, heroic image of the masses offers an escape from the helplessness of the second.

#### 2. The Cultural Tribespeople of the Postmodern

In a lecture titled “The Proletarianization of Sensibility,” Stiegler argues that the culture industry proletarianized consumers by stripping them of know-how.[^reproduction-simulacra-16] A person who listens to music on the radio need not know how to perform it; someone who admires reproductions of paintings need not understand a painter’s craft. Technology impoverished the amateur’s practical knowledge and reduced the amateur to a consumer.

As digital tools displaced many older industrial means of production, however, sensibility underwent a second machinic turn. Video editing, postproduction, and digital drawing became accessible through consumer software. This brought a renaissance of the amateur and allowed cultural consumers to reclaim part of the libidinal economy:

> What is the amateur, if not a figure of libidinal economy? The amateur “loves” (*amat*, from the Latin *amare*, “to love”); that love is precisely what makes an amateur. Works of art act upon those who love them. In revealing themselves, they can also give rise to doctrines, schools, circles, sects, and even schisms. Kant says that when I judge a work beautiful, I necessarily suppose that everyone ought to find it beautiful—though I know that things never will be exactly as I wish.[^reproduction-simulacra-17]

First, this passage describes the political condition of art in the digital age: art becomes art through the amateur’s love, and culture and its devotees construct one another. It also points toward digital culture’s tribal tendency. Devotion to a cultural “idol” gathers congregations that repeat shared doctrines internally and oppose rival congregations externally. In terms of the database model, each congregation shares a smaller database nested within a larger one and set against neighboring databases—a set-theoretic structure of discourse systems within discourse systems.

The “helpless subject” described above still seeks a collective, but under postmodern conditions that collective need not be a unitary People. It can fragment into countless smaller publics. By becoming an amateur of a particular culture, the subject joins a fan community or cultural sphere: a place to form ties, speak a shared language, and circulate or jointly create cultural products.

What such a mode of cultural consumption brings, however, is the tribalization of culture:

> Unlike historical “grand narratives,” the database’s “small narratives” have no sovereign authority to draw a public boundary. To defend their legitimacy and habitat, expel noise and incompatible elements, and distinguish friend from enemy or inside from outside, they exclude and attack other narratives. The commonality produced by these small narratives is therefore exclusionary.[^reproduction-simulacra-18]

The Japanese critic Tsunehiro Uno develops Azuma’s account into a problem of communication between cultural spheres. Azuma’s database theory suggests that characters generated from shared codes remain related, and that this web of relations could connect different small narratives. Uno objects that although analysts can decompose characters into common signs, a character becomes a coherent object of devotion only by effacing that constructed origin. The exclusionary narrative and the worshipful identification it sustains depend on that effacement:

> Strictly speaking, a character is not simply a sign. A character exists when a story establishes the common identity of its traits. As the character separates from the original work, changes, and is consumed through derivative works, that process reinforces both the shared identity of those traits and the narrative that defines it. Azuma treats the character in database consumption as a critique of narrative, but characters often supply the very commonality on which narrative depends; they can become the source of “small narratives.” A worldwide technical connection may be possible, but narrative connection is not. What forms instead is a small narrative without internal discrepancy, open only to those who recognize the character’s defining traits.[^reproduction-simulacra-19]

From the standpoint of production, digital culture appears to offer a vast space of connection and exchange. People draw codes from many databases, add their own inventions, and make new images, characters, and works. Because a character assembles widely shared codes, it might seem able to link cultural spheres. Uno’s argument reverses that logic. At the moment a character coalesces from fragments, it suppresses the fact of its own construction, presents itself as unique, and becomes the “idol” of a new exclusionary narrative. The common elements visible from outside do not necessarily provide a channel between devotees. Resemblance may instead provoke sectarian defensiveness: only *our* character may possess those traits, while anyone who confuses the two is mocked as ignorant. The response reveals the tribe’s exclusionary structure.

On this reading, database consumption does not by itself provide a position from which to criticize its own discourse systems. Members of cultural tribes find no route to the other from within their small narratives, because the tribe secures itself by excluding that possibility. Communication becomes the era’s sustaining myth. After the decline of the grand narratives embodied by *Battleship Potemkin*, contemporary subjects may no longer have to identify with a monumental political aesthetic; they nevertheless retreat into closed cultural tribes.

It is therefore not enough to say that a capital-controlled culture industry manipulates individual taste. The cultural ecology itself is moving toward an isolating and exclusionary tribalism. This is not only an external problem of production but an internal problem of reception. No single conspiratorial machine has to separate people; the tribes reproduce themselves through refusals of communication encouraged by cultural form. Worse, the culture industry’s images and discourse systems may overwhelm those scattered tribes and bring a “third machinic turn of sensibility”: a cultural machine that conscripts amateur creativity and erects a socially unified aesthetic standard. Aesthetic standards can never be literally universal, but they can become sovereign, suppressing every narrative outside the imagined social whole. Art and literature would again bear the stamp of politics—not in the name of an explicit ideology, but in the name of “popular taste.”

### IV. Conclusion

Today aesthetic experience and cultural consumption are more tightly bound than ever, and their political character has not diminished since Benjamin’s time. Together they have become a form of social bond after the weakening of older interpersonal ties, as well as an object of secular belief. The cultural consumer remains a powerless figure, captured by images and compulsively repeating the act of viewing. Yet the database also opens new possibilities for cultural creation. We may hope, with Deleuze, that the differential repetition of cultural codes can trace lines of flight beyond dominant discourse. We must also recognize the conservatism within that differentiation, and the closure and exclusion that “aesthetic” affiliation can now imply.

This closure does not arise simply because people respond differently to the same work. In Benjamin’s experience of shock, sensibility received a rush of unfamiliar stimuli. In the contemporary aesthetics of the simulacrum, sensibility recedes behind a constant labor of reading: the elements of a work become legible codes that extend textual chains through the database. Every expressive device becomes citable. Exclusion grows not from the universal capacity to read, but from the particular contexts and objects of reading—from the database and cultural sphere in which the reader stands. The aesthetic subject is still captured by the work, but no longer only through passive sensory overwhelm. Capture now also works through the subject’s own active reading, classification, and belonging. . . .
