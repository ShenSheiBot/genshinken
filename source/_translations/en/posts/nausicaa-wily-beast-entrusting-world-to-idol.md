## The Animal in the Mirror

Animals may be the richest source material for human culture. They crowd the bronzes of the Shang and Zhou dynasties and the walls of Egyptian tombs; they stalk through Wu Cheng’en’s *Journey to the West* and Ryūnosuke Akutagawa’s *Kappa*; they return, cheerfully redesigned, in Fujiko F. Fujio’s *Doraemon* and Hypergryph’s *Arknights*. We have conquered and enslaved animals, but we have also admired them, desired them, and worshiped them as gods.

Ever since the first cave paintings, we have imagined animals in two broad ways: by finding the animal in the human, or by making the animal more human. *Journey to the West* offers a classic example of the first. The pilgrimage leads the master and his disciples away from unruly appetite as they submit their animal impulses to Buddhist discipline. Yet animality need not imply degradation. In the Suwa-region legend of Kōga Saburō, a warrior becomes a serpent and takes on its divine power.[^1]

[fig] An adult doujinshi based on *Is the Order a Rabbit?* (*Gochūmon wa Usagi Desu ka?*). Rabbits’ prodigious fertility made them spirits of prosperity and abundant harvests in European folklore. At Easter, springtime rebirth and Christian belief merge, yet the central figure is not Christ but the rabbit that “creates new life.” Rabbit lore plainly has an erotic side, and the modern bunny girl developed from it; the fantasy casts the wearer as “a woman ready for sex at any time.” *GochiUsa* plays the connection between rabbits and girls in an entirely wholesome register, but the series’ explosive popularity may still owe something to the rather different meanings modern fans have found in it.

![Cover of an adult doujinshi featuring a bunny-girl version of a GochiUsa character](attachments/roof-archive/cv8622821/01-rabbit-girl-doujin.png "=33%")

Ancient shamans took animal form to open a path to heaven. Modern capitalism has found other uses for the same trick. Cat ears became a reliable way for both fictional girls on-screen and real performers in front of the camera to win otaku hearts; now even a rabbit can be “ordered” at a café. Animal traits have entered the database of contemporary popular culture, ready to be combined with every other available element and sold back to us as something new but oddly familiar.

There has never been a pure animal inside this machinery of the imagination. Once an animal passes through human fantasy and our systems of classification, it emerges with a human outline. This is one implication of Jacques Derrida’s phrase “the animal that therefore I am.”[^2] Outside the strict domain of zoology, “human” and “animal” have always been mirrors for one another. We ask whether animals feel, whether they have rights, whether they think as we do. Humanity remains the yardstick.

Modern philosophy’s great rediscovery of the animal is that the line between animal and human is not merely scientific. It also organizes power. The two sides have never enjoyed equal standing, and the border can move. A society may extend some of what it calls human rights to selected animals, then strip inconvenient people of those same rights in war or disaster and treat them as livestock. Giorgio Agamben calls the system that performs this sorting an “anthropological machine”: it manufactures the human by identifying—and sometimes destroying—the nonhuman within it.[^3]

Seen this way, modern fantasies of superior and inferior peoples belong to the same family as the dream of conquering nature. Some scholars locate their social roots in the old opposition between the human soul and the animal body. Eurocentrism and the Hua–Yi division between a civilized Chinese center and a barbarian fringe are simply variations on the claim that humanity stands at the summit of creation. Nazi Germany was one such anthropological machine, built on superiority and domination. Even after its destruction, Iron Curtain politics, patriarchal authority, racial discrimination, environmental crisis, and religious conflict kept mocking the ideal of equality. Idealists and artists could do little more than summon avenging giants such as Godzilla into the heart of the city, let them fire their destructive beams and sweep evil away, and entrust their hopes once more to the friendship human beings ought to be capable of.

[fig] The Reunion Movement logo in *Arknights*, drawn like a broken gene sequence. Biopolitics—the power to classify, manage, and expend forms of life—is the keynote of the game’s story.

![The angular black-and-white logo of the Reunion Movement in Arknights](attachments/roof-archive/cv8622821/02-reunion-movement-logo.png "=25%")

The animal question, then, is no hobbyhorse for sentimental advocates. Without other animals, human beings could scarcely have built agricultural societies; how we live with our “companion species” remains bound up with our own survival. The coronavirus pandemic that began in 2020—the Year of the Rat—was entangled with human arrogance toward animals. Modern society’s cold machinery reduced infected people to animalized “bare life.”

Those of us who came of age around the millennium were shaped, in no small part, by the popular culture of Heisei Japan (1989–2019). Its creators returned obsessively to animals. Jun’ya Ōta—better known as ZUN, and nearly as famous for drinking as for making *Touhou*—likes the proverb “curse another and you dig two graves.” The meaning is simple: a curse rebounds on the person who casts it. The animals of Heisei stories work in much the same way. Whatever fate we devise for them returns to us. Derrida’s animal does not merely receive our gaze; it looks back.

## The Sea of Corruption and the Ohmu: An Artificial Ecosystem for Purifying Humanity

The artists who confronted this question most fiercely belonged to the Shōwa generation, which had lived through war before Heisei began. They had seen human beings reduced to animals by the machinery of empire. In the postwar decades, they became the mentors—and often the bad conscience—of manga and animation. Hayao Miyazaki began his long manga *Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind* in 1982, near the end of Shōwa, and finished it in 1994, early in Heisei. The friendship it imagines between a girl and immense, alien creatures is one of that generation’s most searching encounters with the animal question.

The nonhuman protagonists of *Nausicaä* are insects and fungi. After the Seven Days of Fire destroys industrial civilization, the ecosystem they form—the Sea of Corruption—spreads across the earth. Its spores poison human lungs, and its forests consume the dwindling habitable land. Humanity clings to the edge of this insect kingdom and goes on slaughtering itself over what remains.

[fig] Nausicaä confronts the intelligence inside the Crypt of Shuwa: “We are birds who, though we may spit up blood, will go on flying beyond that morning, on and on!”[^4] The “winged savior” answers a program for human perfection with an animal metaphor.

![Manga panels of Nausicaä confronting the Master of the Crypt of Shuwa](attachments/roof-archive/cv8622821/translations/en/03-nausicaa-manga-ohmu.png "=75%")

The space between human and nonhuman in *Nausicaä* is densely populated: Torumekians, Doroks, Wormhandlers, Forest People, Heedra,[^5] God Warriors, Ohmu, slime molds, and the trees grown as insect food. More importantly, the wasteland contains no pure, original humanity. Biotechnology has already altered the survivors of the Seven Days of Fire so that they can endure a poisoned world. Their ancestors also designed the Sea of Corruption, an artificial ecosystem meant to purify the planet. For all the messianic imagery of her flight, Nausicaä herself is one more engineered, animalized life.

Nor was the Sea’s purification merely environmental. It was the centerpiece of a political program to purify the human race.

To the program’s designers, every community and every form of life outside the Crypt of Shuwa is an instrument. The great insect tribes, the Torumekians and Doroks, the people of the Valley of the Wind, the Wormhandlers, the Forest People who have learned to live inside the Sea, and the artificial Heedra all exist to complete a plan for beneficiaries not yet born. They are biologically alive but have no recognized claim on the future: bare life, in Agamben’s sense, stripped of political standing and therefore of any right to endure.[^6]

At the manga’s climax, Nausicaä accuses the intelligence at the center of the Crypt of deceiving a population already condemned to die. “Do you intend to go on deceiving us until the very day you plan to destroy us?” she demands. “Because no matter how much knowledge and technology you have, you will still need slaves to do the work for you on the morning you replace the world!” The Crypt is both the headquarters of the purification project and a vast biological artificial intelligence. It trades in the lost biotechnology of the old world, manipulating governments, prolonging wars, and cultivating agents who will make the surviving humans into flesh-and-blood servants as obedient as the Heedra.

The Crypt is also a womb. It preserves embryos of the “new humanity” designed by the old civilization: bodies suited to the purified planet and minds supposedly more intelligent and docile than those of their ignorant, violent, acquisitive predecessors. Once the Sea has completed its work, the old humans—unable to breathe in a clean environment—will cough blood and die on the “pure blue land.” The Sea and its inhabitants will perish with them, their assignment complete. Taken literally, this ecosystem is an organic anthropological machine built to cleanse the earth by removing everyone now alive.

[fig] An armed Heedra in the *Nausicaä* manga, one of the biological weapons the Doroks obtain from the Master of the Crypt, repository of the old world’s science.

![Armed Heedra looming over Dorok soldiers in the Nausicaä manga](attachments/roof-archive/cv8622821/translations/en/04-nausicaa-manga-shuwa.png "=75%")

Seen through humanist eyes, the plan has the grandeur of something like the Human Instrumentality Project. Its architects belonged to the same old humanity that had poisoned the world and fought without end. In an age of despair, an intellectual elite devised a brutal form of atonement: their own kind would die at the dawn of a better race. The Master of the Crypt presents this program to Nausicaä as light and hope.

She refuses. Beneath the language of penance, she recognizes the same old arrogance, still intact. Whether it speaks for the old humans or the new, the Crypt assumes that humanity is the sovereign subject of the world. Everything else is raw material to be redesigned or destroyed. Its artificial organisms do not end war; they accelerate it. Heedra supersoldiers, infant Ohmu used as decoys, and a gigantic, all-devouring slime mold fall into human hands and feed the cycle of revenge. Even the wasteland’s endless wars serve the program, because they hasten both the spread of the Sea and the extinction of the old race.

[fig] “The sympathy and love of the Ohmu were born from the depths of nothingness.… Life is the light that shines in the darkness.”[^7]

![Nausicaä and the Master of the Crypt arguing over life, nihilism, and the Ohmu](attachments/roof-archive/cv8622821/translations/en/05-nausicaa-life-in-darkness.png "=75%")

The plan’s final vanity is its claim to have manufactured perfect humanity. The new race will supposedly possess what animals, artificial organisms, machines, and old humans lack: intelligence, benevolence, and the other virtues the Crypt gathers under the name “the light of life.” Nausicaä exposes the claim as empty. Care for another being and the wisdom to live beside it are not hereditary ornaments. They emerge through contact. The Ohmu’s compassion, she insists, arose from an abyss of nothingness.

The designers cannot have intended the insects guarding the Sea to befriend human beings. Yet over the centuries, the Ohmu learned to accept them. The artificial ecosystem even made room for human inhabitants: the Forest People live beneath the poisonous canopy, and the insects share their eggs with them for food. Human armies, by contrast, cannot manage friendship within their own species. After a thousand years of collisions between the Sea and the human world, an insect swarm learns to pity a human girl. A system built to use living things as tools has produced, against its makers’ design, what Emmanuel Levinas calls a welcome for the infinitely alien other. Humans fail to pity humans; “animals” pity them instead. For purifiers who treat stupidity, greed, cruelty, and violence as animal traits to be cut from the human race, the irony could hardly be sharper.

But if the planet is dying and the Crypt’s answer is intolerable, what remains? Nausicaä’s answer is coexistence among companion species. Her civilization has reached catastrophe because it does not know how to make friends. It cannot meet alien forms of life, inside or outside humanity, as equals—or even with humility. Anthropocentrism divides the world according to a human standard, then uses the anthropological machine to decide who belongs on either side.

![Cover of Donna Haraway’s The Companion Species Manifesto](attachments/roof-archive/cv8622821/06-companion-species-manifesto.png "=25%")

Donna Haraway replaces this rigid opposition with the messier idea of “companion species.” In *The Companion Species Manifesto*, she begins with dogs, among the animals most intimately entangled with human history, and shows that neither side simply made the other. Dogs and people have shaped one another and evolved together.[^8] This is not a one-way relation in which one side gazes, tames, and masters while the other is objectified, tamed, and enslaved. It is a dialogue between different forms of life. Humanity is not one half of a universe divided into human and nonhuman; it is one species among many, caught in networks of mutual transformation.

The creatures of the Sea of Corruption are therefore two things at once: an apocalyptic host consuming human civilization and companions through whom another future becomes imaginable. “Living things change,” Nausicaä tells the Crypt. Ohmu, slime molds, plants, and humans all change; human beings may yet learn to live with the Sea. They may cough blood along the way and still fly beyond that morning. Coexistence promises no clean path, only the long labor through which each side alters the other. Compassion awakens in beings built as tools; perhaps people freed from the spell of their own centrality can learn the same welcome.

The final confrontation at Shuwa is thus a clash between two kinds of hope. The Crypt says: refine the design, perfect the species, eliminate the defect. Nausicaä answers: remain humble, accept mixture, and let living things become what their makers never intended. At the crossroads of human survival, Miyazaki chooses the second: a posthumanist future made by companion species rather than a humanist future planned in advance.

The swarm that overwhelms civilization is only one of Miyazaki’s images of nature overwhelming human mastery. The radiant Forest Spirit of *Princess Mononoke* and the flood in *Ponyo* likewise bring human authority to its knees. Miyazaki reveres the animal other while landing glancing, sometimes gleeful, blows on human self-importance. *Nausicaä* also bears the imprint of the Cold War. The Seven Days of Fire is thermonuclear war carried to its endpoint; the wasteland is divided between Torumekia in the west and Dorok in the east; the small Valley of the Wind, a vassal on the edge of the Sea, recalls Japan under American occupation. Against the two blocs’ endless killing, the friendship of the insects becomes a rebuke. War is the anthropological machine in its highest gear: everyone inside it becomes either a tool or an animal marked for slaughter.

[fig] In *Weathering with You*, a story steeped in sacrifice and distrustful of human mastery, Makoto Shinkai finally grants Miyazaki’s old wish to let a great flood swallow Tokyo.

![Japanese theatrical poster for Weathering with You, showing its protagonists beneath a rain-filled sky](attachments/roof-archive/cv8622821/07-weathering-with-you-poster.jpg "=33%")

Nazi Germany and imperial Japan shattered the humanist promise of liberty, equality, and fraternity with two of modernity’s most terrible machines for turning people into nonhumans. In their wake, philosophers placed the animal and the friend on the political agenda. Japanese artists who had seen war, meanwhile, made animals the standard-bearers of posthumanism: Miyazaki in *Nausicaä* and *Porco Rosso*, Osamu Tezuka in *Jungle Emperor* and *Phoenix*, and Fujiko F. Fujio in numerous *Doraemon* stories and feature films.[^9] These works from the Cold War and its final years set human and animal directly against one another. In stories simple enough for children and strange enough to trouble adults, they exposed human arrogance amid war and pollution and offered fragile friendship as an answer worth tending, however difficult it might be to realize in the world.

The Cold War’s constant nuclear threat has receded from more recent animal stories. Works such as *Yurikuma Arashi* and *Kemono Friends* prefer the gentler—and more treacherous—form of the parable.

## The Girl Who Became a Bear: Love for the Other

Kunihiko Ikuhara studied stage arts before moving, like Miyazaki, into animation, where he brought a theatrical sensibility to the medium. He has not directed many works, but that intensely personal body of work is central to the visual language of Heisei anime. Together with his close friend Hideaki Anno’s *Neon Genesis Evangelion*, Ikuhara’s *Revolutionary Girl Utena* helped define the medium’s fin-de-siècle imagination. In *Yurikuma Arashi* (2015), four years after *Mawaru Penguindrum*, he returned to animal themes.

[fig] The three principal characters of *Yurikuma Arashi*: Lulu (left), Kureha (center), and Ginko (right). As in *GochiUsa*, once young women are given animal motifs, even the most wholesome story seems to acquire an erotic charge forever on the verge of breaking the surface.

![The three principal girls on the sixth Yurikuma Arashi home-video cover](attachments/roof-archive/cv8622821/08-yurikuma-arashi-cover.jpg "=33%")

The series begins with a familiar premise: love across species in a world divided into hostile camps. Ginko, a black bear, crosses the Wall of Severance with the brown bear Lulu, takes human form, and pursues a promised kiss from Kureha, the human girl who befriended her in childhood. Bear and human, predator and prey, have been cast as opposites; Ginko and Kureha’s love must cross that divide.[^10]

Ikuhara distinguishes two kinds of love. The first begins in sameness: it calls those who resemble us friends and casts the alien as an enemy. This is the Western tradition of thinking about friendship that Derrida dismantles in *The Politics of Friendship*.[^11] At Arashigaoka Girls’ Academy, an anthropological machine called the Exclusion Ceremony enforces friendship based on likeness. Its oath makes the logic explicit:

> “Friends are what matter most, aren’t they? The friends in this classroom are our whole world. Anyone who rejects how we feel is the worst. Anyone who sets herself apart is no good. Anyone who refuses to become one of us is a nuisance. Anyone who can’t read the room is evil. Now we must decide which evil person to exclude.”

Uniforms, hunting rifles, smartphones, electronic ostracism,[^12] and a bureaucracy of class presidents, ordinary students, and “bad girls” turn the academy into a miniature modern state. Its language of friendship creates the heretics it needs in order to discipline them.

![Cover of Jacques Derrida’s The Politics of Friendship](attachments/roof-archive/cv8622821/09-politics-of-friendship-cover.jpg "=25%")

Against this fellowship of sameness, *Yurikuma Arashi* sets love across difference: between Kureha’s mother Reia and the golden bear Yuriika, between Kureha and Ginko, and in Sumika’s resistance to the Invisible Storm and its Exclusion Ceremony. Sumika is the human avatar of Kumaria, the bear goddess. Kumaria sets the conflict in motion—she gives bears intelligence and allows some to assume human form—but also preaches coexistence across the resulting divide.

After Kureha is separated from Ginko in childhood, Sumika becomes her closest friend. When Yuriika’s plotting makes Kureha the next target of exclusion, Sumika declares herself a heretic and gives her life to shield Kureha from persecution. She also guides Kureha back to Ginko. Through another ursine avatar, the presiding judge of the Severance Court, Kumaria repeatedly asks the girls, “Is your love the real thing?”

Her answer appears through Reia, her messenger, in “The Moon Girl and the Forest Girl,” a fairy tale Kureha’s mother wrote for her. The moon is the human world, the forest the world of bears. To meet, the two girls must pass a divine test: they must shatter the “I” in the mirror. The image belongs as much to fairy tale as to Lacan. When the ego recognizes itself in a reflection, it forms a self for whom the world is organized as “for me,” then takes that self as the measure of everything else. Other beings are admitted only if they can be made into versions of the self—alter egos that the self can absorb and possess.

[fig] The Invisible Storm made visible: the Exclusion Ceremony turns ostracism into an app, surrounding each voter with the names of those eligible to be cast out.

![A student using the phone-based Exclusion Ceremony in Yurikuma Arashi](attachments/roof-archive/cv8622821/10-yurikuma-arashi-scene.png "=75%")

Levinas argues that reducing the other to an alter ego is already a form of conquest. Derrida finds the same master–slave relation hidden in friendships founded only on likeness. *Yurikuma Arashi* gives this regression to sameness a bodily form. Bullied as a child for befriending a black bear, Kureha asks Kumaria to make Ginko human. The wish does not restore their friendship; Kureha forgets Ginko. A love that makes the other resemble the self has merely replaced difference with possession.

Ginko takes the opposite route. She masters her own desire to devour humans, surrenders her bear identity, and approaches Kureha with a human face. The Severance Court repeatedly affirms her love, but the story does not end with an animal becoming acceptable to a human. At the climax, Kureha confesses the arrogance of her childhood wish: by making Ginko the same as herself, she tried to control her.

Kureha then makes another wish. This time the Moon Girl breaks the mirror. During the Exclusion Ceremony that the human students convene against Ginko, Kureha becomes a bear and kisses Ginko in her human form. She does not pull the other into her own category; she gives up the safety of that category and crosses into the other’s place. In Levinas’s severe language, she becomes the other’s hostage. She steps in front of the rifle aimed at Ginko and answers love with unlimited responsibility.

[fig] The famous final scene and the conclusion of *Yurikuma Arashi*: the Moon Girl shatters the “self” in the mirror, breaks free of the solipsism of friendship among one’s own kind, and turns to face the other.

![A riflewoman framed by flying mirror shards in the Yurikuma Arashi finale](attachments/roof-archive/cv8622821/11-yurikuma-arashi-transformation.png "=75%")

The same logic governs Ginko’s bond with Lulu. Earlier, Ginko leaves Sumika to die rather than lose Kureha, while Lulu tries to remove Kureha as a romantic rival. Both treat love as possession. Subjectivity emerges instead through giving: Kureha becomes a bear, and Lulu takes a bullet for Ginko. For Levinas, I become a self only by giving myself for another. The Exclusion Ceremony’s friendship based on sameness is therefore narcissism in disguise. Ginko’s human form and Kureha’s bear form tear down the Wall of Severance from both sides. Loving oneself and loving another no longer have to be opposites, because each self has allowed the other to transform it.[^13]

## From Zoo Exhibits to Friends

TATSUKI, whose birth year has not been made public, belongs to the millennial generation of Heisei animators and became one of late Heisei’s defining creators through his animal-centered stories. “Ruins” and “animals” were an interwoven pair of late-Heisei motifs. Together they spoke to the two forces dominating Japan: a bureaucratic class forever bowing to the United States and an economy left to stagnate, breeding an office-drone culture gone to seed. For generations raised in mid- and late Heisei, Japan’s wars of aggression had receded into distant, hazy memory. The political energy of postwar Shōwa had faded as well: young people carrying portraits of Mao, demonstrating against the US–Japan security treaty, and occupying the University of Tokyo now belonged to another age.

Miyazaki’s politics shifted between the eras, and he became increasingly explicit about distancing himself from the left. A Heisei office worker—or a student headed for office life—might see society as miserable and resistance as futile, but the screen could still offer a passage into an isekai harem full of animal-girl wives and free of ordinary demands. Fantasy makes that domesticated, petlike version of “animal life” just bearable enough. It is a caustic generational portrait: the hard-bitten “men of Shōwa” give way to Heisei’s exhausted, escapist otaku. By the end of the era, the immense and noble alien beings of Miyazaki’s early-Heisei apocalypse have shrunk into lovably dim animal girls for an exhausted office worker to collect on a phone. *Kemono Friends* emerged from this change in scale and mood.

[fig] The transformation of Heisei’s animal characters: from the fearsome, noble giants of early Heisei to the adorably dim animal girls displayed for office workers at the era’s end.

![Nausicaä beside an Ohmu contrasted with Serval and Kaban from Kemono Friends](attachments/roof-archive/cv8622821/12-posthuman-animal-friends-comparison.jpg "=75%")

The 2017 anime made TATSUKI famous almost overnight. Yet neither half of its premise—“animal girls” and a zoo—comes without baggage. An animal girl combines a woman’s body with selected animal features, while the woman remains immediately legible beneath the ears and tail. The bunny girl, the form’s modern emblem, belongs to a male erotic culture. Most such designs invite the viewer to enjoy a woman rendered petlike: cute, available, and safely subordinate.

The zoo carries its own history. It is anthropocentrism in architectural form, a place where humans look and animals are displayed. In *Discipline and Punish*, Michel Foucault compares the Panopticon to a royal menagerie: both arrange bodies so that power can inspect them from the center. But *Kemono Friends* begins after that center has collapsed. Its zoo consists of broken walls and rubble in the aftermath of a human apocalypse.

Levinas attacked the sovereignty of the Cartesian subject—the world revolving around “me”—and imagined an ethical subject called into responsibility by another being. His philosophy helped open a path out of anthropocentrism, but Levinas himself stopped at the human border. Animals, he thought, entered philosophy only in relation to human needs: as tools, pleasures, or objects of aesthetic appreciation. They could not confront us as the radically other. This limit in his thought reproduces the human-centered tradition of animal philosophy in miniature.[^14]

Derrida begins elsewhere: naked in a bathroom under the gaze of his cat. His embarrassment matters. An object cannot look back, but this animal does. The philosopher discovers himself becoming an object inside another creature’s world. In *The Animal That Therefore I Am*, he uses the encounter to approach the animal as an other whose difference cannot be absorbed into a general category.[^15]

[fig] Derrida’s cat, recast as a cat-eared girl facing cat-shaped morsels: “We look at them, and they look back at us.”

![A blue-haired cat-eared girl peering at small cat-shaped morsels on a plate](attachments/roof-archive/cv8622821/13-kizuna-ai-eating.png "=75%")

The “animal friends” of Japanese popular culture can be read as attempts to give this other a body. Miyazaki makes animal bodies majestic precisely because they remain radically unlike ours. Ikuhara has humans and bears exchange forms to break the narcissism of the mirror, giving his animals human faces. *Kemono Friends* chooses the hybrid: each girl is both human and animal, an intermediate body that has become one of contemporary Japanese popular culture’s dominant forms.

The hybrid’s double nature keeps its meaning unstable. On one side of the hybrid, the animal girl is effectively human. Most viewers see a cute girl first; ears, tails, and markings become “moe elements” attached to her. Perhaps the viewer is only enjoying a human reflection and, like Kureha wishing Ginko human, conquering difference by drawing it back into the self.

On the other, every Friend is different. Each derives from a distinct species, appears to be the only representative of that species, and retains a singular set of abilities. Derrida coined the word *animot*—a play on the French plural *animaux* and *mot*, “word”—to resist the way “the Animal” compresses innumerable lives into one faceless mass.[^16] The Friends are not the anonymous animals on a menu or in a slaughterhouse. Like the people around us, each is a living subject who cannot be exchanged for another.

For Levinas, the other takes ethical priority over me; I become a responsible subject by answering that claim. Kaban learns what kind of animal she is only through the animals she meets.[^17] Japari Park is a society that welcomes the stranger. Swimming, flying, digging, and hunting distinguish one Friend from the next, but those differences become grounds for cooperation and admiration rather than rank. Kaban seems to possess no special ability, yet no one excludes her. Only among these animal others does she discover the specifically human ability to combine their knowledge and make a world.

[fig] The volcanic setting of *Kemono Friends* echoes Disney’s *Brother Bear*: in both, transformation opens the way to love across species.

![The fiery mountain above the clouds in Kemono Friends](attachments/roof-archive/cv8622821/14-kemono-friends-volcano.png "=75%")

In that sense, *Kemono Friends* contains neither animal girls nor a zoo. The Foucauldian machine that cages bodies in order to discipline them[^18] and the Lacanian image that turns the animal girl into a flattering human reflection have both been buried under the ruins, along with anthropocentrism itself. The inhabitants of Japari Park do not call anyone an “animal girl.” They use one word: Friend. In humanity’s absence, the Friends keep asking for a form of love that does not begin by making them human.[^19]

## Silicon Idols and the Heisei Animal Question

ZUN reads voraciously and builds his work out of allusion. Over the decades, the *Touhou Project* has become an unruly archive of its era, a weathervane for Heisei popular culture. *Touhou Kikeijuu ~ Wily Beast and Weakest Creature* appeared in 2019, the first year of Reiwa, and inevitably looks both backward and forward. Behind the game’s nominal incident is a struggle among three powers in Hell: animal spirits, human spirits, and clay idols. Together they form a compact period portrait of the animal question.

Ideas such as the anthropological machine and love for the other predate Heisei. They carry the scars of industrial modernity and the world wars; postindustrial culture both criticizes that inheritance and remains shaped by it. We have already found a Shōwa-era legacy inside Heisei animal stories. Does the animal question also have a distinctly Heisei form?

![Cover art for Touhou Kikeijuu ~ Wily Beast and Weakest Creature](attachments/roof-archive/cv8622821/15-wily-beast-and-weakest-creature-cover.jpg "=50%")

Heisei was the age when society moved onto the screen. A device carried in a pocket could connect almost anyone to a network almost anywhere, while ubiquitous screens and sensors produced the enormous datasets on which artificial intelligence thrives. Today’s systems remain limited, but AlphaGo’s victories over the world’s strongest Go players made one fact difficult to ignore: inorganic circuits could produce moves that even experts experienced as creative.

Artificial intelligence and animals may seem worlds apart, but they are closely connected: AI shakes the foundations of anthropocentrism and blurs the boundary between humans and “animals.” What, then, separates humans from animals? Pascal called man a thinking reed; Engels said that labor created humanity. Put another way, humans are living beings who can make artificial systems of their own, through both thought and labor. “Creativity” is the most distinctively human form of intelligence on Earth. Termites build “cities,” apes make “tools,” and wolves have their own “societies,” but these seemingly artificial systems lack creativity and remain static. The patterns behind them are already encoded in each species’ genes, resist change, and may ultimately vanish under natural selection. Only human cities, tools, and societies continue to develop and evolve autonomously—until the appearance of artificial intelligence.

Keiki Haniyasushin’s profile gives her “the ability to create idols.” This is the kind of human intelligence that artificial intelligence imitates: the creative “spirit” behind humanity’s claim to stand at the summit of creation. Humans are physically frail beside wolves, tigers, and leopards, but this creative capacity allowed them to conquer other species and become masters of the earth.

Artificial intelligence is itself a human-made system with some capacity for self-organization and creativity. The idea of Kizuna AI, the cheerfully fallible “Ai-chan,” replacing humanity was comic in 2019. The possibility of strong AI overturning human supremacy was not; science fiction had rehearsed that thought experiment for decades. If humans lost their throne, would they willingly kneel before a superior intelligence and become its servants? Of course not. Anthropocentrism therefore contains a trap. If greater power gives humans the right to conquer animals, then a still greater intelligence may conquer humans by the same rule. If we may trample animal dignity, on what grounds can we object when a machine tramples ours?

*Wily Beast and Weakest Creature* builds a funhouse mirror that reverses the present hierarchy. Adapting the Buddhist scheme of the ten realms, ZUN places the action in an Animal Realm on the outskirts of Hell. Its real crisis is not the incident that sends animal spirits into Gensokyo, but a machine revolt back home. Because they possessed every natural advantage, the animal spirits had dominated the nearly defenseless human spirits and confined them in the Primate Spirit Garden as playthings. Confinement, however, made the humans organize. They founded a religion and summoned Keiki, a creator god of idols. She protected them, fashioned an army of clay figures “with neither flesh nor soul,” expelled their animal masters, and became the new ruler of the Animal Realm.

The triangle—animal, human, artificial intelligence—is now in plain view. The Animal Realm’s proximity to Buddhist Hell is misdirection; ZUN has simply inverted the arrangement of the real world. Animal spirits occupy the human position, and humans the animal one. The Primate Spirit Garden clearly points beyond the zoo, toward every space where outsiders and foreign peoples are exhibited, degraded, confined, or killed, from Roman arenas to Nazi concentration camps.

The idols are haniwa, terracotta figures placed around burial mounds in ancient Japan. Made to accompany human beings into death, these artificial figures acquire new life as Keiki’s soldiers. ZUN’s inspired joke is material as well as mythological: clay figurines and computer chips both depend on compounds of silicon. A single element runs from the ritual technologies of antiquity to the machine intelligence of the present.

[fig] The Beast Metropolis appears as a corridor of screens and circuitry. ZUN, too, could not resist the games industry’s well-worn cyberpunk formula.

![Gameplay from Wily Beast and Weakest Creature set in the neon corridors of the Beast Metropolis](attachments/roof-archive/cv8622821/17-wily-beast-gameplay.jpg "=75%")

“The strong devour the weak” recurs throughout *Wily Beast*. In ZUN’s in-game Music Room commentary on the Stage 5 theme “Beast Metropolis,” he writes in part:

> “The Animal Realm is a place where survival of the fittest is the one and only law. Economic success reigns supreme, and individual thought is not respected. Personal feelings are nothing but an obstruction in such a rationalized society.”

This also lays bare the operating logic of Nazi Germany and imperial Japan, two terrifying anthropological machines for manufacturing the nonhuman. The domination—and even extermination—of “inferior peoples” by “superior peoples” could be sold as economically efficient and rational for humanity as a whole. The victory of the imperial state was all that mattered, and the lives of ordinary people at the bottom were expendable.

The law of predation is a cage that anthropocentrism closes around humanity itself. It sounds hardheaded, but it always measures only part of what matters, and no one holds the throne forever. AI has already made human intelligence look less singular. *Kemono Friends* likewise teaches that no single ability can solve every problem. Pursue strength alone while trampling whoever appears weak, and the result is an endless circuit of master and slave, predator and prey.

That circuit is the hell *Wily Beast* depicts. The animal spirits cage the humans in the Primate Spirit Garden, then find themselves expelled by the runaway machine army that the Garden has produced. They suffer the consequence of their own law. In the mirror, their faces are ours.

## Who Gets to Answer?

In philosophy, the animal is humanity’s absolute other: another living being radically unlike us. To think seriously about that other is also to ask how we meet people unlike ourselves—people with other cultures, convictions, and ways of life—and whether such relationships can be peaceful and mutually sustaining. The ordinary name for that hope is friendship. Yet friendship between humans, no less than friendship across species, is both a beautiful ideal and perhaps an illusion. Neither the God Warrior’s violent judgment nor Keiki’s promise of friendship across difference provides an answer. Even a god made by human beings—artificial intelligence—cannot settle the matter for us.

*Yurikuma Arashi* moves like a fairy tale and ultimately attributes friendship to divine grace: Kumaria allows the girls to cross the boundary. The welcoming society of Japari Park rests on human extinction, an irony too large to miss. Nor do we know what would happen if the Ceruleans, the mysterious safety valve regulating its ecology, disappeared. Would the park’s politics remain so gentle if those carefully bounded conditions failed?

*Wily Beast* begins in war and ends in war. Again and again, Heisei animal stories either promise an unattainable paradise of friendship or leave bewildering wreckage for the audience to sort through. Only Miyazaki lets his winged savior demolish the entire architecture of power, as if the ardor of his youth had never cooled. When student protest confronted him in real life, however, Miyazaki turned tail and slipped away, while ZUN, in his usual fashion, leaves his unfinished story to the players.

It would be too easy, however, to accuse these creators of cowardice for “entrusting the world to idols”: asking a god to answer, a hero to lead, or an artificial mind to relieve us of judgment. The animal question really is difficult. God does not know the answer, and neither does AI. Perhaps we can learn it only slowly, through the gaze we exchange with animals—and through the changes that gaze demands of us.

In the West, dragons are often associated with wealth through their hoards; in China, the dragon became the supreme animal emblem of power in feudal society. The Animal Realm is a funhouse mirror for human political intrigue, so placing the Chinese dragon and its kin in *Wily Beast*’s political struggle feels only natural.
