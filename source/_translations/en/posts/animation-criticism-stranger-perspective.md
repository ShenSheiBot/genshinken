(This essay grew out of a passing thought in a group chat. What follows is a tentative, personal account of anime criticism: a few ideas, and a statement of where I stand.)

## The Quarrel Behind This Essay

The dispute that prompted this essay centered on prominent anime writers on Zhihu, a Chinese question-and-answer platform with a large community of anime writers and readers. They were attacked for citing theory and writing in a style their detractors found deliberately obscure. The insult of choice was “anime Brahmin”: a self-appointed elite dressing up ordinary opinions in borrowed intellectual finery. Some of the attacks were nonsense, some people had simply come to enjoy the fight, and a few critics took the underlying questions seriously.

Those questions matter more than the personalities involved. Does theory illuminate anime, or merely allow critics to pull rank on other fans? Can anyone claim authority in a field built out of personal responses? And if there is no single correct way to interpret a work, can we still say that one argument is better than another?

For all the disagreement, one point seems clear: there will never be a rulebook telling us how anime criticism must be done. Criticism brings a particular viewer—with their memories, education, desires, and blind spots—into contact with a work that other people can encounter differently. Interpretation is therefore situated, not final.

That resistance to final authority has a particular history in Chinese anime, comics, and games culture, commonly abbreviated as ACG and often called the “two-dimensional,” or *erciyuan*, world. The culture grew from fan communities rather than from established cultural institutions. It inherited an upstart spirit close to the Japanese idea of *gekokujō*: those lower in a hierarchy refusing to defer to those above. Suspicion of official expertise followed naturally. But when professional language and theory came to look like badges of rank, suspicion of experts sometimes widened into hostility toward theory itself. Some critics began treating immediate feeling as the only honest basis for writing; at the extreme, even asking what social or cultural forces shaped a work could be dismissed as pretension.

![Two figures overlap beneath a wash of blue, suggesting different perspectives on the same image](attachments/roof-archive/cv1652116/02-opening-illustration.jpg)

Most mockery of anime criticism turns on this pair of anxieties: theory may crowd out the pleasure of the work, and theoretical fluency may be used to claim authority over other fans. Neither anxiety is foolish. But when they harden into a refusal to think beyond first impressions, criticism loses both range and depth.

## I. Why Theory Feels Like a Claim to Rank

I do not intend to offer a complete sociology of anti-theoretical feeling. Two conditions, however, help explain it.

First, ACG culture began as a subculture and at times acted like a counterculture: it gave people tastes, jokes, relationships, and values that set them against the mainstream. The culture industry eventually learned how to sell that difference back to them. What had been a shared practice of making and defending an alternative culture could now be expressed by buying the right commodities. This did not erase the culture or make its pleasures false, but it weakened the sense that fandom might change anything beyond one’s private life.

Second, contemporary consumer culture encourages us to organize life around personal preference while distrusting large explanations of how society works. Fans whose interests are quietly belittled in school, at work, or at home have an additional reason to retreat into private enjoyment. Watching what one likes can become a way to protect a small zone of autonomy. But that refuge can also become passive: everyday experience displaces ideology, and criticism stops reaching for breadth or depth. From there, theory may sound less like an invitation to understand that enjoyment and more like another outsider telling fans what their pleasure really means.

This is not a condemnation of otaku, nor is the suspicion unique to ACG culture. Theory is often difficult, sometimes tedious, and easily abused. Anyone who invokes it risks sounding as though they value a fashionable vocabulary more than the work in front of them. The real conflict is therefore not “clever theorists versus ignorant fans.” It is a conflict between two legitimate needs: the wish to preserve the authority of lived experience, and the wish to place that experience within a larger cultural account. Turning those needs into warring camps teaches us very little.

Criticism begins in personal experience, but publishing criticism makes that experience public. Finishing an essay gives the writer a reason to believe in the labor they have done; before the essay influences anyone else, it has at least persuaded its author. Each essay therefore establishes a relation between the critic’s experience and the work, and a local center of discourse around that relation. The moment a critic names a pattern, defines a term, or persuades a reader to notice something, they exercise a modest form of discursive power: they influence what can be said about a work and which questions seem worth asking. If the essay travels, its vocabulary and assumptions may travel with it.

![An anime character declares, “This is realism”—a comic reminder that naming a mode also frames what counts as real](attachments/roof-archive/cv1652116/translations/en/03-anime-dialogue-still-en-v2.png "=66%")

This is the useful point behind talk of “discursive authority.” It does not mean that a critic occupies an official throne or gets the last word. Power, in the Foucauldian sense relevant here, circulates through ordinary acts of description and persuasion. We are all spoken about by others, and we all speak in ways that position other people. A popular essay may establish a critical vocabulary; a mocking label such as “anime Brahmin” may make certain styles of writing embarrassing before anyone has considered their claims. Both interventions alter the conversation.

No critic can therefore claim sovereignty over the method of anime criticism. Status alone does not make an interpretation deeper or better. But a plurality of interpretations does not mean that every argument is equally good. We can still ask whether a critic has described the work accurately, supported their claims, considered plausible objections, and shown us something the evidence can bear. What remains open is not the possibility of judgment, but the identity of the final judge. There is no fixed seat of authority—only arguments gaining, losing, and regaining influence as people answer one another.

## II. Breadth, Depth, and the View from the Edge

### Breadth: What a Partial Outsider Can See

![Illustrated portrait of Georg Simmel, whose “stranger” belongs to a group while retaining some distance from it](attachments/roof-archive/cv1652116/04-georg-simmel-portrait.jpg "=50%")

I am not a professional critic, and I would not pretend to settle the methods of literary criticism. What I can do is bring my own experience and specialist knowledge to a work, ask what they reveal, and let that encounter alter my understanding of both. This is one way to make criticism more varied.

The sociologist Georg Simmel gives us a useful figure for this purpose: **the stranger**. Simmel’s stranger is neither a tourist looking in from outside nor a native who feels entirely at home. The stranger belongs to the group but remains distant enough to notice assumptions that full insiders take for granted. Two points matter here:

1. The stranger is part of the group. “Stranger” describes a position within a community, not exclusion from it.
2. Someone may enter that position from outside or grow into it from within. What matters is the combination of attachment and distance: the stranger puts down roots but never sees the group as wholly natural or self-evident.

Every critic brings a different mixture of memory, social position, culture, and knowledge. Those traits have been shaped by the social worlds they move through, including their encounters across cultures. When that history meets the conventions of a community, it creates a particular angle of vision. Someone who belongs partly elsewhere may spot a formal pattern, social assumption, or source of pleasure that longtime insiders no longer notice. The stranger’s contribution is not automatic superiority. It is the chance to ask a question that the group’s usual categories have left unasked.

Otaku occupy a similar position in relation to mainstream culture. They live within the wider society and depend on its institutions, yet they maintain enough distance to build their own references and standards. Some of their in-group expressions emerge from friction: mainstream suspicion and distortion provoke replies that harden into shorthand whose meaning depends on shared context. Consider *zhe♂xue*—“philo♂sophy,” with a male symbol inserted into ordinary *zhexue*, the Chinese word for philosophy. It is the Chinese name for the sprawling *gachimuchi* remix meme built around footage of muscular men. The stylization turns exaggerated masculinity and homoerotic imagery into an instantly recognizable wink among participants. To an outsider it is baffling; within the group it can summon an entire history of clips, jokes, and remixes in a few characters.

Such language shows how a marginal culture makes meaning on its own terms. It also brings us back to the Zhihu dispute. People who denounce “anime Brahmins” for imposing an orthodoxy may be defending the community’s irreverence. Yet the moment they decree which forms of criticism count as authentically fannish, they begin constructing an orthodoxy of their own. Who, then, is playing the Brahmin?

![Hiro and Zero Two in *DARLING in the FRANXX*, a series whose romance and world-saving plot invite sharply different critical emphases](attachments/roof-archive/cv1652116/05-anime-character-relations.jpg)

I am not arguing that anime criticism should have no standards. I am arguing that ACG culture has boundaries too unstable to be secured by a single critical orthodoxy. A critic interested in the emotional dependence between Hiro and Zero Two will read *DARLING in the FRANXX* differently from someone using a *sekaikei* framework—a way of reading stories in which an intimate bond between young protagonists is tied directly to the fate of the world, with ordinary social institutions pushed to the side. Those approaches ask different questions. Either may produce a perceptive or a careless essay; their difference alone does not decide the matter.

Absolute neutrality is neither possible nor desirable. Criticism should be intelligible, and it should answer to the work, but a critic’s sensory and emotional experience matters more than obedience to a standardized procedure. That experience cannot be separated from personal history. Nor can writers control how readers will respond. An essay can invite reflection, but it cannot dictate what kind of reflection, how much, or to what end.

![Four characters gathered over tea: one scene may support readings of friendship, performance, genre convention, or consumer fantasy](attachments/roof-archive/cv1652116/06-anime-tea-scene.jpg)

Anime criticism therefore needs breadth. No critic can account for everything. Every interpretation leaves features unnoticed and questions unasked, which creates room for the next writer. An unusual angle deserves a hearing when it makes a clear claim, attends to evidence, and can answer reasonable objections. It should not be rejected merely because it comes from outside the community’s familiar repertoire. We expand our understanding of a work—and of the culture around it—by testing such arguments rather than policing them out of existence.

### Depth: My Case for Structural Thinking

Here is a small hobbyhorse of mine. Otaku often push back when cultural studies or critical theory explains their pleasures from above, and some theoretical accounts deserve the resistance. Two failures recur.

The first is a failure of empathy. An outsider identifies otaku as alienated from the mainstream and assumes that alienation can only mean oppression, misery, and a desire to be rescued. Social work sometimes uses an oppression model to examine how institutions constrain a marginalized group. That can be valuable. Applied without listening to the people concerned, however, it becomes paternalism: the analyst decides in advance what is wrong with otaku life and what its inhabitants ought to want. Are their lives really so wretched? How do otaku themselves understand ACG culture? Those questions must come before the rescue plan.

The second failure is to turn one powerful cause into the only cause. Markets plainly shape ACG culture: they determine which projects receive funding, what gets distributed, and which desires can be sold. But “capital did it” cannot explain every formal choice, audience response, or act of creation. Creators and viewers still make decisions within those conditions. A commercial origin does not make a beloved work worthless, any more than affection for that work makes its industrial setting irrelevant.

We know that loving anime and its fictional worlds is not the same as confusing fiction with reality, whatever a certain advertisement for *Mr Love: Queen’s Choice* may have insinuated when it mocked players for treating virtual boyfriends as real ones. Yet I used to be troubled by two questions: Why can’t the values, relationships, and intensities I love in fiction take firmer shape in ordinary life? Why can’t the works that move me matter more in the wider world?

Many fans know this tension. We cherish fictional worlds partly because they give form to desires that reality frustrates, and we resist crude demands that fantasy justify itself by ordinary standards. At the same time, we want people beyond the fan community to recognize that these works—and our attachments to them—have value. We protect the distance between fiction and everyday life while hoping that each might transform the other. This is the contradiction I have in mind: subculture offers an alternative to the mainstream, but it also wants the mainstream to hear it. As Simmel’s account of cultural modernity suggests, culture matters partly because it gives form to possibilities that everyday life has not yet realized.

The culture industry intensifies that contradiction by making ACG at once more visible and more commercial. The older boundary around a relatively enclosed fan community no longer holds. Mainstream platforms, advertisers, consumers, and professional production now occupy the same spaces as the practices fans once treated as their own. That does not mean the ACG community has literally vanished. It means that membership and authenticity can no longer be secured by a clean inside/outside line.

One response is to retreat further into hardcore fandom. Fans measure authenticity by how many titles they have seen, which games they play, how obscure their favorites are, or how difficult those works are for newcomers to understand. Another response is concealment. In offline life, people may downplay their ACG interests because they expect ridicule, then hesitate to call themselves otaku at all. “I’m just someone who watches cartoons,” they grumble. The sentence both claims the pleasure and disowns the identity. It lets a person move between fan and non-fan according to the demands of the moment, without resolving the gap between the richer life imagined in fiction and the compromises of everyday life.

![A lone performer framed by converging blue lights: staging, character, production, and cultural context can all belong to the same reading](attachments/roof-archive/cv1652116/07-blue-stage-scene.png)

In the quarrel that prompted this essay, one slogan claimed that bad shows and undiscriminating audiences create each other. The way out is not to order audiences to acquire better taste. Nor is it to demand that producers make whatever a particular critic considers excellent. Anime criticism can instead cultivate what I call **structural thinking**: a way of seeing how a work’s parts act on one another, how those relations change over the course of the work, how the work resembles and departs from other works, and how production, circulation, and reception shape what appears on screen.

These levels belong together because neither the work nor its audience exists in isolation. A character relationship may organize a plot; the plot may draw on a genre convention; that convention may serve a market category; and fans may adopt or resist the category in building an identity. Moving along that chain does not replace close attention with sociology. It makes reading reflexive: we examine the object we love while also examining the position from which we love it.

Borrowing from Stuart Hall’s account of how subcultures learn to see themselves in relation to the mainstream, I call this awareness **otaku self-awareness**. It means recognizing both the particular pleasures of otaku culture and the social structures in which those pleasures take shape. It asks what ACG shares with other subcultures, what makes it distinctive, and how its tense proximity to the mainstream affects both works and fans. This is not a portrait of a single ideal anime critic. It is simply a critical possibility I find worth pursuing. Whether anyone takes it up is their choice. I admire conscientious critics of many kinds, from meticulous readers of minute formal details to theorists who bring anime into conversation with foundational works of criticism.

I have sketched two diagrams to make this plurality easier to see. They are maps of possible approaches, not rankings, prescriptions, or complete taxonomies; the four quadrants in the second are provisional ideal types, not limits on what criticism can do.

![Schematic of critical breadth and depth: sample readings occupy overlapping areas, while the unmarked space represents questions still open to criticism](attachments/roof-archive/cv1652116/translations/en/08-breadth-analysis-diagram-v3.png)

In the first diagram, breadth increases from left to right and depth from top to bottom. The four colored ellipses—“Reading A” through “Reading D”—represent sample pieces of criticism, each covering a different combination of range and concentration. Their overlap shows that approaches can share material; the blank areas are simply topics or methods that these examples have not covered. The “broad and deep” label is playful, not a claim that one form is objectively best.

The dashed “Kyon line,” named for the famously skeptical narrator of *The Melancholy of Haruhi Suzumiya*, marks a comic warning threshold: go far enough into interpretation and some reader will accuse you of overreading. The very narrow “Reading D” is the eccentric specialist reading; its catlike “Meow, meow… meow?” and the note about desire in erotic manga carry the joke to an extreme. The point is not that criticism must stay above the line, but that depth and plausibility are separate questions. A deep reading still has to show its evidence.

![Two-axis map of anime criticism: work and content lie to the left, culture and form to the right; theory and structure lie above, experience and history below](attachments/roof-archive/cv1652116/translations/en/09-criticism-dimensions-diagram-v2.png)

The second diagram has two axes and four quadrants, not four dimensions. Its horizontal axis runs from attention to the work and its content on the left to attention to cultural and formal context on the right. Its vertical axis runs from theory and structure at the top to experience and history at the bottom.

The upper-left quadrant includes literary and art theory, literary criticism, and close analysis of particular ACG works; its “unreliable narrator” example shows how theory can clarify the relation between a character’s words and an author’s position. The upper-right contains cultural studies, cultural sociology, and industry analysis; the *Ghost in the Shell* example treats the series’ cybernetic society as a way of thinking about political paralysis and ordinary life after confidence in collective ideologies has faded. The lower-left collects reviews, personal essays, and compilations of *neta*—references, memes, and inside jokes. Its example takes Hachiman Hikigaya’s rejection of the idealized “springtime of youth” and asks how he nevertheless cobbles together a version of it, one that still leaves room for growth. The lower-right turns to cultural history, creative process, and cast or staff profiles. Its Jun Maeda anecdote treats biographical criticism as near-gossip: his brush with death after heart trouble evokes the tragedy of *Angel Beats!*, while a failed flirtation with a nurse becomes another possible source of creative renewal. That looseness reminds us that locating an approach on the map says nothing by itself about the quality of its evidence.

A critic or a single essay may move among all four quadrants, remain productively within one, or work outside this scheme altogether. The chart describes differences of emphasis; it does not set out four skills that every accomplished critic must master.

Anime criticism needs more curiosity and less condemnation. I hope we can use theory without losing sight of experience, attend to the culture around a work without neglecting the work itself, and remain conscious of what an otaku identity means while we inhabit it.

These are thoughts prompted by one quarrel, not a final program. Thank you for reading; corrections are welcome.
