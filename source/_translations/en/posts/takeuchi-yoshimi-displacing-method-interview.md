## I. Deprovincializing Takeuchi Yoshimi

**Fu Shi:** Thank you very much for taking time out of your busy schedule for this interview. I briefly introduced the project to you earlier: we have selected essays by a number of scholars working outside Chinese and Japanese academia to show how you read Takeuchi Yoshimi, particularly the China of his writings. The contributors include Naoki Sakai, Richard F. Calichman, Jon D. Solomon, Christian Uhl, and yourself. We have called the project “Displacing Takeuchi Yoshimi.” Originally, it was called “Takeuchi Yoshimi in the West.” I changed the name because everyone featured in it maintains a highly critical awareness of the so-called “West” and the disciplinary norms founded on that concept. In a sense, choosing Takeuchi as a subject of research already expresses a critical stance toward disciplinary traditions claiming universality. But we also have to confront the fact that there is something heterogeneous in your work. Much of this unfamiliarity comes from your thinking about Takeuchi “between” scholarly traditions—from displacing him through cross-cultural and transnational inquiry, translation, and postcolonial thought. My first question is: how did you arrive at these displacements? What led you to choose this kind of Takeuchi?

[person] Richard F. Calichman

![A white-haired man speaking at a lectern, with “The University of Chicago” visible behind him](attachments/wechat/dxhLMHKR4T14AhOvsrj3zA/002.jpg "=25%")

[person-bio] Professor at the City University of New York and Senior Research Scholar at Columbia University’s Weatherhead East Asian Institute. He received his doctorate from Cornell University, where he studied with Naoki Sakai. His wide-ranging research encompasses modern Japanese intellectual history, modern Japanese literature, literary criticism, postcolonial theory, film studies, and the philosophy of time. His books include *Before Identity: The Question of Method in Japan Studies* (2021), *Beyond Nation: Time, Writing, and Community in the Work of Abe Kōbō* (2016), *The Coming Death: Traces of Mortality Across East Asia* (2022), and *Takeuchi Yoshimi: Displacing the West* (2004). He has also translated a number of works by Abe Kōbō and Takeuchi Yoshimi.

[person] Jon D. Solomon

![Portrait of a bald man wearing round glasses and a red scarf](attachments/wechat/dxhLMHKR4T14AhOvsrj3zA/003.jpg "=25%")

[person-bio] A graduate of Brown University in 1984, Solomon received his doctorate in modern Chinese literature from Cornell University in 1997. He is a tenured professor in the Department of Chinese at Jean Moulin Lyon 3 University in France and a researcher at the interdisciplinary multilingual research center at Paris Nanterre University. His research interests include sovereignty and biopolitics, nationalism and globalization, postcolonialism, racism, and modern Chinese literature.

**Viren Murthy:** The main issue is the relationship between different disciplines and Takeuchi. I think that is the central question. Displacement may have something to do with these disciplinary differences. In the West, particularly in the United States, I think Calichman may have been the first to study Takeuchi systematically. What makes his work interesting to me is that, within East Asian studies, he belongs to the literary studies side, but what he actually studies is philosophy—continental philosophy, especially Derrida. So his Takeuchi has a strong Derridean coloring, which also has something to do with his teacher, Naoki Sakai.

[person] Christian Uhl

![Portrait of a man wearing glasses in front of a blackboard](attachments/wechat/dxhLMHKR4T14AhOvsrj3zA/004.jpg "=25%")

[person-bio] Uhl received his doctorate, with the highest honors, from Heidelberg University; his dissertation won the JaDe Prize in 2003. He was appointed Professor of Japanese Studies at Ghent University in 2007 and is currently a full professor there. At Frankfurt, Heidelberg, Leiden, and Ghent, he has taught a wide range of courses in philosophy, intellectual history, the history of political ideas, Japanese literary history, general Chinese and Japanese history, research methodology, theory, modern and classical Japanese, classical Chinese, and *kanbun kundoku*—the Japanese reading of Literary Chinese—as well as numerous more specialized courses and seminars. Working from a historical-materialist perspective, he examines questions in Chinese and Japanese philosophy and intellectual history. His research focuses on epochal transformations in systems of knowledge, their political-economic foundations, and the specific historical conditions that make such transformations possible. On this basis, he also addresses broader theoretical questions beyond the conventional boundaries of East Asian studies.

Uhl, too, can be placed within East Asian studies, but he did his doctorate at Heidelberg. His dissertation was also about Takeuchi’s Lu Xun, and his teacher, Wolfgang Seifert, studies Takeuchi as well. Their tradition includes some philosophy, but it is more philological. Uhl is mainly concerned with where Takeuchi’s intellectual resources came from, which is why he focuses on the Kyoto School.

[person] Wolfgang Seifert

![Portrait of an older man wearing glasses](attachments/wechat/dxhLMHKR4T14AhOvsrj3zA/005.jpg "=25%")

[person-bio] After receiving his doctorate in Frankfurt in 1975, Seifert became Professor of Japanese Studies at Heidelberg University. His research focuses on modern Japanese politics, intellectual and political history, philosophy, and sociology. His books include *Nationalismus im Nachkriegs-Japan. Ein Beitrag zur Ideologie der völkischen Nationalisten* [Nationalism in Postwar Japan: A Contribution to the Study of the Ideology of Ethnic Nationalists].

If we turn to China, we have to mention Sun Ge. She is an important scholar who also originally came from a Chinese department. But her approach differs from both of theirs. She tends to approach Takeuchi through intellectual history, placing his thought in its historical context. She and Calichman have some things in common: both are interested in postmodern theory. But Sun Ge develops her account from a historical context.

[fig] *The Political Philosophy of Zhang Taiyan: The Resistance of Consciousness*

![Book cover featuring a silhouetted figure and Chinese lettering](attachments/wechat/dxhLMHKR4T14AhOvsrj3zA/006.jpg "=25%")

[fig-note] Viren Murthy; Chinese translation by Zhang Chuntian, Ren Zhijun, and others. East China Normal University Press, 2018.

My background is a little different from theirs, so my point of departure is different too. I began by studying comparative philosophy at the University of Hawaiʻi, mainly Chinese and Western philosophy, though I also encountered some Kyoto School thought. Only later did I enter a history department. In fact, when I was in philosophy, I did not even know who Takeuchi was. I learned about him while researching Zhang Taiyan in Japan. I was particularly impressed by Japanese scholarship on Zhang, and I began wondering why these scholars understood him in this way. Where did this anti-modern reading come from? Gradually, that question led me back to Takeuchi. I think these scholars were all influenced by him, including Nishi Junzō. Of course, Nishi may have belonged to the same generation as Takeuchi, but Nishi’s student Kondō Kuniyasu told me that he had indeed been influenced by Takeuchi. Then this idea of an anti-modern Zhang Taiyan emerged.

[fig] Faculty members of Hitotsubashi University’s Faculty of Social Sciences

![Black-and-white group photograph of faculty members at Hitotsubashi University’s Faculty of Social Sciences](attachments/wechat/dxhLMHKR4T14AhOvsrj3zA/007.jpg "=66%")

Nishi Junzō (1914–1984), a historian of Chinese thought, began working as an assistant at the Ministry of Education’s Research Institute for National Spiritual Culture in 1938. In 1942, he was appointed associate professor in the Faculty of Letters at Keijō Imperial University. In 1944, while in Keijō, he was enlisted as a private second class in the 106th Infantry Regiment of the Imperial Japanese Army’s 49th Division, serving for three months. His appointment ended in 1946 when Keijō Imperial University was abolished following the end of the Second World War. He returned to Japan and that year became a professor in the preparatory division of Tokyo University of Industry, now Hitotsubashi University. He was promoted to professor in the Faculty of Social Sciences in 1958 and became its sixth dean in 1966. One of the pioneers of modern Chinese intellectual history in the postwar Shōwa period, Nishi was known for single-handedly compiling *Genten Chūgoku kindai shisōshi* [Modern Chinese Intellectual History in Primary Sources], originally published in six volumes by Iwanami Shoten. He also took an interest in social issues such as discrimination against Asians living in Japan and actively participated in citizens’ anti-discrimination movements. His works include *満州国の宗教問題* [Religious Problems in Manchukuo], *中国思想論集* [Essays on Chinese Thought], and *日本と朝鮮の間：京城生活の断片、その他* [Between Japan and Korea: Fragments of Life in Keijō and Other Writings].

[person] Kondō Kuniyasu (1934–2023)

![Frontal portrait of a white-haired elderly man](attachments/wechat/dxhLMHKR4T14AhOvsrj3zA/008.jpg "=25%")

[person-bio] Kondō served as associate professor at Hokkaido University and professor at the University of Tokyo’s Institute of Social Science. On reaching retirement age in 1995, he became professor emeritus at the University of Tokyo. He subsequently taught in the Department of Political Science, Faculty of Law, at Daitō Bunka University, leaving in 2006. He served as president of the Japan Association for Modern China Studies and as a board member of the Society for Studies of Chinese Society and Culture. His principal fields were modern Chinese intellectual history, Chinese politics, and Mao Zedong Thought. His books include *毛沢東：実践と思想* [Mao Zedong: Practice and Thought], *中国近代思想史研究* [Studies in Modern Chinese Intellectual History], and *辛亥革命：思想の形成* [The 1911 Revolution: The Formation of Its Thought].

So I think that behind this anti-modern Zhang Taiyan stands an anti-modern Lu Xun, and that anti-modern Lu Xun is connected to Takeuchi. That was how I became interested in him. But my background differs somewhat from these scholars’. I started in philosophy and later moved into history, and it was while studying philosophy that I became interested in Marxism. My work on Takeuchi consequently has a stronger Marxist coloring, particularly compared with Calichman’s and Sun Ge’s. My particular interest in Marxist studies and Hegel also gives it a more Hegelian-Marxist orientation, which leads me toward somewhat different texts and ideas.

That may be the key issue. To give an example, in *Pan-Asianism and the Legacy of the Chinese Revolution*, I raise a question about time: Takeuchi’s 無限の瞬間, “the infinite instant.” I think the instant itself is infinite here, so there are possible connections with Heidegger and others. This is completely different from Calichman’s translation. He translates the expression as “innumerable instances,”[^calichman] and the Chinese translation likewise has “countless instants.” But “countless” means that each instant is the same, and there are countless such instants. My point is that the instant itself is infinite: a revolutionary instant—or a political instant, an instant of action. (Note in the Chinese edition: “History is not an empty form of time. Without countless instants of life-and-death struggle to establish the self, not only the self but history too would be lost.” Takeuchi Yoshimi, *Overcoming Modernity*, Chinese translation by Sun Ge.)

[fig] *Pan-Asianism and the Legacy of the Chinese Revolution*

![Cover of Viren Murthy’s Pan-Asianism and the Legacy of the Chinese Revolution](attachments/wechat/dxhLMHKR4T14AhOvsrj3zA/009.jpg "=25%")

[fig-note] Viren Murthy, University of Chicago Press, 2023.

That is one example. So I think background and discipline are very important. But what is particularly interesting about Takeuchi is that no discipline can fully grasp him. You need an interdisciplinary perspective to study him.

**Fu:** So Takeuchi is himself essentially in this displaced condition, as he has always shown.

**Murthy:** Yes, I think that is how he saw himself. You could say he worked in literature or literary criticism, but he also had a relationship to philosophy. He discusses things like “time,” yet he constantly reminds us that he is not doing philosophy. So he is always in this in-between position, a little like what Lu Xun called a “historical intermediate.” Sometimes he really is like that.

**Fu:** You have just discussed the differences between yourself and these scholars, particularly Calichman. I think he certainly deserves close attention, not only as a pioneering Takeuchi scholar in the West, but also as Takeuchi’s translator—as Uhl is, of course. You mentioned the connection between Calichman’s and Sakai’s philosophical approaches. As Sakai’s student, Calichman does seem to have pushed Sakai’s positions to an extreme of theorization. From the Sakai essay selected for this project to “‘You Asians’: On the Historical Role of the West and Asia Binary,” Sakai emphasizes that, although Takeuchi gives us an opening for postcolonial reflection and resistance to Eurocentrism, he shares the logic of Hegelian historicism with Maruyama Masao—though perhaps Takeuchi is closer to Nishida Kitarō. Takeuchi can criticize modernism precisely because he is himself a profound modernist, an adherent of modernity. Sakai emphasizes “starting from Takeuchi,” whereas Calichman has completely Derrideanized him.

[person] Naoki Sakai (1946–)

![A man holding a microphone and speaking in front of bookshelves](attachments/wechat/dxhLMHKR4T14AhOvsrj3zA/010.jpg "=25%")

[person-bio] Sakai taught in the Departments of Comparative Literature and Asian Studies at Cornell University and was also a member of its graduate field of History. He has published in multiple languages on comparative literature, intellectual history, translation studies, racism and nationalism, and the histories of semiotic systems and diverse literary forms, including speech, writing, bodily expression, calligraphic regimes, and phonographic traditions. He led the founding of *Traces*, a multilingual scholarly series published in Korean, Chinese, English, and Japanese, with German, Italian, and Spanish added in 2008. Its editorial office was based at Cornell, and he served as its founding senior editor from 1996 to 2004. In addition to *Traces*, Sakai has served on the editorial boards of *positions: east asia cultures critique* (United States), *Postcolonial Studies* (Australia), *Tamkang Review* (Taiwan, China), the *International Dictionary of Intellectual History* (United Kingdom and Germany), *History of Modern Japanese Culture* (Japan), *Perspectives* (Korea), and *Multitudes* (France). His internationally acclaimed books include *Voices of the Past: The Status of Language in Eighteenth-Century Japanese Discourse* (『過去の声 18世紀日本の言説における言語の地位』), *死産される日本語・日本人：「日本」の歴史－地政的配置* [Stillborn Japanese Language and Japanese People: The Historical-Geopolitical Configuration of “Japan”], and *Translation and Subjectivity: On Japan and Cultural Nationalism* (『日本思想という問題 翻訳と主体』).

[fig] *Stillborn Japanese Language and Japanese People: The Historical-Geopolitical Configuration of “Japan”* (『死産される日本語・日本人：「日本」の歴史—地政的配置』)

![Cover of Naoki Sakai’s Stillborn Japanese Language and Japanese People](attachments/wechat/dxhLMHKR4T14AhOvsrj3zA/011.jpg "=25%")

[fig-note] Naoki Sakai, Kōdansha, 2015.

What stands out here is the reflection on theory, especially within historical research. Calichman’s work may be excessively theoretical, but he takes a resistant stance toward precisely this objection. At the end of *Before Identity: The Question of Method in Japan Studies*, he recalls a job interview shortly after completing his dissertation, when a professor of Asian studies challenged him: “Don’t you think all these assorted theoretical claims only get in the way of our reading the original texts?” His response in the book is that theoretical work helps us rethink those original texts. For him, there is no unmediated text, and ignoring this is precisely the arrogance of Japanese studies in the West. This resistant stance also appears in his translations of Takeuchi. Besides the example you mentioned, he translates 観想の場 as “place of theoretical contemplation” and 行為の場 as “place of action.” Yet in his translation from more than a decade earlier, *What Is Modernity?: Writings of Takeuchi Yoshimi*, he used “site of contemplation,” without “theoretical.” In the essay selected for this project, he also states explicitly that Takeuchi’s opposition between the “place of contemplation” and the “place of action” is the opposition between theory and practice. I actually find this difficult to imagine in Japanese, even if 観照 was indeed originally Nishi Amane’s translation of *theōria*.

[fig] *Before Identity: The Question of Method in Japan Studies*

![Cover of Before Identity, with a woodland path in the background](attachments/wechat/dxhLMHKR4T14AhOvsrj3zA/012.jpg "=25%")

[fig-note] Richard F. Calichman, State University of New York Press, 2021.

[fig] *What Is Modernity?: Writings of Takeuchi Yoshimi*

![Cover of What Is Modernity?](attachments/wechat/dxhLMHKR4T14AhOvsrj3zA/013.jpg "=25%")

[fig-note] Richard F. Calichman, Columbia University Press, 2005.

Many Chinese-language scholars, especially historians, may find your language excessively philosophical and theoretical too. But as I mentioned earlier, this stance may express your reflection on theory itself. It may prompt you to consider the complicity between theory and the West, and between universality and particularity, and how that complicity works to construct a form of universal hegemony. Calichman develops his critique through Takeuchi and the relation between theory and practice. Takeuchi, too, is certainly “resisting,” as we can see from his criticism of traditional Sinology. What role do you think Takeuchi’s resistance plays in this context?

**Murthy:** How to think about theory is, of course, an enormous question. We have been discussing theory and philosophy, and this may be precisely where Calichman and I differ. He is also very fond of philosophy, but we could say that his presuppositions are basically Derridean. As you say, he criticizes a kind of positivism: the idea in American Japanese studies that the texts are simply there, without theory. On this point I basically agree with him. I also think theory is unavoidable. Just because I do not consciously use theory does not mean there is no theory. The point is that reading any text involves presuppositions. Without theory, those presuppositions are used uncritically.

The next step is more abstract and concerns the difference between Hegel and Derrida. Calichman’s next step differs from mine. Since there are always presuppositions, all we can do is acknowledge ours and then choose them. Calichman chooses Derrida’s. But this presupposition carries a further presupposition: that Derrida’s presuppositions are better than others. Here, I think, we encounter Hegel’s question: how can we demonstrate that one presupposition is better than another? The whole of Hegel’s *Logic* addresses this problem—how to clarify these presuppositions. It deals with mediation and self-mediation. Hegel argues that we must begin with the most basic category, *Sein*, or being. In Chinese this is sometimes translated as 有 or 在, and sometimes as 存在—though 存在 is closer to *Existenz*. The *Logic* begins with the simplest, most indeterminate category and derives the others from it. For example, it derives *Dasein*, determinate being, from being. I think this is what genuinely critical theory means.

What this position has to do with Takeuchi is a question. Takeuchi is not a philosopher in Hegel’s sense; he is more inclined to participate in history. But what categories do we use to discuss him? I think this question helps us consider how we view history. How we view Takeuchi’s history or Japanese history is connected to the larger question of how we view history as such, and thus to how we understand basic categories: being, purpose, life, and human life. This may be a very important difference between Calichman and me.

So, returning to Sakai: he thinks Takeuchi is actually too Hegelian. My view is exactly the opposite—Takeuchi is not Hegelian enough. He has neither a complete logic of history nor a basic logic. To return to 無限の瞬間, the “infinite instant”: Takeuchi sees it as an instant of action. But what is the purpose of that action? That is difficult to say. We can interpret it historically; we can say revolution, or liberation, and so on. But Takeuchi does not theorize these things. He only hints at them. You could also bring them together through his writings on Mao Zedong. I may have made Takeuchi more Hegelian and more revolutionary, whereas Calichman takes the same phenomena—though he reads their temporality differently—and makes him postmodern. Sakai also discusses Asia, but thinks Takeuchi is too Hegelian and too supportive of Asianism. He sees support for Asia and support for Eurocentrism as two sides of the same coin. Those are the differences among the three of us.

Calichman might then say that perhaps Takeuchi is not Derridean enough, but that he can make him Derridean. I do Hegelianize Takeuchi somewhat, because I think his implicit presuppositions may contain Hegelian ideas. Sakai might think this makes Takeuchi too teleological. But there is another question I must raise here: the question of “Asia.” I return to this throughout my book, and I think it is also the principal difference between me and the two of them. They both think that once you distinguish Asia from the West, you have already fallen into a kind of essentialism and bad identity politics. But I think that, in the context of imperialism, we cannot completely give up identification with the nation-state; we cannot even give up nationalism. In the book, I also discuss Takeuchi’s dialogue with Umesao Tadao, which I mentioned at Nankai the other day. “Asia” here is not simply a cultural or geographical concept. It is an anti-imperialist concept: wherever there is opposition to imperialism, that is Asia. So he says Cuba can be in Asia too. Seen this way, Takeuchi effectively sublates Asia, and Asia becomes the Third World. But Sakai and Calichman are not particularly interested in Third World movements or Third World Marxists. So they throw away this whole possibility and then proceed to criticize. Their conclusion is basically that Takeuchi is not postmodern enough, and that is where they stop.

**Fu:** You have identified many differences. But at Nankai you also mentioned drawing on Calichman and Uhl, as well as Chinese and Japanese scholars such as Sun Ge, Wang Hui, Nishi Junzō, and Mizoguchi Yūzō. Of course, this is much less evident in Calichman: whether discussing Takeuchi or Abe Kōbō, he seldom develops his argument by citing specific positions. What particularly interests me is Sun Ge’s and Mizoguchi’s engagement with Takeuchi’s China, which is also one of your concerns. Both criticized the fictive image of China in Takeuchi. Mizoguchi, for example, argued that Takeuchi’s “China” remained “Sinology without China,” still circling within Tsuda Sōkichi’s framework of knowledge. For Mizoguchi, Takeuchi’s affirmation of “China” was ahistorical; his understanding of it amounted to no more than “longing.” Sun Ge likewise writes that Takeuchi’s work “was strongly colored by commentary and excessively idealized China” (*Takeuchi Yoshimi to iu toi* [The Question of Takeuchi Yoshimi]). Such criticism is widespread. But it seems to me that Takeuchi was profoundly aware of this problem. I remember a passage in “What Is Modernity?”: “Only through encountering a heterogeneous object can the self establish itself. Europe’s longing for the Orient has existed since ancient times—or rather, Europe itself was originally a confused, amorphous existence—but this movement in the form of invasion appeared only with modernity.” I think that, for Takeuchi, such “longing” has existed “since ancient times,” but it is only with modernity that it becomes a problem. Or rather, the desire for an authentic object and a heterogeneous other is precisely an expression of the historical violence of modern capitalism.

[fig] *The Question of Takeuchi Yoshimi*, expanded edition, volume I (『増補 竹内好という問い 上』)

![Cover of the expanded edition of The Question of Takeuchi Yoshimi, volume I](attachments/wechat/dxhLMHKR4T14AhOvsrj3zA/014.jpg "=25%")

[fig-note] Sun Ge; Japanese translation by Suzuki Masahisa, Shimizu Ken’ichirō, and Satō Ken. Iwanami Shoten, 2026.

I think Takeuchi clearly recognized the inevitably fictive character of his “China.” This was a conviction he and his associates had proclaimed since founding the Chinese Literature Research Society. In his dispute with Yoshikawa Kōjirō over translation, Takeuchi said: “For me, it is I who make Chinese literature [*Shina bungaku*] Chinese literature. For Yoshikawa, the scholarly attitude consists in approaching Chinese literature ever more closely.”[^shina] “China” has meaning only in relation to one’s own life. For Takeuchi, the most urgent issue is first of all one’s own way of existing. How do you view these criticisms of him? Or, to put it another way, how do you view Takeuchi’s China?

**Murthy:** Yes, Mizoguchi’s and Sun Ge’s criticisms of Takeuchi raise a very important question, especially Mizoguchi’s “Sinology without China.” What he is saying is that China becomes a mirror: Japanese Sinologists are not looking at “China.” He also criticizes Nishi Junzō, saying that these Sinologists’ “China” is not the real China, but an imagined China—a China of resistance. We cannot say that this criticism is entirely wrong. I think Takeuchi grasps one aspect of the whole experience of the Chinese Revolution. As you just said, when Europe invaded and global capitalism entered China, China began to resist, and through this acquired subjectivity. Mizoguchi’s question is: what was China like before the invasion? Without understanding that, how can we explain why China reacted as it did when invaded? In particular, when it received foreign ideas, why did it interpret them in these ways? So I think we have to bring the two together. I do not see Mizoguchi and Takeuchi as completely opposed, and Mizoguchi himself would certainly not deny Takeuchi’s great influence on him.

At the end of “Asia as Method,” Takeuchi speaks of 巻き返す—a “counteroffensive.” Discussing a cultural counteroffensive, he says: “When [we] launch a counteroffensive, there must be something distinctive within [us]. If we ask what that something is, perhaps it is not a substance. But it may exist as method.” This seems a little empty to Mizoguchi. I find the passage interesting because it reminds me of Hegel’s discussion of substance and subject. Takeuchi is basically saying that what we need is “subject,” not “substance.”

But Hegel wants precisely to break down that opposition. He says that substance is not unchanging; it is constantly changing. From this perspective, we can understand Mizoguchi. What he calls Chinese culture or Chinese thought is itself constantly changing, and this change has a logic intimately connected to the revolutions of the twentieth century. His *The Vicissitudes and Development of Premodern Chinese Thought* describes an important transformation during the Ming and Qing. The formation of the subject is implicit in the thought of figures such as Huang Zongxi and Gu Yanwu, as well as Song-Ming Neo-Confucians such as Zhu Xi. That formation must be situated within changes in substance—that is, historical conditions and community—before we can understand “resistance.” So I think we need to bring Takeuchi and Mizoguchi together to understand the Chinese Revolution.

[fig] *The Vicissitudes and Development of Premodern Chinese Thought* (《中国前近代思想的屈折与展开》)

![Cover of The Vicissitudes and Development of Premodern Chinese Thought](attachments/wechat/dxhLMHKR4T14AhOvsrj3zA/015.jpg "=25%")

[fig-note] Mizoguchi Yūzō; Chinese translation by Gong Ying. SDX Joint Publishing, 2011.

**Fu:** You say that the formation of subjectivity needs to be situated within changes in substance. Could we understand this in terms of “being” and “becoming”?

**Murthy:** Yes. In fact, on substance and subject, Hegel says in the *Phenomenology of Spirit*: “We must grasp and express the true, or truth, not only as substance but also as subject.” Hegel himself places the emphasis on subject. He thinks that people see things as real, but substance actually also includes history, community, and other factors. Subject and substance are inseparable for him. The subject is not empty: it always operates within the domain of history, with an immanent logic. Mizoguchi wants to grasp this logic in Chinese history and intellectual history, and to observe how it changes. This becomes the presupposition for his understanding of the Chinese Revolution. He thinks that without understanding it, we cannot understand modern China. Many people studying China in the West emphasize rupture. Takeuchi does too. Mizoguchi, however, thinks that although there are ruptures, continuity lies behind them.

## II. Within “Tianxia,” Between “Tianxias”

**Fu:** At this point, if we follow Spinoza’s understanding of substance, I think we could see it as a kind of immanent universal, or a universal of universals—somewhat like Hegel’s “the universal.” In the sense you have been discussing, “substance is subject” can indeed prompt us to think about possible configurations of universality and particularity today.

For Sakai, this possibility lies in translation, in homolingual and heterolingual communities. Harry D. He is currently Professor of the Philosophy of Human Rights at the University of Padua, where he previously taught political philosophy.[^tomba] During periods in Germany—in Würzburg, Munich, and Hamburg—he specialized in classical German philosophy. Since 2012, he has codirected an international project reexamining dominant frameworks for interpreting global society and challenging the Eurocentrism prevalent in theories of universalism, space, and time. His books include *Krise und Kritik bei Bruno Bauer. Kategorien des Politischen im nachhegelschen Denken* [Crisis and Critique in Bruno Bauer: Categories of the Political in Post-Hegelian Thought], *La vera politica. Kant e Benjamin: la possibilità della giustizia* [True Politics: Kant and Benjamin—the Possibility of Justice], *Marx’s Temporalities*, *Attraverso la piccolo porta. Quattro studi su Walter Benjamin* [Through the Narrow Gate: Four Studies on Walter Benjamin], and *Insurgent Universality: An Alternative Legacy of Modernity*.

**Fu:** So this is why you choose to return to Hegel. You recently wrote a paper on the Hegel–Marx question. You have also said that some Marxists reject Hegel’s political philosophy because they reduce his metaphysics entirely to historical categories, sometimes interpreting it through empirical concepts as well. Within the postcolonial tradition, many thinkers, including Robert Young and Naoki Sakai, have criticized Hegel’s historicism. Yet here you take Hegel up again.

One passage reads: “Because the structure of reason is not purely historical, it provides a standpoint for social criticism. By bringing Hegel and Marx together, we can criticize capitalism on this basis—we might regard capitalism as a deficient, alienated form of objective spirit. We can also explore ways of bringing about a determinate transformation of capitalism, toward a new society consonant with the spirit of right. More specifically, Hegel’s metaphysics and concept of spirit can provide a theoretical perspective from which to examine critically Marx’s concept of the ‘automatic subject.’” (“Hegelian Critiques of Capitalism: Rethinking Modernity through Speculative Metaphysics.”)[^right]

You have answered the question of how to bring Hegel and Marx together, how to uncover the specter of Hegel, and how to find Marx within the postcolonial field in many places. You could say that most of what we have discussed already addresses it. But could you explain this passage further for our readers?

**Murthy:** This is a very complicated question. If I place it within my own experience and the development of my thinking, I have to return to Postone. He had a particularly strong influence on me when I was writing my dissertation. Postone develops the Marxism of the Frankfurt School. One of his fundamental claims is that absolute spirit and capital coincide: Hegel’s absolute spirit is actually a misrecognition of capital. Postone thinks Hegel grasped capital but dehistoricized it, turning a historical category into absolute spirit. This argument is persuasive because it makes capital the subject of history. The twentieth century then has distinct phases: liberal capitalism, Fordist capitalism, and neoliberal capitalism. In this account, the entire twentieth-century world becomes capitalist. Actually existing socialism and so on are all different forms of capitalism.

[person] Moishe Postone (1942–2018)

![Portrait of Moishe Postone](attachments/wechat/dxhLMHKR4T14AhOvsrj3zA/038.jpg "=25%")

[person-bio] An internationally renowned Marxist historian, social theorist, and political thinker, Postone was Professor of Modern History at the University of Chicago, codirector of the Chicago Center for Contemporary Theory, and a member of the committee of the Center for Jewish Studies. He worked extensively on nineteenth- and twentieth-century European intellectual history and critical theory, focusing on critiques of capitalism and modern antisemitism, and on memory and identity in postwar Germany. His landmark *Time, Labor, and Social Domination* represents a creative engagement with classical Marxist theory on the contemporary Western left. His other books include *Marx Reloaded: Rethinking the Critical Theory of Capitalism* and *History and Heteronomy: Critical Essays*.

When I became interested in these theories, I reread Hegel to understand them—to work out what absolute spirit was. Without reading Hegel carefully, one tends to think absolute spirit is this capital-S Spirit that eliminates difference. That is indeed how Postone understands it: it eliminates difference, or at least shapes difference within itself, but this is not genuine difference either.

Rereading the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, I found that Hegel was continually interested in difference, and particularly interested in the state. But the state here does not itself eliminate difference; it recognizes difference. How should we bring these things together? I think that to understand Hegel, we have to return to his fundamental work, the *Science of Logic*. Many people read the *Phenomenology*, but it is actually preparatory. He calls it only a “ladder”: after reading it, you can go on to the *Logic*, which is his real philosophy. There we can understand the relationship between Hegel’s and Marx’s philosophies.

First we need to understand what Hegel’s philosophy is. Many people say that Hegel and Marx both use dialectics, but one is an idealist and the other a materialist. These concepts are too simple to help us understand what Hegel means by idealism. In the *Logic*, he discusses *Idealismus*, idealism. He does not mean that thought belongs on one side and matter on the other. What he calls idealism actually means seeing things as a whole, as a process, rather than merely reifying them. He does not use “reification”; he speaks of individuation. Looking back now, we could say that the Marxist concept of reification is already present here. If idealism means regarding things or individuals as moments within the process of a whole, then Marx could also be called an idealist. So I do not think the idealism–materialism distinction is especially important.

Hegel’s basic philosophy differs somewhat from Marx’s, but I think they complement each other. His *Logic* seeks to derive our basic categories. These are not merely historical categories, however, but the basic categories of all things. It is therefore an ontology. There are different interpretations here. Some call it an ontology; that is the interpretation I emphasize and agree with. Another is Kantian: logic can be understood as the conditions of anything whatsoever, effectively the conditions of thinking or thought. I think that at the beginning of the *Logic*, there is no distinction between thought and things, since that distinction would itself be a presupposition. The conditions of thinking, or of thought, are therefore identical with the conditions of being.

These basic logical categories are prior to practice; practice appears after the philosophy of nature. They are therefore not historical ideas. Purpose, for example, first appears in the *Logic*, but purpose is also connected to life. Every living being has a purpose: to exist, to form itself anew, and so on. Human purposes differ from those of living beings in general. They are relatively free—that is, human beings are self-conscious. Purpose here connects to Hegel’s philosophy of history and philosophy of right, because the conditions under which people can realize their purposes involve social conditions and social structures. That is the problem his philosophy of right seeks to address.

[fig] *True Materialism: Hegelian Marxism and the Modernist Struggle for Freedom*

![Cover of True Materialism](attachments/wechat/dxhLMHKR4T14AhOvsrj3zA/039.jpg "=25%")

[fig-note] Jensen Suther, Stanford University Press, 2025.

This is where Marx comes in. He thinks that people cannot realize their purposes in capitalist society, so they need to “change the world.” From this perspective, I think the two complement each other. Without Hegel, it is difficult to find Marx’s philosophical foundations. The early Marx speaks of species-being, *Gattungswesen*, which is a very Hegelian concept. The species itself is purposive, and capitalism conflicts with it. I have learned a great deal from recent Hegel scholars. Jensen Suther recently published *True Materialism: Hegelian Marxism and the Modernist Struggle for Freedom*. I think it is very good on life and species-being. I also intend to write an essay about the book, but have not yet had time.

**Fu:** I have asked you many questions about Marx, Takeuchi, East Asian studies, and your own intellectual development. Finally, given your participation in so many events in China recently, I would like to know whether you have any questions about Chinese-language scholars and academia. Could you ask us a question?

**Murthy:** First, I am very happy that you are interested in these theories, as well as in Japanese and Chinese studies and Takeuchi. Of course, there are many questions. To begin with, what influence do you yourself think studying these theories—Harootunian, for example—might have on contemporary Chinese academia? Or what would you hope to see come of it?

**Fu:** Well … first of all, I am absolutely pessimistic about the work of theory. Especially after the “age of theory,” theory has of course become a technique internal to academia. You certainly understand the talk of the “death of theory” better than I do, particularly how theory proclaims itself in what remains of its own death. In my view, this self-promotion and self-reflection are ultimately also forms of theory. So my pessimism will not change. The same goes for Harootunian. I really do not see any possibilities here. That may include this interview too.

Perhaps this could be called “despair”—though perhaps not, since we can still talk about something. But at least in this respect, it is a feeling Lu Xun and Takeuchi consistently held on to: in “The Passerby,” for example. So I suppose it should be revenge. I do think that counts as one of theory’s tasks—an *Aufgabe*. Readers will probably have other thoughts.

**Murthy:** Very interesting.

**Zhang Jinniu:** By a fortunate coincidence, I had the opportunity to sit in on Fu Shi’s interview with Professor Murthy. I can understand Fu’s despair and pessimism to some extent. His depth of thought and store of knowledge are extraordinary for someone his age. They may, to a degree, convey the concerns and intellectual reach of the most incisive young scholars of our time, something already evident in the interview above. Fu asked me to offer a few reflections by way of conclusion. I can only venture some brief comments on the parts I feel able to understand. For that reason, this account is bound to leave much out.

Ultimately, Professor Murthy’s affinity with Chinese academia is not accidental. It may have something to do with his Indian heritage: India and China are both developing countries, providing a basis for a shared sense of the problems at stake. As he says, many Western scholars pay little attention to these problems. He also points out that for Third World countries suffering imperialist oppression, the nation-state and even nationalism cannot simply be rejected or discarded. Left-wing revolutionaries in these countries must therefore carry these questions with them as they think about how a Marxist future might be possible. This is not merely a matter of the particularity of Third World countries. Whether in sheer numerical terms or on a global scale, unevenness will determine, in the most historical and practical ways, whether a universalist project against oppression is genuinely possible. A strategy of resistance to capitalism organized around a “multitude,” dispensing with the nation-state and taking an anarchist form, is therefore bound to remain unworkable. This seems especially self-evident at the present moment of geopolitical crisis.

Murthy’s appeal to a Hegelian subject is closely connected to this. Postmodern approaches to deconstruction keep their distance from particular subjects because they regard all such subjects—even resistant ones—as potentially becoming new oppressors in a new cycle. But this amounts to foreclosing the possibility of offering a realistic response to the existing unequal and uneven international order. Some left-wing scholars try to imagine a decentralized structure of resistance. Yet this clearly fails to consider how such a structure could be possible or helpful in Third World countries, much as Lenin long ago identified the problem of “‘left-wing’ infantilism.” In fact, even within developed Western countries, decentralized movements of this kind do not help advance a genuinely transformative left-wing agenda. Conversely, their caution is not entirely unfounded. Some postcolonial scholars reject universality on the basis of an emphasis on particularity, in a manner approaching the Japanese right’s historical fate of “trying to overcome modernity only to be overcome by it.” In doing so, they show that the earlier concern is not groundless.

Perhaps Fu’s pessimism can be understood from this perspective. It is also my concern. If, as theory works through its implications, history can only repeat the script in which the dragon-slayer becomes the dragon—at whatever scale—then we cannot escape a sense of despair, like facing a hopeless black wall, as Lu Xun once did. It is in this sense that the significance of Murthy’s attempts and explorations becomes perceptible and worthy of attention. He seeks to assert Hegelian and Marxist universality while also attending to the particularity within it. His hoped-for result is that asserting universality will not lead us to evade organized revolutionary practice in the real world, often organized through nation-states; and that attending to particularity will not lead to depoliticized, alienated agents of oppression, such as fascism in Japanese history.

I am clearly not qualified to say where a possible solution lies. That is precisely the problem Murthy is trying to address. I can therefore offer only a few generalities based on my reading. As Kōhei Saitō observes, in *Capital*, Marx both engages in apparently pure theoretical reasoning and inserts the more historically situated chapters “The Working Day” and “Machinery and Large-Scale Industry.” This suggests that a way out of the problem must be sought by returning to practice. Taken together with the question of the nation-state discussed above, it means that perhaps we can find solutions and answers only in the historical and present-day practices of socialist states. What does the nation-state mean for the left? This question cannot be evaded or dismissed; it must be addressed directly and constructively. Only then can we build on that basis an alternative international order aimed at interrupting the cycle of oppression. After all, Marxism points not only toward programs of resistance whenever the capitalist system enters a cyclical crisis. It points also toward alternative, constructive choices beyond capitalism’s institutional order.
