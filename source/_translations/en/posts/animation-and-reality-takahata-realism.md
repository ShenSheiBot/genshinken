## Foreword

In a commentary on *Tamako Love Story* (2014), Ryōta Fujitsu places the film in a lineage of Japanese animation devoted to everyday life. He traces that lineage from Isao Takahata’s *Heidi, Girl of the Alps* (*Arupusu no Shōjo Haiji*, 1974; hereafter *Heidi*), through *Magical Emi, the Magic Star: Semishigure* (*Mahō no Star Magical Emi: Semishigure*, 1986), and on to *Tamako Love Story*. “By deliberately excluding dramatic incident and accumulating depictions of daily life,” Fujitsu writes, “what we now call ‘everyday-life animation’ had already reached a kind of completion with *Heidi, Girl of the Alps* and *3000 Leagues in Search of Mother* (*Haha o Tazunete Sanzenri*, 1976; hereafter *3000 Leagues*).” (1)

In *Heidi* and *3000 Leagues*, Takahata observes ordinary life without constant melodramatic pressure. That choice distinguished him from his contemporaries and proved that animation could be sustained by daily experience. But once conventional drama recedes, everyday detail has to carry unusual weight. The work must persuade us that its characters do not merely perform a plot: they live. This tacit compact with the viewer is the basis of everyday-life animation.

Takahata approached his fictional worlds almost as a cultural anthropologist. He established their social fabric—clothes, food, housing, transportation, education, labor, politics, and economics—as well as their natural and geographical conditions: climate, temperature, light, terrain, water, and ecosystems. (2) This is a broad program for grounding a story in a credible world. Yet animated space and the movement of bodies through it are not only matters of craft. They raise the deeper problem of realism, and Takahata’s changing answers to that problem are central to his practice.

I will use Takahata’s everyday-life animation as a point of entry into the larger question of reality in animation. Two kinds of question need to be kept distinct. The first is methodological: by what techniques does animation represent reality? The second concerns the medium’s nature: what makes such representation possible when no camera has photographed the things we see? The same distinction applies to time and space. We can ask how animation depicts them, but we must also ask what kind of time and space an animated image creates. Method and ontology are different, yet inseparable here.

Live-action film has an immense literature on realism, and André Bazin is impossible to avoid. His ontology of photography turns on the claim that “the photographic image is the object itself”; from that premise grew a whole system of film theory and criticism. What matters here is the affinity between Bazin and Takahata. They worked with different media, but both asked how an image could make reality felt.[^42]

While making *3000 Leagues*, Takahata drew on Italian neorealism, especially Vittorio De Sica’s *Bicycle Thieves* (*Ladri di biciclette*, 1948), to face social conditions without softening them. Bazin, of course, was among Italian neorealism’s great champions. Toshio Suzuki recalls Takahata often discussing Bazin’s *What Is Cinema?* (3), and Takahata himself cited “The Virtues and Limitations of Montage” (“Montage interdit”) when arguing for “composition in depth,” which “gathers into a single frame antagonistic figures pregnant with crisis.” (4) These recollections and citations indicate that Bazin’s film theory mattered greatly to him.

For the present argument, the author groups Bazin’s realism under three headings: the reality of what is represented, the integrity of its time and space, and the credibility of its narrative structure. Bazin’s long takes and composition in depth—often, though not exclusively, using deep focus—help hold these concerns together. (5)

In “Respecting Real Time, Committed to Acting-Led Direction: A Technical Study of Isao Takahata’s Direction” (6), Seiji Kanō identifies three hallmarks of Takahata’s work: a fixed point of observation, with the viewpoint held still throughout a shot; frequent long takes of more than ten seconds; and the meticulous scene design and acting design that make both possible. A fixed shot helps produce Takahata’s cool, observant restraint. A long take, central to Bazin’s composition in depth, does something cutting cannot: it preserves the continuity of a space, the duration of an event, and the connections among the things within it.

Fujitsu’s history leads to later directors who also favor fixed long takes, especially Takashi Anno and Tomomi Mochizuki. Anno’s examples include episode 27 of *Maison Ikkoku* (1986), *Magical Emi, the Magic Star: Semishigure*, and *Yokohama Kaidashi Kikou* (1998). In a characteristic passage, he dispenses with non-diegetic music and lets ambient sound carry the scene. As the landscape changes almost imperceptibly, time becomes visible; the characters’ inner lives remain unspoken, and the poetry of the everyday emerges from the waiting itself. The first episode of Mochizuki’s *Twilight Q* (1987) relies almost entirely on fixed shots. Episode 2 of *Seraphim Call* (1999) is organized around an extended surveillance-camera conceit. *Kimagure Orange Road: I Want to Return to That Day* (1988), meanwhile, uses long, oppressive shots to convey adolescent unease and the suffocating pressure of a love triangle.

Naohito Takahashi’s *To Heart* (1999) belongs here as well, though his approach descends from Osamu Dezaki and differs from the others in method. The governing idea is still to accumulate ordinary details until everyday life seems to say, “nothing has happened, and yet something seems to have changed.” (1)

Directors outside this lineage, including Mamoru Oshii and Yoshiyuki Tomino, have also named Takahata as a major influence. Both value location research and the texture of lived experience; Oshii also makes highly stylized use of the fixed long take.

Tracing every branch of Takahata’s influence would be a different essay. Here, his evolving realism will serve as a point of reference. Like Osamu Dezaki, he is a major node in several lineages of twentieth-century animation, but that point of reference moves because Takahata’s own practice changed.

Live action—Bazin’s theory and the fixed long take—can therefore offer animation a methodological model. It cannot explain what animation is. The two media differ at the level of their materials and thus in their expressive means. I will approach that difference through three overlapping problems: shot and space, movement and time, and reality and sign. The last is the heart of the essay and will require a short detour through painting.

## Shot and Space

On *Heidi*, Takahata and Hayao Miyazaki made layout a fully systematized stage of Japanese animation production.[^43] A layout is the working drawing that fixes the viewpoint, staging, scale, and spatial relation between characters and background before key animation begins. Scene design is the broader architecture of a place and a shot; a layout gives that design a concrete visual form. The Mushi Production tradition tended to emphasize graphic design, concept, and overall impression, as in Osamu Dezaki’s theatrical *Aim for the Ace!* (*Ace o Nerae!*, 1979) and *Haguregumo* (1982), whose layouts were drawn by Yoshiaki Kawajiri. The Toei and A Pro traditions represented by Miyazaki and Tsutomu Shibayama paid closer attention to constructed space, camera position, and the visual effects associated with different lenses.

Miyazaki was responsible for scene design, expressed through layouts, on both *Heidi* and *3000 Leagues*. This made spatial continuity a deliberate part of production. The present article treats his use of standard and telephoto-like constructions as one important influence on the later development of layout.

In live-action cinematography, apparent spatial compression comes chiefly from camera position and framing, while focal length helps determine what framing can be obtained from that position. Depth of field also depends on aperture, subject distance, image format, and viewing conditions, although long-lens setups commonly show fewer planes in sharp focus. Bazin’s composition in depth often favors wide-angle staging and deep focus so that foreground, middle ground, and background remain legible at once. Cel animation is not bound by the same optical combination. Because those planes are separately drawn and photographed, an image can imitate telephoto-like compression while keeping every part of the frame sharp.

Takahata made extensive use of such views in *Heidi* and *3000 Leagues*. Miyazaki’s production notes treat standard and telephoto-like constructions as closer to the way he believes the human eye reads space, and therefore as calmer and easier to take in. In his account, their compression of distance can convey the atmosphere of a place, while a wide-angle construction can feel more urgent or unsettled. These are aesthetic judgments about drawn space, not universal laws of optics or perception.

Animated space can also combine spatial systems that could not coexist in front of a physical camera. The governing rule is often that “how it looks” matters more than optical consistency. A nearby object may be drawn with wide-angle exaggeration while the distance is treated as telephoto. In geometrically correct one-point perspective, parallel lines converge at a single vanishing point; an animator can shift or multiply those points whenever the composition reads better that way. Several viewpoints can occupy one frame, each directing the eye toward what matters.

This is one sense in which animated space is more plastic than mechanically photographed space. Miyazaki writes in the notes, “I set up layouts according to how the human eye sees.” His layouts do not reproduce the projection of a single lens with mathematical consistency. They organize space according to visual judgment: how the mind expects an object to sit, approach, or recede. That departure from strict optical geometry can make the result feel more immediate, not less real.

Animation’s freedom to escape a fixed shape or spatial system recalls Sergei Eisenstein’s idea of “plasmaticness”:

> “A rejection of once-and-for-all allotted form, freedom from ossification, the ability to assume any form dynamically… I call this ability ‘plasmaticness,’ for here we have a being represented in drawing—a being that already possesses a given form, that has already attained a particular appearance—which nevertheless behaves like primal protoplasm, without any stable form, capable of assuming the form of any animal life upon the evolutionary ladder.” (7)[^44]

Plasmaticness is often taken to mean the drawn line’s freedom to change shape. Animation scholar Nobuaki Doi argues that it also operates in the viewer’s mind. In Yuri Norstein’s phrase, animation can set an abstract, formless “metaphor” in motion. That metaphor is not the drawn object alone but the fluid mental image it calls up. A mark can invite several readings; a space can be felt in more than one way. Animation thus lets us entertain possible realities rather than treating our habitual relation to the world as the only one. It does not abandon reality so much as loosen its foundations. Once a transformed world convinces us, the uniform photographic texture of live action may itself begin to look artificial: animation becomes intimate and live action estranged. (8) (9)

Takahata and Miyazaki put this metaphorical space in the service of realism. Because it is built around the way a body sees and moves rather than the way one lens projects light, it can sometimes feel closer than live action to bodily experience.

Painting can also combine viewpoints. Édouard Manet’s *Luncheon on the Grass* (*Le Déjeuner sur l’herbe*, 1863), for instance, contains more than one perspectival system in a single image.

An animation, however, is not perceived like a still painting. Characters cross its space; the viewpoint pushes in, pulls back, pans, and tracks. This movement, joined to the unfolding situation, makes life perceptible. As Doi puts it, “A still image has no need of the magic that makes life visible. That magic must occur within a story, in the situations the characters inhabit.” (8)

A striking moving example appears in the opening of *Galaxy Cyclone Braiger* (*Ginga Senpū Buraigā*, 1981), animated by Yoshinori Kanada. One shot combines three viewpoints—looking down, straight ahead, and up—each aligned with a different character. The drawing is spatially warped, and then the movement follows that warped design. Ryūsuke Hikawa remarks: “Ordinarily, there would be no way to fit a layout like this into a single drawing. Kanada did it without batting an eye. More than that, he made the image move according to its distorted perspective. The sensation of the whole warped picture turning is uniquely his; conventional visual imagination could never have produced it.” (10)

Kanada’s layouts, especially those for *Invincible Super Man Zambot 3* (*Muteki Chōjin Zambot 3*, 1977), have influenced animation history as profoundly as Miyazaki’s.[^47]

## Movement and Time

In “The Virtues and Limitations of Montage,” Bazin writes: “When the essence of a scene demands the simultaneous presence of two or more factors in the action, montage is ruled out. It can reclaim its right to be used, however, whenever the import of the action no longer depends on physical contiguity, even though implied.” (5) This is the domain of composition in depth: several actions or forces remain physically present on different planes of one shot, and their relation matters more than the cut.

For weekly hand-drawn television animation, such shots can be exceptionally demanding. The longer a shot runs, the more exposed its acting choices become; when it depends on continuous character movement, it may also require many more drawings. The constraint was especially severe in the 1960s and 1970s, when budgets, schedules, and staff were all tight. Productions answered with montage and what Bazin called “dramatic time,” but also with held drawings, cycles, pans across still art, reuse, and selective motion.

“The practice of breaking a scene down into shots, and the montage of those shots,” Bazin writes, “amount to an expressionism of time—to rearranging events according to an artificial, abstracted time: dramatic time. Neorealism raised an all-around challenge to virtually all of these general rules of cinematic spectacle.” (5) Dramatic time is discontinuous. In animation, its leaps and compressions can make not only clock time but a character’s experience of time feel uneven.

Mushi Production developed omission, abstraction, simplification, exaggeration, abbreviated motion, and camera or compositing effects in pursuit of a famous ideal: “to use as little movement as necessary while drawing as much dynamism as possible from animation.” (11) Together, these methods are known as limited animation. Takahata did not reject them outright. In *Heidi* and *3000 Leagues*, however, he worked against their usual dramatic compression. He let gestures run their course and gave scenes enough duration for acting to reveal small changes in feeling. His style was a compromise, worked out in productive tension with limited animation.

The drawing counts make that tension concrete. An episode of *Heidi* used two or three times as many drawings as an ordinary television series, averaging between 5,000 and 6,000. (12) Mushi Production tested the opposite extreme in *Sabu to Ichi Torimono Hikae* (1968): episode 9, handled by Moribi Murano, used about 1,200 drawings, while episode 14 used only 800. (13)

Sustaining the quality of a long, drawing-intensive series depended on exceptional collaborators such as Miyazaki and Yōichi Kotabe. After the Takahata–Miyazaki–Kotabe team dissolved, Yoshifumi Kondō, recommended by Miyazaki, served as animation director for the whole of *Anne of Green Gables* (*Akage no An*, 1979; hereafter *Anne*). With extraordinary energy, he kept the characters consistent while preserving minute variations in their acting. Miyazaki left scene setting and layout after episode 15, and Michiyo Sakurai assumed layout duties from episode 18. The transition was rough, but the series recovered its poise despite severe production pressure.

At the end of episode 1 of *3000 Leagues*, Marco parts from his mother in a shot lasting more than a minute. She says goodbye; he cannot answer. After she leaves, he stands with his head bowed for nearly thirty seconds. Only when the crowd has dispersed does the camera begin a painfully slow move toward him, as if entering his thoughts. He hears the fading voices of other farewells. The time spent waiting and the steady accumulation of feeling make the brief close-up of his tears overwhelming. Marco runs, falls, gets up, then stumbles and rises again. The design is simple and almost unshaded; the movement never shows off. Yet his grief seems to fill his whole body. Toshiyuki Inoue believes Miyazaki handled the passage from layout through key animation himself. To Inoue, it has an emotional force live action could not achieve: he can feel the pain of Marco hitting the ground. (14) The scene shows Takahata’s respect for duration and for the full physical course of an action, as well as his dependence on animators capable of uniting layout and performance.

Yet in Bazin’s phrase “two or more factors in the action,” the decisive word is not “action.” What matters is the actual or potential relation between elements. Spatial unity makes that relation visible; duration lets it gather force.

Writing about Nanook waiting beside a seal’s breathing hole in Robert Flaherty’s *Nanook of the North* (1922), Bazin observes in “The Evolution of the Language of Cinema”: “The length of the hunt is the very substance of the image, its true object.” (5) Hunter and hole, one moving and one still, remain in the same shot. Their spatial relation holds, while the wait becomes the event.

Animation can use still compositions in much the same way. They conserve drawings while concentrating attention on a relation that is already present or about to change. But a held drawing (*tome-e*) or a background-only shot lacks the tiny fluctuations of light, shadow, and weather through which live action registers time. In the roughly one-minute hold at the end of episode 24 of *Neon Genesis Evangelion* (1995; hereafter *EVA*), music continues, but time seems to stop rather than pass.

How, then, can animation make time palpable while a character remains still?

Before *EVA*, episode 4 of *Anne* and *Angel’s Egg* (*Tenshi no Tamago*, 1985) had already tried. Both use a fixed viewpoint, an unusually long duration—the *Anne* shot lasts about twenty seconds, the one in *Angel’s Egg* more than two minutes—and a tense confrontation, actual or potential, among several nearly motionless figures.

Unlike the hold in *EVA*, these two shots give time a visible carrier. In *Anne*, grass sways in the foreground while dark clouds move overhead. In *Angel’s Egg*, a flame flickers and dies, changing the light around it.

The pairings Bazin–Takahata and Tarkovsky–Oshii help explain what Takahata’s and Oshii’s images share. Oshii makes his own connection explicit: “I wanted to try making a film like *Stalker* (Сталкер, 1979).” (15) He kept exploring ways to make time felt in later works: through the unusually precise in-between drawings devoted to Batou in the grocery-store scene of *Innocence* (2004), for instance, and through Suito Kusanagi’s involuntary micro-movements—movement at the edge of stillness—in *The Sky Crawlers* (2008). Time becomes heavy, almost viscous.

Anno, Takahata, and Oshii all connect an image to a span of time that does not depend on large character movement. This is different from saving drawings by moving only a mouth (*kuchi-paku*) or eyelids (*me-pachi*) over a held body. Their aim is to make viewers share the character’s duration while remaining aware of the distance between their own attention and the character’s inward state. Waiting becomes a form of identification without collapsing that distance.

In *EVA* and *Angel’s Egg*, duration also builds suspense for what follows. In Gilles Deleuze’s terms, these are deliberately prolonged sensory-motor situations: perception is held open, but it still prepares an action. The scene in *Anne* comes closer to his time-image, in which movement no longer drives the image.

Anne jumps from the carriage and sits on a fallen fence some distance away. After a long silence, she stands and returns without speaking. The ordinary link between perception and action has broken down. Her withdrawal prevents what she sees and feels from passing directly into an effective response; thought occupies the gap. Anne and Marilla sit motionless, unable to decide what to do. “No foreseeable, determinate action is going to occur—only the arrival of the improvised event of the present… latent affect and spirit are made manifest through the impotence and poverty of action.” (16) (17)

*Anne* organizes time even more radically. The series followed *Heidi* and *3000 Leagues*, works in which everyday-life animation had already “attained a kind of completion.” Yet its first episode immediately began a new experiment and became celebrated within Japanese animation history.

Animation historian Seiji Kanō described episode 1 on Twitter as “a pace faithful to real time, long takes and fixed shots, too much dialogue, reportorial male narration, detached observation, and so on—the entire episode is an exception to the rules.” (18) Mamoru Oshii was equally shaken by Takahata’s handling of time. Put simply, he says, what takes thirty minutes in life takes thirty minutes on screen. Could television animation really be allowed to do that? The example still gives him courage whenever he thinks about it. (19)

The “thirty minutes” is an exaggeration, but it captures the episode’s audacity. Several long passages transcribe time with very little omission or compression. The viewer’s time becomes the characters’ time.

From the eighth minute, when Matthew meets Anne at the station, until the closing scene, when she is still riding toward Green Gables, the documentary impulse is pushed to an extreme. The tension in the script, Anne’s breathless and extravagantly childish stream of talk, and the beauty of the open countryside combine into a peculiar experience: the journey feels endless and fleeting at once.

The series spends its first five episodes on two consecutive days in Anne’s life. Episode 4 is essentially one long carriage conversation between Anne and Marilla. They set out at the end of episode 3; when episode 4 ends, they still have not reached Mrs. Spencer’s house. Takahata lavishes time on ordinary detail in pursuit of the third part of Bazin’s realism: the integrity of narrative structure. An event is allowed to keep its shape instead of being reduced to its plot function.

Oshii later returned to Takahata’s influence:

> “I cannot say how much *Anne of Green Gables* taught me, and *Chie the Brat* (*Jarinko Chie*, 1981) benefited me enormously as well, because I watched it more times than I can count. As a director, I don’t think any work has been as helpful as that one, and everyone in the industry says the same. Let me say it again and again: Takahata at that time was a director of the very first rank. … He was one of those rare people who could properly use the axis of time in directing a film as a whole, and I once studied his work obsessively.” (15)

Takahata’s work could serve as a point of reference for a whole study of Oshii. Here, however, we must turn from the methods they share to the different kinds of image in which those methods operate.

## Reality and Sign

In “The Ontology of the Photographic Image,” Bazin writes:

> “Perspective made it possible for painters to create the illusion of three-dimensional space, within which things could appear to resemble our direct perception of them… Since the picture was painted by a human hand, doubt about the image could never be dispelled. In the passage from baroque painting to photography, the most essential phenomenon… is a psychological one: photography completely satisfies our appetite for illusion by a mechanical reproduction from which man is excluded.” (5)

This is the heart of Bazin’s ontology: “the photographic image is the object itself,” because it arises through mechanical reproduction with the human hand excluded. Photography satisfied the appetite for resemblance so completely that it freed painting from its “obsession with likeness.” Painting could turn from imitation toward representation—or, in Nelson Goodman’s term, denotation, which does not require an image to resemble its real-world counterpart.

If animation’s realism is to begin from animation as an art, it cannot reduce itself to likeness, even when it borrows directorial methods from live action.

A painting can organize visual elements—figures and geometric forms, tonal and color effects, shadows and transparency—into either flatness or perspectival depth. We can think of these elements as signs.

Animation adds time. Distortion drawings that convey high speed (*obake*), changing speed lines, timing—the pace and rhythm of an action—spacing—the distance a form travels from one drawing to the next—and even the key animator’s exposure sheet can all function as signs. Just as perspective produces the illusion of three-dimensional space, timing and spacing produce an illusion of continuous time. An exposure pattern (*koma-uchi*) determines whether each drawing is held for one, two, or three film frames; position and the relation between drawings then bring time into being under the animator’s control. Norman McLaren made this property the basis of his definition: “What happens **between** each frame is much more important than what exists **on** each frame … Animation is therefore the art of manipulating the invisible interstices that lie between frames.” (20)[^45]

Tatsuyuki Tanaka describes the attraction of such signs this way: “Isn’t our attachment to sign-based expression—our taste for it, our fetishism of patterned expression—the very essence of drawing in manga and animation?” (21) Animation’s wonder lies less in copying reality than in the expressive force of the sign and its plasmatic freedom to change. Lines and timings carry traces of how a body has perceived, analyzed, and imagined the real world; another body, watching, activates those traces. In that sense, the sign has a latent corporeality.

To understand how this happens, we need a simple account of seeing. Within animation’s illusion, present perception sinks into memory while memory returns to inform perception, as in Henri Bergson’s famous “memory cone.”

[fig] Figure 5 from *Matter and Memory*. The cone $SAB$ contains the recollections accumulated in memory. Its broad base, $AB$, is pure memory, not yet mixed with present perception, and remains still. The apex, $S$, is the body’s present perception and capacity to act. It moves forward where it meets the plane $P$, our current representation of the universe. Because the body is concentrated at $S$ and belongs to $P$, it both receives actions from the other images on that plane and responds to them. Thought continually moves between the apex $S$ and the base $AB$. (22)

![Bergson’s memory cone, with nested ellipses labeled AB, A-prime B-prime, and A-double-prime B-double-prime narrowing to apex S on plane P](attachments/roof-archive/cv8255716/12-matter-memory-cone.png)

“The present moment is always the meeting-point of a double, opposed movement: perception ceaselessly sinking into memory, and memory ceaselessly becoming perception.” (16) When we watch an animation, drawn fluttering (*nabiki*), explosions, walks, and runs recall experiences of the physical world. But those animated movements also teach us to see that world again.

Take a contour line. Things in the world do not come with outlines; the line is a painterly device that traces the apparent edge of a surface. Precisely because it can trace an edge, however, it became one of painting’s great instruments of recollection. A spare or elaborate contour can swiftly call up our experience of a body or object. Memory descends, in Bergson’s diagram, from the latent base $AB$ to the present apex $S$. We project what we remember onto the pattern before us.

Yet a contour never has only one possible meaning. Freed from the object, it can stir different memories and set the viewer’s formless mental “metaphor” in motion. The familiar duck–rabbit makes the point.

[fig] The duck–rabbit figure, reproduced in *Ludwig Wittgenstein: The Duty of Genius*.

![The same black outline can be seen as a duck facing left or a rabbit facing right](attachments/roof-archive/cv8255716/13-duck-rabbit-figure.png)

The same marks can be seen as a duck or as a rabbit. Nothing on the page changes; the aspect under which we see it does. Wittgenstein calls this “aspect-seeing,” the ability to see one thing **as** another. (23) It requires imagination, but also cultural knowledge and familiarity with visual conventions—the resources stored across the latent plane $AB$. The duck–rabbit reminds us that even a persuasive illusion is built from lines, forms, shadows, and colors that could be read otherwise.

A contour need not be accurate to convince us. As we saw in “Shot and Space,” an artist may bend linear perspective or use several viewpoints to create a stronger visual relation. There is no single measure of a “correct” outline. Ernst Gombrich writes of Paul Cézanne: “In his ardent search for a sense of depth without sacrifice of the brightness of colours, for an orderly arrangement without sacrifice of the sense of depth—in all his struggles and gropings there was one thing he was prepared to sacrifice if need be: the conventional ‘correctness’ of outline.” (24)

[fig] Cézanne, *Compotier, Glass and Apples* (1880).

![Cézanne’s Compotier, Glass and Apples, with the bowl and fruit held by distorted and intermittent contours](attachments/roof-archive/cv8255716/14-cezanne-compotier-glass-apples.png)

Cézanne distorts the painting’s perspectival relations, most visibly around the rim of the compotier. His contours appear and disappear, stabilizing the objects within restless air and light. In this way he seeks what the early Impressionists neglected: “the solid and durable shapes of nature.” Roger Fry writes in *Cézanne: A Study of His Development*:

> “For the pure Impressionists this question of the contour was not so insistent. Preoccupied as they were by the continuity of the visual weft, contour had no special meaning for them… But for Cézanne, with his intellectual vigor, his passion for lucid articulation and solid construction, it became an obsession. … The contour is continually being lost and then recovered again. The pertinacity and anxiety with which he thus seeks to conciliate the firmness of the contour and its recession from the eye is very remarkable. It naturally lends a certain heaviness, almost a clumsiness, to the effect; but it ends by giving to the forms that impressive solidity and weight which we have noticed. … At first sight the volumes and contours declare themselves boldly to the eye. They are of a surprising simplicity, and are clearly apprehended. But the more one looks the more they elude any precise definition. The apparent continuity of the contour is illusory, for it changes in quality throughout each particle of its length. There is no uniformity in the tracing of the smallest curve. … We thus get at once the notion of extreme simplicity in the general result and of infinite variety in every part. … In spite of the austerity of the forms, all is vibration and movement.”

Here the sign’s latent corporeality becomes visible. Contour does not merely describe an apple from the outside; it records a bodily sensation of weight, order, and instability. In Cézanne’s words, “nature speaks in oneself, and completes itself.” The body concentrated at the apex $S$ is both the starting point of perception and the center from which action becomes possible. Cézanne felt the solidity of form and nature’s endless variation together, and the contour carries both.

Gilles Deleuze makes the bodily relation even more explicit:

> “At one and the same time I become in the sensation and something happens through the sensation, one through the other, one in the other. … And at the limit, it is the same body which, being both subject and object, gives and receives the sensation. As a spectator, I experience the sensation only by entering the painting, by reaching **the unity of the sensing and the sensed**. … This was Cézanne’s lesson against the Impressionists: sensation is not in the ‘free’ or disembodied play of light and color (impressions); on the contrary, it is in the body, even the body of an apple. Color is in the body, sensation is in the body, and not in the air. Sensation is what is painted. What is painted on the canvas is the body, **not insofar as it is represented as an object, but insofar as it is experienced as sustaining this sensation**.” (25)[^46]

Sensation has a subjective side—the nervous system, movement, instinct, temperament—and an objective side—the fact, place, or event that acts on us. They cannot be pulled apart. In phenomenological terms, the perceiving body does not stand outside the world; it dwells among things. A painting therefore need not render the body as an object. It can render a world as encountered through a body.

We can now return to animation. In a conversation with Yūichirō Oguro, Tatsuyuki Tanaka calls the process *saikigōka* (再記号化), literally “making signs anew.” I will use **remaking signs** as a working translation, not as established Anglophone animation terminology. Reality, he says, is a springboard: the process takes the established signs of manga and animation, tests and rebuilds them against experience, and offers them again as deceptively simple signs. (26) This resembles Gombrich’s “schema and correction,” but Tanaka has something more historically disruptive in mind than adjusting an existing formula to a new subject. His example is Satoru Utsunomiya’s work on *Gosenzo-sama Banbanzai!* (1989), whose treatment of volume and light transformed later *sakuga*—passages of animation prized for their distinctive movement and an animator’s individual style. In the present author’s shorthand, Utsunomiya’s and Mitsuo Iso’s work helped set off a “realism revolution” in animation.

Two features of remaking signs matter here. Reality is only the starting point. The goal is not to approach it by ever closer imitation, but to rebuild an equivalent pattern of relations from the way we perceive it. A schema changes through “not the discovery of likeness, but the discovery of equivalences—equivalences that enable us to see reality in terms of an image, and an image in terms of reality. And the basis of this equivalence lies not so much in the similarity of elements as in the identity of responses to certain relationships.” (27)

The other feature is economy. A single well-chosen device can produce several effects more powerfully than a mass of detail. In *Gosenzo-sama Banbanzai!*, Utsunomiya rejects the conventional attached shadow at the seam between head and neck. He treats the head as an object blocking the light and casts its almost circular shadow directly across the torso. The rough, simple shape makes the head feel solid and locates the light source at the same time. A drastic reduction of reality’s complex light can therefore feel uncannily real. Literal optical accuracy is beside the point.

[fig] Episode 4 of *Gosenzo-sama Banbanzai!*

![A low-angle frame from episode 4 of Gosenzo-sama Banbanzai! in which the pale leg of a red-clothed figure, mostly outside the frame, dominates the foreground while a blue-haired figure in pink stands beyond it; simplified light and shadow build volume](attachments/roof-archive/cv8255716/15-gosenzosama-banbanzai-episode-4.png)

Takahata, Miyazaki, and their collaborators pursued the same kind of realism in *Heidi* and *3000 Leagues*. Reality was their springboard; they rebuilt animation’s signs with an artlessness that conceals enormous control. Consider Grandfather toasting cheese over the fire in episode 2 of *Heidi*. Animation had never quite made cheese mean this before. The form is almost absurdly spare: a golden mass bends over a stick, while one long white highlight and one bright dot give its surface a wet sheen. As it warms, the shape sags and stretches. Those changes tell us that it is soft, viscous, and yielding; memory supplies texture, heat, smell, and taste.

Toshiyuki Inoue remarks: “There is not one unnecessary line in the image. It has only simple contours, highlights, and shadows, yet somehow the cheese looks more delicious than real cheese. Even when an image has been pared down to almost no information, viewers can still draw from it the impression of the real thing they know. That is precisely animation’s advantage.” (14) The highlights do not resemble every glint on a real piece of cheese. The melting motion does not copy the exact behavior of dairy fat. Together, however, they produce an equivalent bodily response: we recognize both the substance and what heat is doing to it.

[fig] The melting-cheese scene in episode 2 of *Heidi, Girl of the Alps*.

![A yellow piece of cheese sags on a stick over a fire in episode 2 of Heidi, its surface defined by two white highlights](attachments/roof-archive/cv8255716/16-heidi-episode-2-cheese.png)

No real cheese melts in exactly this way. That is why the example matters. Plasmatic movement completes the sign, allowing sensation to flow through an image that is not a copy of the world. Remaking signs discovers bodily knowledge from within.

Eisenstein associated the protoplasmic image with “pre-logical, sensuous thinking.” In the dated primitivist vocabulary of his period, he linked that mode of thought to children and to peoples then described as “primitive”: a temporary release from formal reason, a chance to “forget” logic. (28) Whatever we make of that historical framework, the point about projection remains useful. Recollection is selective. We remember more in some images and less in others, and the exchange between memory and perception produces very different convincing worlds—the worlds of Hayao Miyazaki and Hiroyuki Okiura, for example.

This returns us to the apex $S$. A sense of reality is made through a perceiving body; it is not a property that reality simply hands to an image. Perspective makes a flat plane look deep. A contact multiplane shot (*micchaku multi*) slides tightly stacked image layers at different speeds to simulate parallax. When animation is shot on threes—one drawing held for three film frames—viewers still perceive continuous movement.

In Bazin’s ontology, a camera mechanically records a body from the outside and preserves the time and space around it. The hypothesis developed here is that animated signs can address bodily sensation through another route, closer to what the British painter Francis Bacon described as an image striking the “nervous system.” This does not mean that live action cannot act just as forcefully on sensation; it identifies animation’s selective reconstruction as one distinctive means. Our attachment to a style of line or movement can become a fetishism of pattern; Yoshinori Kanada’s *sakuga* gives physiological pleasure. The animator’s perception of time, space, weight, and flesh is selected, rebuilt, and heightened in the sign. By “intensification,” I mean this sharpening of bodily qualities through drawing and timing, not the addition of detail for its own sake. (29) Consider Yoshinobu Inano’s sensuous treatment of flesh in the ending of *Aura Battler Dunbine* (*Seisenshi Danbain*, 1983), or his drawings of Lynn Minmay, the idol singer at the center of the *Macross* franchise.

Remaking signs is difficult, and successful expressive systems tend to harden into convention. Animation is also made and sold as a repeatable commodity: once a sign works, production encourages its reuse. An innovation can quickly become a house style.

In 2010, Tatsuyuki Tanaka wrote on Twitter that at Studio Ghibli, “pictorial and animated expression has entered another dimension, but the repertoire of character acting has not changed at all since *Heidi* and *3000 Leagues*. Nothing new has been added.” (30) The criticism is severe. Even when innovative animators such as Yoshinori Kanada and Shin’ya Ohira participate, the fundamental system of acting in Miyazaki’s films remains continuous with the Yasuji Mori–Yasuo Ōtsuka–Hayao Miyazaki tradition of the 1960s and 1970s. That continuity also testifies to the strength of Miyazaki’s control. And if one does not value the evolution of expressive convention, the criticism has little force.

Takahata’s own trajectory was different. He had already begun revising his realism in *Only Yesterday* (*Omohide Poro Poro*, 1991); he developed that pictorial turn in *My Neighbors the Yamadas* (*Hōhokekyo Tonari no Yamada-kun*, 1999), then carried it much further in *The Tale of the Princess Kaguya* (*Kaguya-hime no Monogatari*, 2013). In “Why *The Tale of the Princess Kaguya* Is So Stunning” (31), Ryōta Fujitsu argues that works such as *Heidi* pursued the kind of verisimilitude animation had cultivated for more than half a century—what might more precisely be called “live-action verisimilitude.” *The Tale of the Princess Kaguya*, he says, graduates from that tendency and turns toward “pictorial verisimilitude,” offering a route other than ever closer imitation of cinema.

The distinction needs refinement. As Eiji Ōtsuka argues in [a Chinese-subtitled video tour of the Isao Takahata exhibition](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV16741147Z3), Takahata’s *riarizumu* is not *shajitsu shugi*, or pictorial naturalism. (32) The linked video has no English transcript, so this paragraph supplies the relevant claim. His work does not copy nature. *Heidi* asks instead how artless, childlike drawing can make an audience feel reality. (30) The cheese has already shown us the point: the problem is how signs work, not how closely animation resembles live action.

Likeness can still produce a powerful reality effect. If an animation tries to build a dense, continuous world on the model of live action, the temptation is to add ever more visual information. In Takahata’s own later work, the character designs, acting, and art of *Grave of the Fireflies* (*Hotaru no Haka*, 1988) and then *Only Yesterday* move toward greater complexity and outward resemblance. If I had to name a weakness in *Grave of the Fireflies*, it would be that its wealth of detail, further amplified by the sign’s power to heighten sensation, can become so overwhelming that it clouds the calm lucidity of Takahata’s direction.

Likeness is a useful means, but where does that road lead? If animation is judged by live action’s standard, at what point does it become merely a poorer substitute? What advantage remains specific to animation? Takahata seems to have recognized the danger and changed course with *My Neighbors the Yamadas*. He gave up an external realism located in the amount of resemblance within each still image. The characters moved closer to manga and were redesigned at roughly four heads tall. At the same time, he absorbed work associated here with Utsunomiya’s “realism revolution.” Shin’ya Ohira, Shinji Hashimoto, and Masaaki Yuasa worked on *My Neighbors the Yamadas*; Osamu Tanabe, Hashimoto, and Norio Matsumoto worked on *The Tale of the Princess Kaguya*.

Takahata now sought reality between images. It resides in the trembling line as it becomes movement, and in the key animator’s sense of rhythm, force, and weight. Timing, spacing, and deformation carry a bodily understanding of how a person moves without requiring each drawing to look like a photograph. This is an internal realism. It reaches a peak in two passages animated by Shinji Hashimoto: the father peeling and eating a banana in *My Neighbors the Yamadas*, and Princess Kaguya’s frantic run beneath the moon.

How do we recognize Kaguya’s run in lines that twist, knot, and nearly dissolve? Why do our hearts shudder when they do? A single line frozen in one drawing might be hair, sleeve, speed, force, or nothing we can name. Its function becomes clear only through the drawings around it. The images do not articulate motion as a chain of fully independent units; their meanings bleed into and reshape one another. This is the “interpenetration” of successive images. The ambiguity is productive rather than vague: it lets the sequence carry force and rhythm that no isolated frame contains. (33)

In the trembling lines, rage, grief, and madness do not arrive as separate labels. They pass into one another as one turbulent whole. This resembles Bergson’s *durée*, or lived duration: time as consciousness experiences it, rather than as a row of measurable instants. “A violent love or a deep melancholy takes possession of our soul: here we feel a thousand different elements dissolving into and permeating one another, without precise outlines, without the least tendency to externalize themselves in relation to one another.” (34) As the lines move, viewers experience continuous transformation—plasmaticness—not a series of self-contained poses. The animator’s intuition of temporal flow reaches the viewer as bodily feeling.

[fig] The moonlit run in *The Tale of the Princess Kaguya*.

![Princess Kaguya, dressed in red, runs through a sparse charcoal-and-watercolor landscape beneath the moon](attachments/roof-archive/cv8255716/17-princess-kaguya.png)

Hashimoto’s work also shows why realism and expressionism have no clean border. In a conversation with him (35), Yūichirō Oguro repeatedly asks when Hashimoto’s orientation toward “the real” began, although the two men mean different things by the phrase. Oguro asks how long his reference footage was and how closely he consulted individual frames. Some viewers dismiss any sakuga made with live-action or 3D reference, as though reference erased an animator’s imagination. That judgment sees only the source and complexity of a movement; it misses the sensibility that selects, transforms, and times it. Hashimoto relied heavily on live-action reference in *My Neighbors the Yamadas*, yet the result remained unmistakably his and won lavish praise from Miyazaki. Hashimoto also stresses the bodily knowledge he gains by performing an action while filming himself. Mitsuo Iso went further while animating Asuka Langley’s battle against the mass-produced Evas in *The End of Evangelion* (1997): his physical identification with the action was so intense that he knocked holes in his desk and wall as he drew.

With *The Tale of the Princess Kaguya*, Takahata turns away from the prestige-realist tendency to add ever more information to the surface of the image. He returns to spare signs and to movement between drawings whose outward likeness is tenuous. Quality of information matters more than the sheer quantity so often used to praise animation. The same principle governs the film’s look. Its sustained watercolor-on-paper style preserves the naked touch and texture of the line; large areas remain blank. Takahata makes subtraction a directorial method, leaving memory and projection to finish what the brush withholds.

As the *Secrets of Landscape Painting*, traditionally attributed to Wang Wei, puts it: “A pagoda may rise into the sky without its hall being shown, seeming now present, now absent, now above, now below. Among earthen mounds, eaves and granaries may be only half exposed; thatched cottages and reed pavilions need only be faintly suggested.” Blank paper asks the viewer’s memory, projection, and experience to finish the world.

This method also rejects the conventional cel system. Takahata had long understood its built-in fracture, described by Tatsuyuki Tanaka as follows: “The characters are line drawings; the background is painted like an oil painting.” (26) The two layers possess different textures, and that separation affects the reality of the image. In *Chie the Brat* (*Jarinko Chie*, 1981), Takahata used pen-drawn backgrounds that resembled colored manga, giving both characters and settings outlines. (36) Even then, the layers remained materially distinct. The shared watercolor style of *My Neighbors the Yamadas* and *The Tale of the Princess Kaguya* instead integrates character and setting into a single pictorial texture, without implying that every production layer was made from the same physical material.

Much contemporary animation moves in the other direction. Backgrounds imitate photographs of real locations, widening the textural gap between them and drawn characters; filters, lighting, simulated depth of field, and other compositing effects then try to stitch the layers together through the language of live-action cinematography. Kyoto Animation art director Mutsuo Shinohara, discussing *Sound! Euphonium* (*Hibike! Euphonium*, 2015), described the problem:

> “We were told to ‘aim for the real’ in the backgrounds, but in the first season we went too far: they began to look like live action. Too much is as bad as too little. Because this is animation, it ultimately has to work as pictorial expression. The backgrounds needed to suit the characters’ movement, and in that respect the first season did not quite succeed. … I kept that lesson in mind for the second season and made sure everyone in the department understood it.” (37)

Shichirō Kobayashi calls ours “an age of the loss of tactility.” That loss helps explain why even animation so readily accepts images that look as though a camera photographed them. (38) Live-action verisimilitude remains useful; the question is what happens to bodily sensation when animation strains to reproduce live action’s effects.

[fig] Kobayashi’s art for *Urusei Yatsura 2: Beautiful Dreamer* (1984) controls emphasis by changing the density of information with distance. The far plane is not treated with *bokashi*—gradated color and softened contours—and it never falls out of focus. Instead, lines break up and details disappear as they recede. Because the distance remains as sharp as the foreground, it keeps a powerful presence rather than fading photographically. (38) Here again are “the solid and durable shapes of nature.”

![Gas tanks fill the foreground of Kobayashi’s industrial cityscape for Urusei Yatsura 2: Beautiful Dreamer; the distant city stays sharp as line and detail grow sparser](attachments/roof-archive/cv8255716/18-urusei-yatsura-2-kobayashi-art.png)

In an essay on Kobayashi, Osamu Dezaki puts the principle bluntly: “From beginning to end, I have rejected the attitude that merely copies the object correctly. A sense of reality lies not in realistic depiction but in the essence of things.” He adds, “Cel drawing and background painting cannot be considered entirely apart… only when the two form a unified whole does something begin to exist as a mode of expression.” (39) The directness is part of their force. By “the essence of things,” Dezaki does not mean a hidden substance in the physical world. He means the essence of our response to it. (27) What matters is not reproducing a cause but discovering the visual relation that produces an equivalent effect.

The production of *The Adventures of Gamba* (*Ganba no Bōken*, 1975) began with a question: how do mice see the world? Dezaki writes, “For a mouse, the overall shape of a rock matters less than the pitted, uneven surface pressed close to its body. If the mice reached Gunkanjima [Battleship Island], the first thing to fill their vision would be the corroded skin of its steel.” The “essence” is the world at the scale of a mouse’s body. In that unity of the sensing body and the sensed world, we identify with the painter—and with the mice.

[fig] Episode 5 of *The Adventures of Gamba*.

![A towering corroded Battleship Island looms over tiny mice on a raft at the waterline in episode 5 of The Adventures of Gamba](attachments/roof-archive/cv8255716/19-gamba-episode-5.png)

In *In This Corner of the World* (*Kono Sekai no Katasumi ni*, 2016), Sunao Katabuchi, deeply influenced by Takahata, made a related choice. Like *The Tale of the Princess Kaguya*, his film casts off an external, photographic standard of realism. Its characters remain close to their manga designs, yet their lives feel vivid and immediate.

Discussing Satoshi Kon in the August 2020 special issue of *Eureka*, Toshiyuki Inoue made the larger stakes clear:

> “From the beginning, Kon searched for something new in image-making, and I think that brought both strengths and drawbacks. A detailed drawing is not necessarily a good drawing. There is nothing inherently wrong with pursuing detail, but detail also has costs for animation. … Once you can draw at a certain level, you understand that **detail and correctness alone can never make a drawing compelling**. … Looking again at the pursuit of detail and correctness that has advanced since Kon’s debut, I feel a strong need to **return to first principles** and ask what gives a drawing its appeal. Isn’t Japanese animation standing at exactly that turning point now?” (40)

Takahata helped define twentieth-century realism in animation, then enacted just such a turn within his own career. The radicalism of *The Tale of the Princess Kaguya* is not a retreat to Japanese television animation of the 1960s. It reaches much further back, to the picture-scroll tradition of the Heian period. (31) Its significance deserves sustained thought.

Takahata’s place in animation history extends far beyond the argument I have made here, and the medium has not yet absorbed the full legacy of *The Tale of the Princess Kaguya*. From the author’s perspective, the twentieth-century ink-wash films of the Shanghai Animation Film Studio offer an important Chinese comparison. *The Cowherd’s Flute* (1963), for instance, develops an animated idiom from Chinese ink painting. This essay does not attempt to survey contemporary Chinese animation or its many local visual styles. Its narrower question is why this particular ink-wash lineage has had relatively few direct successors in recent mainstream production. Modern technology helped *The Tale of the Princess Kaguya* join new production methods to a classical sensibility. What might those same tools make possible for artists seeking to renew ink-wash animation in China?

## Conclusion

I have used Isao Takahata’s realist animation as an entry into the problem of reality in animation. Bazin’s film theory gives us one way to understand Takahata’s direction: animation can take live-action methods, including the fixed long take and composition in depth, as a model for representing reality. But that methodological debt does not erase the difference between the media. Animation’s materials produce time, space, and movement by other means.

An orientation toward the real is therefore not a race to copy the world, and animation is not an asymptote forever approaching live action. Its reality draws on how human beings know the world. In space, it can organize the image around the way vision and bodily judgment construct a scene rather than around the optics of a single lens. In time, it can make duration perceptible through movement, stillness, and the relation between drawings.

“Shot and Space” began with Takahata’s scene design and Miyazaki’s layouts: concrete plans that hold acting, viewpoint, and background in one coherent place. Built around human sight, those layouts became a powerful point of reference for later animation and showed how the medium could construct a reality anchored in bodily perception. “Movement and Time” followed Takahata’s respect for duration: gestures allowed to unfold, time made visible while characters remain still, and events granted enough room to preserve their integrity.

“Reality and Sign” asked the more fundamental question: how can animation make reality felt at all? Bergson’s memory cone and the psychology of projection helped describe the answer, while painting clarified what a sign can do. Animated signs possess expressive force and plasmatic freedom; they carry bodily knowledge of weight, rhythm, texture, and sensation; and they convince us not through likeness alone but through equivalences that awaken the same response as a relation in the world.

This can only be a preliminary inquiry. It is, in fact, the first half of the essay I originally imagined: a set of points of reference from which a second half might begin. I thought I could finish this part in five thousand Chinese characters. Instead, it kept growing until it curled around the whole plan like a monster and swallowed the latter half. If this imperfect essay nevertheless gives its readers something useful to think with, I will be more than happy.
