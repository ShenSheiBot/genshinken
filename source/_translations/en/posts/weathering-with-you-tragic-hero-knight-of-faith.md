A possible psychoanalytic reading of Makoto Shinkai’s *Weathering with You* treats its hero’s final choice as an ethical one. At the decisive moment, Hodaka chooses his own desire: to save Hina, he is willing to sacrifice everything. In that sense, is his choice not a challenge to the “big Other,” an attempt to suspend the existing social order altogether? Yet perhaps we should turn the question around and return to Kierkegaard’s crucial insight into ethics: what if ethics itself is a temptation? Hodaka sacrifices everything for Hina, but what stance should he take toward his own sacrifice?

To pursue that question, we must follow Kierkegaard through the triad of the tragic hero, the knight of infinite resignation, and the knight of faith. The tragic hero sacrifices himself for the psychoanalytic *Thing*. The knight of infinite resignation goes further and affirms that sacrifice, transforming it from a gesture of being-for-another into one of being-for-itself. Finally, the knight of faith renounces the very gesture of sacrifice; in Žižek’s words, “he sacrifices the sacrifice.” This is a characteristically Hegelian triad: through the mediation of infinite resignation, ethics is *sublated*—negated but preserved—into faith.

![Tokyo at sunset in *Weathering with You*](attachments/roof-archive/cv3929743/02-weathering-with-you-sunset.png)

It is not difficult to map this triad onto the film. Is Hodaka’s choice not precisely a sacrifice for the Thing? Does he not reaffirm that sacrifice at the end? On this reading, however, the third moment of the dialectic is missing: the film does not let Hodaka sacrifice his sacrifice, but instead turns that sacrifice into a source of *jouissance*—an excessive form of enjoyment.

On the essay’s reading, the romance between the film’s protagonists perfectly bears out the notorious psychoanalytic formula “woman is a symptom of man.” Hina gives Hodaka an external perspective on his own actions: only through her eyes can his sacrifice appear “meaningful,” even justified—which is why the film’s first half repeatedly emphasizes the price Hina pays for becoming the sunshine girl. The essay identifies this anchoring, meaning-conferring function of Hina’s gaze as phallic. In Lacanian terms, the phallus rescues the subject from the drive’s endless “bad infinity” and thereby makes desire possible.

![Hodaka climbing toward the rooftop torii](attachments/roof-archive/cv3929743/translations/en/03-hodaka-rooftop-torii.png)

For Hodaka, Hina’s existence allows him to evade the impasse of his own sacrifice—the very impasse that a genuinely ethical subject, in the psychoanalytic sense, would have to confront. Faced with the choice between “saving Hina” and “saving Tokyo,” the truly ethical stance would correspond to a nonexistent third option: losing both Hina and Tokyo. Imagine an ending in which Hodaka ultimately decides not to bring Hina back to the ground. When he opens his eyes again in the pouring rain, he finds himself under arrest; worse still, he has lost every memory of Hina. All that remains is the ring. The essay calls it a “skull” without explaining the metaphor: the ring is the truly indelible, inert remainder Lacan calls *objet petit a*.

![The ring in its box in *Weathering with You*](attachments/roof-archive/cv3929743/translations/en/04-ring-in-box.png)

Let us return to the dialectical triad. Its missing third moment is precisely the crucial “negation of the negation,” and its absence turns the entire film into an attempt at obscene transgression. Is the same obscene transgression not also at work in Hodaka’s defiance of the Tokyo Metropolitan Police, which can be read as a metaphor for resistance to the law-and-order system? Hodaka’s defiance readily recalls medieval carnival rituals. On one day of the year, the women of a city would take to the streets and be allowed to exercise rights ordinarily reserved for male citizens. The existing social order was suspended for the day, but everything returned to normal as soon as the carnival ended. So too in the film: the rain over Tokyo never stops, and yet the protagonist’s life returns to its everyday routine.

The ending also differs sharply from Shinkai’s earlier work. Those films remain faithful to impossible love: the Thing must be prohibited, because only that prohibition allows love to emerge as a phenomenon. Here, by contrast, the film affirms that impossible love itself is possible. Is this not the opposition between Kant and Spinoza? Yet we must notice that this affirmative ethics is precisely one of the most prevalent ideologies in contemporary capitalist society. Think of Nike’s famous slogan, “Just Do It.” If *Weathering with You* could speak to its audience, perhaps it would say the same: “Just love. Love as Hodaka does; hold fast to love, whatever the cost…” From this claimed Spinozist affirmation, the essay makes a further, explicitly speculative Deleuzian leap: it reads the flooding of Tokyo as deterritorialization and the flooded city as a smooth, depthless plane.

![A car beneath cherry blossoms in a Makoto Shinkai film](attachments/roof-archive/cv3929743/05-car-under-cherry-blossoms.png)

This brings us to a recurring concern in Shinkai’s work: the post-Oedipal age. *Weathering with You* presents a third option beyond the drive represented by *5 Centimeters Per Second* and the prohibition represented by *The Place Promised in Our Early Days*: it replaces the father’s prohibitive law with the superego’s injunction to enjoy. Unfortunately, psychoanalysis cannot respond to this substitution very effectively. We have to admit that we live in a perverse century, one in which absolute affirmation is also absolute negation. Faced with this challenge, perhaps what we must do is complete the missing third moment, the crucial “negation of the negation.” That is, before undertaking a genuinely radical act, we must first use negative reflection to conceive an entirely new field—one that will be unfamiliar to us all.
