One possible psychoanalytic reading of Makoto Shinkai’s *Weathering with You* treats Hodaka’s final choice as an ethical act. At the decisive moment, he chooses his own desire: to save Hina, he is willing to sacrifice everything. In Lacan’s terms, the decision challenges the “big Other,” the symbolic authority that tells him what a responsible member of society ought to want. He appears to suspend the existing order with a single, reckless act.

Kierkegaard lets us turn that flattering picture around. What if ethics can itself become a temptation? Hodaka sacrifices Tokyo, his future, and any claim to social approval for Hina. The more difficult question is not simply what he gives up, but how he relates to the sacrifice: could he also surrender the heroic meaning he gets from having made it?

Three of Kierkegaard’s figures sharpen the question: the tragic hero, the knight of infinite resignation, and the knight of faith. In the psychoanalytic recasting at work here, the tragic hero sacrifices himself for the *Thing*, the absolute object for whose sake the loss can be justified. The knight of infinite resignation goes further by affirming the sacrifice itself: what began as a gesture for another becomes an inward achievement, a sacrifice pursued for its own sake. The knight of faith makes an even stranger final move. He gives up the sacrificial posture itself; in Žižek’s phrase, “he sacrifices the sacrifice.” He returns to finite, ordinary life without wearing renunciation as a title of nobility.

Read as a sequence, the three figures form a typically Hegelian movement rather than a row of labels: ethics, mediated by infinite resignation, is sublated into faith. First comes sacrifice for the object; then the sacrifice is affirmed for its own sake; finally, the subject’s attachment to sacrifice is itself given up. To call the earlier stages *sublated* means that they are negated without simply being erased.

![Tokyo at sunset in *Weathering with You*](attachments/roof-archive/cv3929743/02-weathering-with-you-sunset.png)

Hodaka moves through the first two stages easily enough. Hina becomes his *Thing*, the object for which everything else can be thrown away. He makes the sacrifice and confirms it again at the end. What never arrives is the third movement. He cannot give up his investment in being the boy who chose Hina over the world. The film instead grants him *jouissance*: not simple happiness, but the excessive enjoyment he draws from his own magnificent defiance.

This is the useful point behind one of psychoanalysis’s most abrasive formulas, “woman is a symptom of man.” The phrase is not a diagnosis of women, nor a claim that women exist only for men. It names a structure within male fantasy. A symptom, in this Lacanian sense, is the private knot that holds a person’s world together. For Hodaka, Hina is that knot. His choice becomes intelligible when he sees it reflected in her eyes: if she receives his ruinous act as love, then the ruin can feel meaningful, perhaps even justified.

That is why the first half of the film repeatedly emphasizes the price Hina pays to be the sunshine girl. Only by taking up her perspective can Hodaka see his own sacrifice as meaningful and confirm it as a sacrifice. Her gaze then performs what Lacanian theory calls a phallic function. The term has nothing to do with anatomy or with Hina becoming “masculine.” It means that her response acts as a seal of meaning: it tells Hodaka what all his frantic motion was for.

The distinction between desire and drive matters here. Desire organizes longing around an object imagined to hold the answer. Drive finds satisfaction in the circuit itself—in chasing, failing, and beginning again. Left to itself, Hodaka’s flight could become that kind of endless repetition, a “bad infinity” of pursuit with no final account of what it means. Hina’s gaze seems to stop the circuit. It gives his desire a name and his destruction a destination.

![Hodaka climbing toward the rooftop torii](attachments/roof-archive/cv3929743/translations/en/03-hodaka-rooftop-torii.png)

But that apparent resolution hides the real impasse. Hodaka rejects the social guarantee embodied by Tokyo, only to receive a romantic guarantee from Hina. Her return, her gratitude, and her love certify that he was right to rebel. An ethical act in the psychoanalytic sense would have to survive without either guarantee: no public authority to approve the choice, but also no beloved waiting to turn catastrophe into a love story.

This is why the excluded third option would be to lose both Hina and Tokyo. That option is not more ethical because it inflicts the most pain. Suffering is not a moral scorecard. It matters because it removes both possible rewards at once—the common good that would justify obedience and the beloved who would justify revolt. Hodaka would be left with an act that no one could redeem for him.

To isolate that possibility, imagine an ending deliberately stacked against every payoff. Hodaka does not bring Hina back to earth. He opens his eyes again in the pouring rain and finds himself in police custody. Tokyo is lost anyway, and, worse still, every memory of Hina has been erased. These events do not logically follow from leaving her in the sky; the thought experiment imposes them precisely to strip his choice of a moral balance sheet. Only the ring remains: a “skull,” a mute, ineradicable lump of matter.

![The ring in its box in *Weathering with You*](attachments/roof-archive/cv3929743/translations/en/04-ring-in-box.png)

The ring in this fantasy is what Lacan calls *objet petit a*: the stubborn remainder that sets desire in motion without ever satisfying it. It is not the same as the *Thing*. Hina as the *Thing* is the fantasy of an ultimate object for which every sacrifice might be worthwhile. The ring as *objet petit a* is what survives after that fantasy collapses. It cannot give Hina back, yet its mute presence prevents the loss from closing. It causes desire rather than fulfilling it.

Now the Hegelian phrase “negation of the negation” has a concrete meaning. Hodaka’s first negation rejects Tokyo’s demand that Hina be sacrificed. The missing second turn would negate the sacrificial logic that makes either Hina or Tokyo the prize retroactively justifying the loss of the other. The imagined ending removes both prizes and even Hodaka’s memory of why he acted. It does not restore the original law; it destroys the terms that made “save Hina” and “save Tokyo” look like the only possible choices. The film stops with Hodaka’s refusal confirmed by Hina and his heroic self-image intact.

Without that second turn, rebellion can become obscene in a precise sense: it enjoys breaking the law but never develops a different relation to law. Hodaka’s defiance of the Tokyo Metropolitan Police, a figure for the machinery of public order, captures the thrill perfectly. Think of the stylized inversion associated with medieval carnival: for one licensed day, women take to the streets and exercise powers ordinarily reserved for male citizens. The established hierarchy is suspended, but as soon as the carnival ends, daily order returns. By giving disorder its allotted holiday, the ritual helps that order resume.

*Weathering with You* complicates that pattern. The rain does not stop, and Tokyo never returns to its old physical state. Hodaka’s revolt leaves an enormous material consequence. Yet Hodaka himself is folded back into ordinary life. His rebellion becomes a contained interval in his biography—an extraordinary adventure after which private routine, and romance, can begin again.

The ending also marks a break with the structures of desire in Shinkai’s earlier films. In *5 Centimeters per Second*, Takaki’s adult life keeps circling the absent Akari. Distance, trains, unsent words, and one last obstructed glimpse at a railway crossing keep the beloved beyond reach. This is the logic of drive: the missed encounter hurts, but it also keeps the circuit going. In *The Place Promised in Our Early Days*, a divided Japan and the immense tower across the border give the young characters’ promise a forbidden destination. Love takes shape through the barrier that delays it. Across both films, the impossible beloved remains impossible: the *Thing* must remain barred for the world of appearances to take shape around its absence. *Weathering with You* affirms instead the possibility hidden behind the barrier. Hodaka reaches Hina and brings her home.

In broad philosophical terms, the shift is from Kant toward Spinoza. The comparison is necessarily schematic. Kant’s critical philosophy bars access to the thing in itself and confines knowledge to appearances. Spinoza, by contrast, offers a language of immanent powers and affirmation. Shinkai’s earlier films preserve love through its impossibility; *Weathering with You* affirms what the attachment can do. Hodaka bets everything on the force of that affirmation.

Affirmation, however, is also one of contemporary capitalism’s favorite commands. Nike’s “Just Do It” sounds like permission, but it is an imperative. If *Weathering with You* could address its audience directly, it might say, “Just love. Love as Hodaka does; hold fast to love, whatever the cost…” Liberation from the old “No” begins to sound like a new order: you must want intensely, act decisively, and enjoy without apology.

Here an admittedly speculative Deleuzian leap becomes possible. The ideological link is the fantasy that removing every fixed limit will release desire into pure movement. The film gives that fantasy an urban form. The flood *deterritorializes* Tokyo: roads, stations, and neighborhoods are pulled loose from the uses that once organized them. Underwater, the familiar city becomes a smooth, seemingly depthless plane. New routes open, but the old coordinates that told people where and how to move have disappeared. Shinkai’s earlier cities were full of rails, crossings, roads, and timetables that channeled desire even when they frustrated it. The cherry-lined traffic in *5 Centimeters per Second* makes a useful contrast: movement remains beautiful because it still has lanes.

![Cars moving along a cherry-lined road in *5 Centimeters per Second*](attachments/roof-archive/cv3929743/05-car-under-cherry-blossoms.png)

The contrast gives visual form to a problem that recurs throughout Shinkai’s work: what happens to desire when prohibition loses its authority? “Post-Oedipal” is useful here as shorthand, not as a claim that fathers or rules have vanished. It names a culture in which authority increasingly speaks less like a father saying “No” and more like a superego saying “Enjoy.” On this reading, *5 Centimeters per Second* gives us the repetitive circuit of drive; *The Place Promised in Our Early Days* gives us a love shaped by the barrier against it; and *Weathering with You* replaces both with compulsory affirmation.

Psychoanalysis is very good at exposing prohibition. It has a harder time resisting an order that arrives dressed as liberation. In its technical psychoanalytic sense, this is what makes ours a perverse century. “Perverse” here does not mean unusually depraved. It describes a relation to authority in which the apparent transgressor becomes the instrument of authority’s enjoyment. Breaking the rule is no longer rebellion if the real command is to break free, pursue your passion, and enjoy.

Absolute affirmation can then turn into absolute negation. To say yes to one desire at any cost is to erase every person and consequence that falls outside it. The answer is not simply to restore the father’s prohibition. It may be to complete the film’s missing third movement and negate the glamour of heroic affirmation itself.

What philosophy calls negative reflection begins with a blunt question: what has the choice in front of us excluded? What becomes thinkable once we refuse both “save Hina” and “save Tokyo,” or both prohibition and compulsory enjoyment? That unfamiliar field offers no guarantee of virtue, which is precisely its value. Only there might an act change the terms of the world instead of merely staging a holiday from them.
