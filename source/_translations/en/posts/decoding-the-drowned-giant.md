> **Abstract:** This essay interprets the central image in J. G. Ballard’s short story “The Drowned Giant” and its animated adaptation. It proposes that Swift’s giants and dwarfs in *Gulliver’s Travels* offer one way into Ballard’s story. More specifically, it reads the relationship between the giant and his spectators as a contemporary continuation of the Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns in Western intellectual history.

**Keywords: Ballard; the Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns; giants and dwarfs; Swift.**

## I. Ballard’s Life and Poetics

“The Drowned Giant,” Volume 2, Episode 8 of *Love, Death + Robots*, adapts the short story of the same title by British science-fiction writer J. G. Ballard. The distinction between the two narrators matters throughout this essay. Ballard’s first-person narrator is unnamed; the adaptation names its corresponding narrator Steven. Because the plot advances largely through Steven’s voice-over, the short can at times resemble an audiobook; added dialogue gives the screen version more dramatic texture. The adaptation trims some details and supplies others without, in this essay’s view, changing the story’s conceptual core. Reading the works together can reveal what the later version inherits, but its additions remain the adaptation’s choices rather than evidence of Ballard’s original design.

Ballard’s life and intellectual concerns provide context for the interpretation developed here. He was born in Shanghai in 1930 and encountered war and death as a child. During the Japanese occupation, he and his parents were confined in the Lunghua civilian internment camp until the end of the Second World War. This essay treats that experience as a formative source of Ballard’s reflections on human nature and his early disaster fiction, including *The Crystal World*, and argues that the trauma of war remained present throughout his writing. In “The Drowned Giant,” the crowd eventually covers the body with graffiti that includes a swastika. The biographical reading proposed here connects that detail with the wartime violence of Ballard’s childhood, though the symbol alone cannot establish a direct autobiographical source.

Ballard devoted himself to science fiction without sharing a simple faith in science or the future. His work repeatedly imagines social and technological development eroding human values. In a 1968 interview, he criticized the dominant strain of midcentury science fiction—exemplified for him by Heinlein, Asimov, and Clarke—for remaining too optimistic after Hiroshima and Auschwitz. He also objected to scientific speculation that approached magic while losing touch with the seriousness of lived reality. Instead, Ballard urged science fiction to explore what he called “inner space”: the meeting point of inner and outer reality, where the mind presses upon the world rather than merely projecting fantasy into it.[^1]

Michael Moorcock’s account of “inner space” shifts the emphasis in a similar direction. Science fiction need not revolve around outer space, spaceships, robots, scientists, or explorers. It can place an ordinary person in the near future and explore psychological life through experimental prose, dystopia, entropy, or pessimistic visions of what lies ahead.[^2] Ballard’s pursuit of inner space helps explain why his fiction so often moves between science fiction and literary allegory. The animated “The Drowned Giant” also works as an allegory, rich in metaphor and philosophical reflection. Ballard himself placed the story on the fantastic or allegorical side of his writing rather than presenting it as straightforward science fiction.[^3]

His skepticism extended to the Enlightenment. In a 2003 interview with *The Age*, Ballard called the Enlightenment view of humankind “a complete myth.” It encourages us to think of ourselves as sane and rational most of the time, he argued, although we are not. He also said the Enlightenment had not succeeded, since we repeatedly discover things in ourselves that do not fit our self-image as its civilized heirs.[^4] Those remarks help orient this essay’s interest in the story’s suspicion of human confidence. They do not determine what the giant means.

## II. Literary Parallels to “The Drowned Giant”

Three literary comparisons open different approaches to Ballard’s story. None proves that the works share a single meaning. Their value lies in showing what each parallel can illuminate—and where it stops fitting.

### A. Melville’s *Moby-Dick*

Ballard repeatedly associates the giant with a whale, making *Moby-Dick* an obvious point of comparison. He may even be invoking Melville: the unnamed narrator compares the drowned leviathan’s mass and dimensions to those of the largest sperm whale.[^5] The body is later turned into fertilizer and animal feed, recalling the rendering of whale blubber. When Steven visits the beach at night in the adaptation, barrels in the frame evoke whale oil without revealing what they contain. The butchers’ conversation more firmly suggests the commercial processing of the giant’s flesh.

These details, along with the remains’ eventual absorption into the environment, invite an ecological reading. That reading need not be displaced. This essay’s narrower claim is that ecology does not exhaust the story’s interest in how people use, mutilate, and remember the body. On this reading, the relation between humanity and nature also refracts a culture’s atmosphere, psychic condition, and way of life. The possible echo of *Moby-Dick* is therefore a step toward the article’s broader concern with human existence.

Ballard’s spectators climb on the giant, mark him, dismember him, and finally forget him. The unnamed narrator does not. The adaptation sharpens this contrast: at the end, Steven says that he still remembers the giant and repeatedly dreams of him returning to life, gathering the scattered pieces of his body, and running back to the sea. Both works begin with disbelief that a giant could exist and end by separating the observing narrator from the public’s amnesia; only the adaptation adds the dream. The body’s dispersal can thus register an ecological process while also staging a disappearance from collective memory.

### B. Kafka’s *The Metamorphosis*

Both “The Drowned Giant” and Kafka’s *The Metamorphosis* begin with an impossible body inside an otherwise comparatively realistic world. A drowned giant appears on the coast; Gregor Samsa awakens as a monstrous insect. Both stories end without full resolution, and both ask how ordinary people respond to the impossible. Yet their perspectives differ sharply. Kafka gives us Gregor’s thoughts after his transformation. Ballard gives us the unnamed narrator’s imaginative projections onto a corpse. Any inward life ascribed to the giant belongs to the narrator’s rhetoric, not to a consciousness represented directly by the story.

Gregor’s first concern is not how to become human again but how to get to work. He worries about the family’s livelihood, his aging father, and his sister’s education. Becoming an insect almost seems less terrible to him than the damage his transformation may do to others. That concern stands against his family’s response. His father remains cold, and his violence helps bring about Gregor’s death. His mother and sister begin with sympathy but gradually become indifferent. Once Gregor concludes that he contributes nothing and has become a burden, his wish to erase himself becomes stronger than his sister’s wish to be rid of him, and he chooses death.

Ballard’s crowd follows a different but comparable movement. The spectators begin in fascination, then revel on the body; as it is dismembered, their excitement cools. Within months, everyone except the unnamed narrator has forgotten that the giant existed. Those who encounter his remains remember only a sea creature. In *The Metamorphosis*, the family’s changing attitudes support readings of Gregor as an alienated worker and as someone driven toward self-erasure by those closest to him. In Ballard, the spectators’ changing attitudes likewise offer an important clue—not to one exclusive identity for the giant, but to the story’s concern with spectacle, use, and forgetting.

If the work’s purpose were exhausted by a simple lesson in environmental protection, the author argues, the painstaking movement from astonishment to indifference and finally to forgetting would remain underexplained. The Kafka comparison therefore adds another emphasis: it makes the crowd’s changing attitude and eventual amnesia central to the allegory, without ruling out ecological meanings.

## III. The Metaphor of the Giant

The giant appears without warning after a violent rainstorm, and no one knows where he came from. Ballard makes his arrival seem almost as if he had fallen from the sky.[^6] With no origin supplied, his significance remains open. This essay regards the choice of metaphor as crucial because it determines the direction of interpretation. A reader who treats the giant chiefly as a whale may be led toward ecology or reverence for nature; the preceding discussion finds that explanation insufficient on its own.

The unnamed narrator’s language changes as the body decays. While the giant remains intact, he tends to describe him as an epic hero. As the corpse is cut apart, comparisons with a whale become more frequent. One possible effect of this shift is to show a giant being replaced in collective memory by a sea creature. The crowd eventually forgets what it saw, and the few who retain some memory misidentify the remains. Ballard’s narrator continues to remember. In the adaptation, Steven’s recurring dream makes his own refusal to forget still more explicit.

That contrast raises the central question: how can so many eyewitnesses lose the memory of the giant so quickly? This essay likens the problem to algebra. If the giant is the unknown X, the reader must return to narration and plot and ask what image, substituted for X, best fits the context and explains the repeated movement from wonder to use and from use to forgetting. The analogy is a heuristic for the reading developed here, not a claim that the story can sustain only one meaning.

Ballard supplies the starting point. The giant “existed in an absolute sense”; beside such absolutes, the spectators appeared as imperfect, puny “copies.”[^7] The narrator’s terms do not prove that the giant is perfect or that the people around him are Platonic imitations. They do, however, invite a speculative thought: in the absence of anything greater than themselves, human beings may mistake their own limits for perfection. The source associates that possibility with anthropocentrism and scientism. The spectators’ loss of interest can then be read as a wish to forget greatness and escape the giant’s shadow.

Several Platonic ideas give this reading a vocabulary: human beings as limited imitators, recollection as the recovery of what has been forgotten, and Socratic wisdom as knowledge of one’s ignorance. These ideas are analogies, not links in a proof. Taken together, they lead the author toward the injunction to “know thyself” and toward the claim that forgetting the giant also means forgetting human limitation. Set beside Ballard’s doubts about Enlightenment confidence and scientific optimism, the author reads that forgetfulness as arrogant ignorance and the giant as an image of greatness or wisdom rather than merely a whale. That remains this essay’s construction; Ballard’s sentence alone does not establish it.

The unnamed narrator repeatedly gives the giant the bearing of an epic hero. On the reading proposed here, his rhetoric makes the corpse resemble an ancient hero while the people who climb over and cut apart the body resemble modern dwarfs. The opposition suggests not two kinds of human being but two dispositions: one associated with the authority of antiquity, the other with modern confidence in its own judgment.

The distinction between body and projection is crucial. Steven imagines that the giant may be asleep and might wake to crush the “copies” around him. Ballard’s narrator personifies the corpse as gazing at the sky, unaware of those copies. Neither perception reveals the giant’s actual inward state. They show the narrators creating a symbolic character from a silent body. A fisherman standing on the giant’s chest signals to the crowd, which responds with astonished, triumphant noise. When Ballard’s narrator and his companions come close, their excited chatter falls silent.[^8]

As the corpse changes, Ballard’s narrator imagines its character changing too. He first frames it as a Greek hero, then reads the altered appearance as modest and mature, and finally imagines pain and helplessness during its dismemberment. Ballard calls the crowd’s treatment a “depredation,” and the scale that makes the body sublime also leaves its material traces peculiarly exposed.[^9]

Within the ancient-modern analogy, this vulnerability resembles the vulnerability of an inherited tradition to later interpretation. For this essay, there is no contradiction between the giant’s greatness and his fragility: the first describes the moral stature attributed to the ancients, while the second shows how exposed their material legacy remains to later distortion. If ancient virtue survives in books, the crowd’s treatment of the body can be compared to quoting those books out of context. The comic graffiti may suggest the trivialization of serious classical writing. The swastika invites a darker and more speculative extension. Read through Ballard’s wartime experience, it can connect distortion with catastrophic war and with a modernity that, in this essay’s view, overestimates reason while forgetting divine teaching and ancient humility. One mark on the body cannot establish that history or prove that forgetting antiquity caused Nazism. The association belongs to the critic’s reading of Ballard’s image. The adaptation notably omits the swastika.

When the giant’s head is cut off, the body loses its most immediately human feature.[^10] It comes to look like an anonymous mass of flesh, or like a sign emptied of a soul, and there is less left to inspire awe. The loss of recognizable features anticipates the loss of any memory that a giant existed. For this essay, the sequence resembles one pattern in the transmission of antiquity: an inheritance is fragmented, altered, and eventually forgotten. Ballard explicitly links the fading of the body’s human features with the fading of public interest.[^11] The crowd first strips the giant of sublimity, then forgets the result of its own actions.

Months later, the unnamed narrator encounters the giant only in fragments. Even the fisherman who claimed to have discovered him now remembers an enormous sea creature rather than a giant.[^12] If the body stands for the soul of the ancients, this amnesia becomes an image of modern people judging ancient greatness by standards that can no longer recognize it. On that reading, both story and adaptation support a reflection on technological reason and scientism.

The essay now gives its answer: the giant represents the great figures of antiquity and the qualities of character attributed to them—qualities the author understands as preserved in classical books and invested with divine wisdom. On this reading, the crowd’s treatment of the body restages a struggle between ancients and moderns. The modern dwarfs appear to win, but Steven’s recurring dream imagines the giant gathering his fragments and running back to the sea. This is not evidence that he survives; it dramatizes Steven’s refusal to forget and gives imaginative form to the author’s claim that ancient greatness might leave its arrogant heirs behind.

The author argues that this interpretation finds corroboration in *Gulliver’s Travels*, whose shifts of scale deepen the connection between giants, dwarfs, and the Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns.

## IV. Reading “The Drowned Giant” through *Gulliver’s Travels*

*Moby-Dick* opens the ecological association, and *The Metamorphosis* draws attention to the spectators’ changing attitudes. *Gulliver’s Travels* leads more directly toward the ancient-modern allegory proposed here. Simon Sellars remarks that someone first encountering Ballard through “The Drowned Giant” might take him for a master of magical realism in a Swiftian tradition.[^13] This establishes a broad affinity, not the specific allegory that follows. The next step belongs to this essay: reading the spectators as modern dwarfs and the giant as the ancients and their books.

The Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns concerned the authority of classical inheritance in relation to modern achievement. The essay finds an earlier analogy in Plato’s *Phaedrus*, where it reads differences in the quality of souls through the difference in stature between adults and children. Swift, it argues, turns scale into fable in *Gulliver’s Travels*. The interpretation cited here calls *Gulliver’s Travels* one of the Quarrel’s most penetrating works of political philosophy and reads Lilliput and Brobdingnag as modern democracy and ancient monarchy.[^14]

Ballard’s short story cannot sustain all the symbolic density of *Gulliver’s Travels*, and this essay does not carry the comparison into a complete allegory of political regimes. Plato’s *Republic* makes a conceptual link between qualities of soul and forms of government, but the present argument remains narrower. “Ancient” and “modern” name dispositions constructed by this reading, not uniform judgments about everyone who lived in either period.

Ballard’s unnamed narrator—and Steven after him—approaches the site as a scientist. He and his companions initially rationalize the reported giant as an optical effect. The essay compares that skepticism with the modern party in Swift’s dispute, which invokes scientific progress to dismiss the ancients as foolish. This essay replies on behalf of the ancients that superiority in science does not guarantee superiority in art or character. If modern scientific categories become the measure of all value, ancient greatness may become impossible to see.

That argument is aimed at scientism, not inquiry. The narrator himself provides the necessary complication: he accepts the evidence before him, continues observing after the crowd loses interest, and preserves the giant’s memory. His scientific humility prevents any simple moral division in which every scientist is an arrogant modern. He begins as a dwarf before the giant, in the essay’s terms, yet becomes the giant’s most faithful witness.

At first the spectators keep their distance. As they approach, the contrast in scale shocks them into excited, triumphant noise; once they climb onto the body, they grow increasingly boisterous. The corpse is literally silent because it is dead. Its dignity comes from the unnamed narrator’s epic framing, not from silence alone. He describes water gathered in the giant’s palm as the residue of another world, only for people climbing the arm to disturb it. Within the Swiftian analogy, the scene can figure the first break in a link with the classical world, followed by trampling, dismemberment, and forgetting. At the literal level, however, the pool is simply disrupted by the act of climbing. The swollen body also prevents the narrator from identifying the giant, and the essay reads that lost identity as an image of historical distance from Greek greatness.

Ballard’s rhetoric develops the contrast. The unnamed narrator gives the giant’s skin a pearly luster and rich, sweet odor, while the people swarm around him like flies; young men crawl into his nostrils and howl like mad dogs. The narrator sets the solemnity he attributes to the body against the crowd’s short-lived uproar.[^15] The adaptation preserves the Greek features and epic bearing, while Steven reads the changing body as restrained, modest, and mature. The spectators, by contrast, become “copies” and are shown as noisy and uncivilized.

On this reading, the rhetoric assigns nobility to the giant and baseness to the dwarfs. Swift’s *The Battle of the Books* offers a comparison: it satirizes the modern Hobbes through dogs fighting over prey, then opposes the ancient bee to the modern spider.[^16] Placed side by side, the works make it plausible that Ballard’s narrator constructs a similar contrast in value. The claim concerns his rhetoric, not an intrinsic soul disclosed by the corpse.

The police first cordon off the site, then withdraw as thousands arrive at the beach, with at least two hundred people standing or sitting on the giant. Later they return, clear a path, and bring university anatomists and marine biologists. The experts decline the officers’ offer to help them onto the giant’s palm, though the story does not say why. Once the authorities leave again, the crowd climbs back onto the body, now compared to gulls gathering on a huge fish.

The author interprets this withdrawal as a failure by both the state and scholars to protect the giant. On the allegorical premise, the episode becomes a warning: when institutions fail to cultivate public memory, greatness can be forgotten and public confidence can harden into arrogance. Ballard does not make that educational or political program explicit, and the experts’ reasons for refusing remain unstated. The claim that scholars should have established the giant’s value and that police should have protected him as part of humanity’s lasting memory remains the critic’s conclusion from the scene.

The crowd is extraordinarily noisy, so the living spectators initially appear more vital than the corpse. Yet Ballard’s narrator says that the giant remains more alive to him than the people looking at it. This metaphorical vitality belongs to his response, not to the giant’s biology. If giant and dwarf stand for ancient and modern dispositions, the essay reads it as the continuing life of the classics and of enduring value.

When the narrator finally climbs onto the body, he senses its surrender to time and imminent decay. The ceaseless transformation he perceives as life within death gives him the courage to step onto it. Two implications follow within the essay’s analogy. Classical tradition and the noble spirit represented by the giant may endure, but they cannot prevent later readers from altering or forgetting them. And the giant is not a god but an embodied, limited being: if even he is vulnerable, the dwarf has still less reason for arrogance. The narrator sees the body’s humiliation as making it more human and imagines that its vulnerability draws out the smaller creatures’ repressed malice.

The story’s swastika pushes this reading toward modern history. The adaptation keeps the graffiti but removes that symbol. The author first treats the mark as an indictment of Nazism, then extends it into a critique of modernity by linking the crimes of the Second World War with the distortion and forgetting of the ancients. That link is an interpretive extension, not a causal history established by the text. The firmer sequence comes after the giant’s head is removed: his identity becomes harder to recognize, and public interest disappears.

When the giant appears again, he survives only as fragments. The essay compares that fragmentation with quoting classical texts out of context and, in postmodern terms, with deconstructing classical tradition. In the author’s analogy, the dwarfs have dismantled the giant’s inheritance and then erased him from memory. Even while looking at his remains, people insist they are parts of a whale. By invoking “God is dead,” the author gives this amnesia a theological and philosophical charge: it becomes a loss of the capacity to recognize sublimity and greatness. That phrase names the critic’s analogy; it is not Ballard’s diagnosis.

The adaptation’s ending works in a different register. Steven repeatedly dreams that the giant comes back to life, gathers his scattered pieces, and returns to the sea. The disappointment and agency in that scene belong to Steven’s projection. Within the allegory, his imagined withdrawal figures ancient wisdom leaving those who have forgotten it.

Allan Bloom’s preface to *Giants and Dwarfs* offers a fitting final comparison. Bloom rejects the familiar image of dwarfs standing on giants’ shoulders as a form of self-satisfied humility: why assume that giants exist merely to be climbed? Perhaps they set us down long ago and slipped away, leaving only the illusion of a broader view. A later generation, taking intimacy with greatness for granted, may eventually deny that giants ever existed and dismiss the story as a lie invented by teachers for their own power. Bloom imagines the giants looking down on this comedy and laughing.[^17]
