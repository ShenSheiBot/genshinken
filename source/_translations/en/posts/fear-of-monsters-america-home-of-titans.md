## Foreword: Fear, Making, and Possession

“Fear of monsters” usually means the fear that beasts inspire in human beings. It can also name fear moving in both directions, from human to animal and back again. That reciprocal relation provides this essay’s starting point, but its larger subject is what people do with the creatures they fear: imagine them, interpret them, capture them, and claim them as their own. From folk legend to the flickering screen, immense and uncontrollable beings have often stood at the heart of a story. The United States became a major producer of modern monster culture. This essay follows gigantic creatures through American natural history and film, asking how beings cast as extraordinary helped define the boundaries of nation, nature, and civilization.

## I. Vaster Than Empires, and More Slow: Beasts in Environmental and Historical Perspective

> My vegetable love should grow
> Vaster than empires, and more slow.
> —Andrew Marvell, “To His Coy Mistress”[^1]

In her short story “Vaster than Empires and More Slow,” Ursula K. Le Guin describes a group of explorers encountering a complex, all-encompassing ecosystem: “sensitive, irrational, immortal, isolated.” Its substance is the only form of life on the planet—the planet’s vegetation taken as a whole. The plants fear the dangerous explorers just as the explorers fear the plants. The planetary organism fears visitors capable of mastering and altering it, visitors who also carry the power of colonial expansion. Each explorer, in turn, fears becoming no more than another casualty in the history of exploration.

Osden, the team’s empath, can perceive this relation because of his extraordinary sensitivity. He says, “I suppose I could feel the roots. Below me in the ground, down under the ground. . . . I felt the fear. . . . I couldn’t stop sending the fear back, and it kept growing. . . .”[^2] Le Guin takes her title from Andrew Marvell; the story might therefore be said to turn his “vegetable love” into vegetable fear. Jeremy Bentham’s famous test for moral consideration begins with the familiar case of animal suffering: the decisive question is not whether a being can reason but whether it can suffer. Le Guin then makes a speculative leap from sentient animals to a sentient but irrational planetary ecosystem. Her story imagines “nature” not as inert matter awaiting human mastery, but as a living presence capable of fearing its human visitors.

Le Guin’s science fiction renews a much older cultural habit: societies repeatedly refashion nonhuman threats into images of contemporary power. The tradition is not exclusively American. Chinese classics, for example, teem with dragons and sea beasts that modern media can recruit for new purposes. In Edward Abbey’s *The Monkey Wrench Gang*, a multinational corporation acquires the anatomy of a planetary sea monster—suckers and tentacles, a parrot’s beak, a computer center for a brain, and liquid capital for blood. As dangerous wildlife receded from everyday experience in many industrial societies, such cultural memories did not disappear; they migrated into print and film and attached themselves to new institutions. The American case that follows asks how this process turned fossils and cinematic creatures into objects through which a nation imagined itself.

## II. Early American Giants and Monsters: The Claverack Giant

> Mild is my behemoth, though large his frame;
> Smooth is his temper, and represt his flame,
> While unprovok’d. This native of the flood
> Lifts his broad foot, and puts ashore for food;
> Earth sinks beneath him, as he moves along
> To seek the herbs, and mingle with the throng.
> —Edward Young, *A Paraphrase on Part of the Book of Job* (1719)[^3]

With Young’s Behemoth as our guide, we can begin with a fact: gigantic animals once roamed the land later called America. The claim is literal only if by “America” we mean the landmass rather than the later nation. In the summer of 1705, near Claverack in the Hudson River Valley, a Dutch farmer unearthed a tooth almost the size of a fist and weighing nearly five pounds. Because it was taken to resemble a human tooth, the find was soon reported as “one of the great Teeth of a Man.”[^4]

In 1650, the Irish archbishop James Ussher published a chronology that dated Creation with apparent biblical precision. His work belonged to an intellectual world in which natural philosophy could proceed alongside theology and Scripture could supply the framework of human history. That background helps explain how the Claverack find was received, but the geography matters. The tooth was unearthed in New York; an account appeared in the *Boston News-Letter*, and specimens and reports then traveled through colonial networks. By 1706, teeth had reached the Puritan minister and poet Edward Taylor in Westfield, Massachusetts. Cotton Mather, writing in Boston, later sent reports of the remains to the English naturalist John Woodward. The story did not begin in Massachusetts, but two of its most influential early interpretations emerged there, within the Puritan ideal of Massachusetts as a Bible commonwealth.[^claverack]

As reports circulated, colonial writers put the supposed giant to different uses. Taylor’s unfinished poem made it an ambiguous American counterpart to the English romance hero *Guy of Warwick*. Mather instead cast the giant as a villain destroyed by the Flood. Their archive of comparisons was heterogeneous: biblical giants, the giants of Virgil’s *Aeneid*, medieval romance, Northumberland dragon lore, and Thomas Robinson’s speculative cosmogony *The Anatomy of the Earth* (1694). What united those examples was not genre but function. They supplied Europe with a storied world of wonders; Taylor and Mather wanted the New World to possess wonders of comparable weight.

The tension lay between possession and demonization. Colonial writers could take pride in the bones as an American wonder while comparing their imagined owner to pagan giants defeated in biblical tradition, especially Og, king of Bashan. In Mather’s version, the Flood’s destruction of the giant could even parallel the military defeat of Indigenous peoples in a providential “holy war.” Taylor, by contrast, incorporated Indigenous accounts into English folk traditions and made the giant a more ambiguous hero.[^claverack] This essay reads both moves as colonial: Indigenous traditions and an unknown natural past were absorbed into a settler framework that claimed authority to interpret and possess them. The comparison is an interpretation of these texts, not evidence that all colonial clergy understood Indigenous peoples as literal giants.

When Mather’s reports reached Woodward and the Royal Society, British naturalists placed the supposed giant within competing histories of the earth. For Woodward, the Flood had destroyed a habitable “first earth,” the world before the Deluge, and natural philosophy could establish the event’s material traces. On this account, the Flood had left behind a wilderness in which science and industry became tools of human survival, while fossils appeared to confirm a scriptural natural history.

Woodward rejected the human-giant story and treated the bones as the remains of a nonhuman monster. In the decades that followed, fossils of the still-unidentified creature acquired the name *American incognitum*—the “unknown American.” The chaos of the postdiluvian world could then support a history of “progress” from a savage, pagan age toward a civilized, Christian one. Following Paul Semonin, this essay treats that scriptural natural history as one strand in the intellectual background that later American founders would secularize.[^semonin] The claim is not that colonial clergy directly caused Revolutionary thought. It is that Benjamin Franklin’s generation inherited a public culture in which fossils already made American nature a subject of theological and learned debate.

## III. “We Possess the Monster”: Fossils and National Myth

> But to whatever animal we ascribe these remains, it is certain such a one has existed in America, and that it has been the largest of all terrestrial beings.
> —Thomas Jefferson, *Notes on the State of Virginia* (1785)[^5]

During the eighteenth century, two expanding empires encountered enormous bones: British colonists pushed westward across North America, while Russian expansion across Siberia brought mammoth remains to wider attention. A later Russian joke would declare that “Russia is the homeland of elephants.” Its humor lies in making a comparatively young political identity possess a natural history measured in millennia. The same temporal sleight of hand matters here. Colonists, and later citizens of the United States, increasingly described prehistoric animals as American. Around the Revolutionary era, the gigantic fossil became useful precisely because it could make the new nation look ancient, abundant, and formidable.

Comparisons with Siberian mammoths and living elephants helped naturalists move away from the human-giant theory. The American bones came to be understood as the remains of an immense quadruped, though its teeth led some observers to imagine it, incorrectly, as a ferocious carnivore. Its nonhuman identity did not destroy the earlier logic. The incognitum could still be treated as a remnant of the Flood. In the historical reading advanced here, the creature therefore migrated from colonial theology into the political imagination of the Revolutionary era without losing its status as a wonder.

The Scottish anatomist William Hunter made the jaw central to this reinterpretation. Comparing fossils from the Ohio Valley with elephant jaws, he concluded that the unknown animal was distinct from living elephants and probably carnivorous. Jefferson later shared the carnivore error. To these naturalists, the formidable molars seemed to reveal a formidable predator. This essay reads that imagined predator as doing two kinds of cultural work: it confirmed America as wilderness, yet also made the creature’s supposed possessors—first the colonies and later the United States—appear exceptional. In that symbolic register, classifying the fossil became a way to claim authority over the wilderness and bind the monster to national identity.

Why did such a claim matter? Around the time of the Revolution, the Comte de Buffon’s theory of American degeneracy held that the New World weakened animal life: its creatures were supposedly smaller and its human inhabitants correspondingly diminished. Jefferson answered Buffon in *Notes on the State of Virginia*, using the incognitum as evidence that American nature could produce terrestrial animals of unrivaled size. The United States did not literally lack a history, but its classically educated white founders privileged the shrines and monuments of Europe. The argument here focuses on a settler canon that did not adopt Indigenous histories as the new nation’s own antiquity, and understands that exclusion through the racial hierarchies of settler education. Within that restricted historical imagination, the incognitum offered a substitute founding monument. Jefferson embraced it as an elephant-like but carnivorous quadruped; immense and supposedly without equal, it became part of his rebuttal to Buffon and gained further attention while he served as minister to France.

Here the essay makes an interpretive rather than a strictly causal claim. Semonin argues that the incognitum became a symbol of national identity; the fossil did not supply a legal or philosophical basis for independence.[^semonin] Instead, zoological parity offered a vivid analogue for political parity. If the New World was not naturally inferior to the Old, the monster could symbolize the rejection of Old World superiority. God, nature, and nation converged in that image. The same fossil could be imagined as a remnant of the Flood and, much later, as material for a national mythology. In this account, a scriptural wilderness was gradually claimed for the United States through cultural production.

Across these changing interpretations, white settler intellectuals could cast themselves as civilizing agents confronting peoples and landscapes they called savage. The jaw of the *American incognitum* supplied an image of prehistoric nature’s supposed ferocity and allowed the desire to explore the natural world to shade into an imperial claim over it. This is a reading of a recurring rhetoric, not proof that every clergyman, naturalist, or founder held an identical doctrine. The creature was eventually identified as the American mastodon rather than a human giant, but that correction did not erase its earlier cultural power.

The pattern established here has three parts. A gigantic body emerges from a space cast as wilderness; learned interpreters convert it into knowledge and spectacle; and its scale is enlisted to define a national community. *King Kong* repeats that pattern in another medium. The analogy is structural, not genealogical: the argument does not require Cooper to have borrowed the mastodon myth directly.

## IV. “It Came to Life!”: The Monster Reborn on Screen

> “At least one of our national characteristics is illustrated in the RKO-Radio production of *King Kong* which loomed over the audiences of both Radio City moviehouses last week. It is a characteristic hard to define except that it is related to that sometimes childish, sometimes magnificent passion for scale that foreigners have remarked in our building of hundred-story skyscrapers, our fondness for hyperbole in myth and popular speech, and our habit of applying superlatives to all our accomplishments. … Efforts to explain it have not been very satisfactory; the result is usually a contradiction in which we are represented as a race that is at once too civilized and not civilized enough.”
> —William Troy, “King Kong,” *The Nation*, 1933[^6]

![Crowds lining the sidewalk beneath the Radio City Music Hall marquee for *King Kong*](attachments/roof-archive/cv14096688/figure-40.jpg)

*King Kong* struck like a thunderclap. At its New York premiere, audiences poured into the city’s concrete jungle to watch a “jungle film.” Merian C. Cooper set out to bring an immense figure to the screen: a dangerous giant primate and, more importantly, a modern monster. The film was not the first to use models and special effects to stage gigantic creatures, but it became a watershed in their cultural history. Dracula and his supernatural kin embodied terror, while the sailors of *Battleship Potemkin* embodied revolutionary passion. They are not direct ancestors of Kong; instead, they mark two kinds of screen power that he would combine. Kong joined terror and combat to model-based spectacle and mythic scale, creating a figure that would run through American giant-monster cinema for decades.

Why does Kong share the features of earlier monsters, and what makes him distinctive as a giant primate? To answer, we must consider the “jungle film.” Although *King Kong* was often treated as horror, contemporary viewers also placed it beside the travel documentaries and adventure films then in vogue. Those forms centered on ethnographic looking and contact with an exoticized “Other.” Within that colonial expedition culture, the camera could occupy a position once associated with the rifle: it let the traveler bring home an image, claim knowledge, and turn an encounter into property. The two instruments were not identical, and early filmmakers did not avoid the risks of field travel. Their footage, however, converted those journeys into spectacles for distant audiences. *King Kong* makes the connection visible through Carl Denham, simultaneously filmmaker and explorer: he first seeks an unprecedented image and later turns Kong’s captured body into a public exhibition for profit.

Yet *King Kong* was more than a jungle adventure. Films that offered an exotic view without adding anything new were already losing their audience; by 1932, journalists were predicting the demise of the animal-adventure film. Cooper combined several elements within the old formula. The romance between Ann Darrow and Jack Driscoll added a damsel-in-distress plot to the expedition, while an expansive publicity campaign drew viewers into the theater. The plot then develops the camera-and-rifle analogy in stages rather than making them equivalent at once: Denham sets out to film, proceeds to capture and exhibit, and finally gives way to the aircraft that kill his subject.

Kong is the creature in whom these themes converge. Denham takes the actress Ann Darrow to remote Skull Island because his planned film requires a “Beauty” to confront a “Beast.” The enormous wall, ritual spectacle, and chief’s offer of women stage the islanders as an undifferentiated, racialized collective before Kong appears. When Denham refuses to exchange Ann, the islanders abduct her as a sacrifice. The encounter becomes a dress rehearsal for the film’s encounter with Kong: both the islanders and Kong are framed as threats who seize a white woman. Denham does not arrange the abduction, but his project places Ann in danger and makes her serve the Beauty-and-Beast story he wants to film. The male explorers then mount their rescue, incapacitate Kong with gas bombs, and turn him into a captive spectacle—a victim, in the essay’s analogy, of the same knowledge-and-collection enterprise that enabled the expedition.

Why call him a victim? Kong is not only an exotic animal eventually sacrificed in New York. Early-twentieth-century natural history often approached animals through violent practices: conservation could begin with hunting exemplary specimens, while anthropology was entangled with colonial rule and its moral disasters. To encounter Kong is to encounter an animal, but also a being insistently made humanlike. As a giant primate suspended between human and animal, he is exposed to both forms of violence, and the expedition’s knowledge places his life in danger.

The sequence that made Kong indelible, however, unfolds in a place he had never entered before: New York. The city becomes the final stage on which his story is completed. Within the film’s crude evolutionary hierarchy, his upright body, attachment to Ann, and combat with machines make him increasingly humanlike: no longer merely a captured animal, he becomes a fighter. Some critics therefore read his rampage beside the figure of the boxer, itself a flashpoint in arguments about race, class, and nation.[^7] Kong attacks the machinery of civilization like a combatant, while “bestial” force becomes a description of the fighter. His destruction of an elevated train and his ascent of the city’s towers may look aimless, but both carry him toward the stage Cooper has prepared for his death: the Empire State Building. At the same time, his struggle symbolically overturns New York’s order from below. Kong submits to the narrative and rebels against it at once. He becomes a popular myth because, in the final moments of his life, he fights like a titan—both as a gigantic body and as a challenger to the gods of modern technology.

Atop the Empire State Building, Kong sets Ann down and fights a futile battle against the biplanes. In an uncredited cameo, Cooper pilots one of the aircraft while his codirector, Ernest B. Schoedsack, plays its machine gunner.[^8] Only here does the camera-and-rifle analogy become literal: the filmmakers enter the frame as the men who shoot their subject. The narrative then acquires two endings. Their aircraft enacts technology’s conquest of the monster; Denham arrives afterward to mourn Kong, or perhaps the ruin of his own spectacle. “Oh, no,” he says. “It wasn’t the airplanes. It was Beauty killed the Beast.”[^9] After a display of American technological power, Denham’s theatrical declaration supplies the classic final line. His romantic myth acknowledges Kong’s tragedy while disavowing the expedition’s violence. That union of ethnographic desire and aggression shapes America’s modern monster mythology.

On screen, Kong moves from an imagined forest jungle into an imagined jungle of steel and concrete. The correspondence with the incognitum can now be stated precisely. Each monster emerges from a space represented as wilderness; each is converted into knowledge and public spectacle; and each lends its enormous scale to a story about American power. The difference is equally important. The incognitum was found on the continent and made to supply the new nation with a prehistoric past. Kong is a fictional foreign captive who becomes an American myth only after he is exhibited and killed in New York. America becomes his “home” not by giving him birth, but by possessing his image and making his death part of its cultural memory.

## Conclusion: The Monsters “We” Make

The relation traced here cannot be reduced to reciprocal fear. Le Guin imagines fear passing between explorers and a planetary ecosystem; the Claverack fossil cannot fear its interpreters, and Kong’s capture matters more to this argument than his inner state. What connects the three cases is the human work of making a monster. Adventurers need trophies, scholars need objects of knowledge, and nations need stories that mark the boundary between themselves and what they call wild. Gigantic creatures can serve all three needs. Once enlisted in a founding myth, the monster helps a community confirm its identity by distinguishing itself from another people, another landscape, or another form of life. The question is therefore not only whether human beings still fear monsters, but what kind of “we” is created by imagining, classifying, and possessing them.

That national “we” provides the essay’s final transition. The Preamble to the United States Constitution begins, “We the People of the United States,” and promises to “secure the Blessings of Liberty to ourselves and our Posterity.”[^10] The analogy is rhetorical, not legal: the Constitution is not an environmental text. The point is that a collective subject speaks in the name of itself and its descendants. Modern humanity increasingly does the same before nature when it speaks as “we humans.” Climate and ecological crises expose the limits of that position, because no collective declaration can make nature merely an object for human use. The task is not to give the monster another unilateral definition, but to relate differently to beings cast as monstrous—to see them as something other than hostile Others or possessions. Only then can we stop measuring every creature, and the shared natural world itself, by a human scale alone.
