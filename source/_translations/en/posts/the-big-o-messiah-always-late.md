## Preface

Sunrise’s robot anime *The Big O* ran from 1999 to 2003, straddling the turn of the millennium. It emerged at a moment when Japanese popular culture drew freely on Christian images of judgment, apocalypse, and salvation. Such images could serve as a shared visual and narrative vocabulary without making a work Christian in any doctrinal sense, or asking either its creators or its audience to believe. *The Big O* sets that religious vocabulary alongside two Japanese traditions: giant-robot anime and tokusatsu monster cinema.

The reading that follows is meant to be adventurous rather than definitive. I do not want to reduce the series to an illustration of theory, or to quote canonical thinkers merely to make the interpretation look learned. The exchange should run both ways: theory changes what we see in the series, while the series tests and sometimes distorts the theory. That leaves room not only for competing readings, but also for productive misreadings.

## I. The Steel Body and the Disciplined Body

When manga artist Mitsuteru Yokoyama first drew the encounter between the giant robot Tetsujin 28-go and its boy controller, Shotaro Kaneda, he could hardly have guessed what would follow. The giant robot went on to become the center of one of anime’s largest and most durable genres. One of the genre’s foundational pleasures is obedience: Shotaro works the controls from outside, and Tetsujin responds at a distance. Yet robot anime has repeatedly worried at that fantasy of command. Evangelion Unit-01 goes berserk; the Unicorn Gundam absorbs its pilot. These examples do not establish a steady historical march from obedient machines to rebellious ones. They expose a tension that becomes unavoidable once the robot is treated as the pilot’s second body.

The giant robot has always offered its operator such a body. In *Tetsujin 28-go*, the machine is created as a substitute for its maker’s son, Shotaro; in *Mobile Suit Gundam: Iron-Blooded Orphans*, Gundam Barbatos becomes the limbs of the paralyzed Mikazuki Augus. Anime often makes this bodily continuity visible: the robot takes a blow, and at the same instant the pilot convulses in the cockpit. Steel and flesh register the same wound.

A rebellious robot seems to break that identification. Yet our bodies have never been identical with the consciousness that claims to command them. The fantasy of perfect bodily control belongs to a particular modern image of the autonomous subject. Actual bodies fall ill, suffer injury, develop appetites, and act against the will. Adolescence makes the gap impossible to ignore: a young person may wake from a wet dream or begin menstruating and find that the most intimate part of the self has become strange. No wonder robot anime so often makes adolescents its protagonists. Bodily experience exceeds the language with which the conscious self tries to contain it. Foucault catches the inversion in a deliberately startling line: “The soul is the prison of the body.”

The changing modes of control tell a parallel story, though not a tidy three-stage progression. Shotaro stands outside Tetsujin 28-go and operates it by remote control. Koji Kabuto flies a small craft that docks in Mazinger Z’s head, placing the operator aboard the robot while preserving the image of a pilot at a command station. In 1979’s *Mobile Suit Gundam*, most mobile-suit cockpits sit in the chest or abdomen, enclosing the pilot within the machine’s core. The difference between a cockpit in the head and one in the torso cannot by itself prove a theory of technology. The broader movement from remote control to bodily enclosure nevertheless matters. The robot ceases to be merely a tool held at a distance and becomes something closer to a body worn from within.

What can a body do? It senses, reacts, and in that sense thinks before the conscious “I” catches up. Body and subject coincide, yet remain divided from within. To imagine myself as coherent and self-identical, I have to treat some of what my body does as though it belonged to an other. The giant robot externalizes that split. It stands outside the pilot as a visible other while also extending the pilot’s body. Once sealed deep inside the cockpit, the protagonist must eventually meet the other that has been repressed within. The brutality Shinji Ikari encounters in Evangelion Unit-01 is not merely the machine’s. It is also his own.

There is a second paradox. Every giant robot is built for a purpose: as a weapon, a tool of production, or some other specialized machine. Yet it can escape that purpose. In Deleuzian terms, the escape is a line of flight, and it happens twice. First the robot slips free of its assigned use, as when the Turn A Gundam is pressed into service drying laundry. Then it slips free of the pilot’s will. At the end of *Martian Successor Nadesico: The Motion Picture – Prince of Darkness*, the liquid running down the Black Sarena’s face is surely not machine oil but tears. This double disobedience—against the machine’s design and against its user—is where the drama begins.

*The Big O* calls its giant machines “megadeuses”; Big O belongs to the Big-type class. These machines are silent protagonists in their own right. They can act without pilots, and they recognize—or reject—would-be operators. Big O answers Roger’s summons, but that response is closer to consent than to mechanical obedience. Alex Rosewater’s encounter with Big Fau makes the danger on the other side of the bargain literal: the cockpit binds him into the machine, turning his claim of mastery into a forced union with it. A megadeus needs an operator, but the operator can no longer assume that he alone is in charge.

That danger gives the series’ theological language its force. Big O’s strength is repeatedly called “the power of God,” and whenever Roger pilots it, the cockpit display announces: “Cast in the name of God, ye not guilty.” The pseudo-biblical English is grammatically incomplete—“ye” has no verb—so the phrase works less like a sentence than an incantation or verdict. Nor is the verdict stable. The image above condemns an army of megadeuses as “YE GUILTY”; the cockpit display answers “ye not guilty.” Accusation and acquittal are built into the relationship between pilot and machine before the series explains either one.

That language opens the theological turn of the argument, but it does not open onto a single “Judeo-Christian” story. Two lines need to remain distinct. Christian ideas of sin, innocence, and salvation shape the series’ imagery and Roger’s role within it. A modern Jewish philosophical language of messianic delay—approached here through Walter Benjamin and Franz Kafka—will later help us understand the series’ peculiar experience of time.

## II. The Monster’s Crime and Punishment, or Divine Violence

R. Dorothy Wayneright, the android heroine, comes from another anime lineage. Like the heroines of *Castle in the Sky* and *Nadia: The Secret of Blue Water*, she carries the dangerous technological inheritance of an earlier age. The people around her cannot be sure whether that inheritance will save them or destroy them, in part because they cannot fully understand what she is. *The Big O* even hints that Dorothy may be a “child of God.” In Episode 11, the elderly butler Norman remarks that her birthday falls on Christmas Day. The date does not turn her into a complete Christian allegory. It places her technological inheritance under the sign of a miraculous birth: what could destroy the world might also save it.

The series distributes its Christian signs instead of arranging them into a doctrinal scheme. It would be too easy to call Dorothy, Roger, and Big O a Trinity simply because there are three of them; the show does not map them onto Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Each instead performs a different part of the messianic drama. Dorothy bears the Christmas sign, Roger repeatedly occupies the narrative position of a savior, and Big O lends him a superhuman second body. Read against the Christian doctrine of original sin, “ye not guilty” may sound like the hoped-for acquittal of a guilt that precedes any individual act. But this remains an echo, not a creed: the words authorize a cockpit, not a sacrament.

Episodes 16 and 17 make the apocalyptic pattern explicit. Paradigm City’s believers decorate the streets for an anticipated advent and repeatedly call a world-ending force into being. The series gives them no developed theology; what defines their faith is their desire for catastrophe. They do not pray to be spared the final day. They call for its arrival, for a destruction that will wash the present world away. Then, with almost comic obedience to episodic convention, the giant monster that answers them is defeated by Roger’s robot and the apocalypse is canceled.

On screen, the monster gives nearly irresistible violence a body. It moves through the city like a natural disaster, carrying the threat of the end times. Yet no disaster is merely “natural.” Calling it that can displace responsibility. A typhoon may be unpredictable, but the scale of its devastation is entangled with where and how people build, and with the damage human beings have done to the environment over many years. Monster films magnify that entanglement. Could the original Godzilla have been born without the trauma of the atomic bomb? In this sense, monster films are always films about human beings. They take the consequences of human action, place them outside the people responsible, and give them an alien body. The monster can then hide the human agency it embodies.

Giving disaster a body also makes the crisis look solvable. No one can kill a typhoon or an earthquake. A monster, however, is alive: it can be targeted, fought, and defeated. To put the point more radically, even fighting monsters can be ideological. Killing a visible enemy stands in for confronting the conditions that produced the crisis. Once the monster falls, everyday life returns to normal, though the causes of the catastrophe remain and the narrative’s contradictions have not gone away.

We can now read the show’s recurring battle this way. Roger, the negotiator, plays a provisional Messiah; Big O serves as his larger and less obedient body. Together they strike down the embodiment of collective sin and acquit the city for another week. But the monster has its own divinity. In the original *Godzilla*, the fishing community that gives the creature its name understands it as a sea god. Overwhelming, irrational force invites worship as well as fear.

Roger and the monster occupy opposing roles, but they share the same form: both embody superhuman, quasi-divine violence. The monster turns that power against the city’s order; Roger and Big O use equally spectacular violence to restore it. Big O is as bodily and unruly as the monster. The difference lies in what their violence does: one threatens the existing world, while the other preserves it by destroying the threat.

This is where Žižek’s reading of Walter Benjamin’s “divine violence” becomes useful. For Žižek, divine violence is the irrational eruption of people trapped inside an order that recognizes no legitimate way out. Think of the bureaucratic runaround: every objection receives the same answer, “You have to follow the procedure.” When the system presents itself as perfectly rational, those it frustrates may lose any language in which the system will recognize their grievance. What remains is an outburst without a program or justification that the existing order can accept. “Divine” does not mean morally good here. It names violence that refuses to explain itself in the law’s terms and appears as a sheer negation of that law.

A monster with no goal beyond destruction gives this otherwise formless violence a symbolic body. Its violence is apocalyptic, and each time Roger defeats it, he postpones the apocalypse. At this point the essay changes theological registers. It is not claiming that Judaism as a whole teaches that the Messiah never arrives. The narrower reference is to a modern constellation of Jewish writing represented here by Benjamin and Kafka, in which redemption presses on the present without submitting to an ordinary historical timetable.

In this account, messianic waiting is not merely passive delay. A damaged and unresolved past makes a claim on the present, while the possibility of redemption lets us judge the present against a future that does not yet exist. *The Big O* turns each rescue into a rehearsal in a precise sense: the city is spared as if salvation had occurred, but its ordinary order then resumes, proving that redemption remains incomplete. Roger postpones the end and, by doing so, renews the possibility that the next deliverance might be more than another reprieve.

## III. Messianic Time

“My name is Roger Smith. I perform a much-needed job here in this city of amnesia. . . .”

Roger’s opening voice-over gives us the premise. Forty years earlier, an unexplained catastrophe robbed everyone in the world of their memories. A society without memory can nevertheless keep running so long as people retain the practical knowledge needed to operate its machinery. The survivors built Paradigm City among the ruins and resumed ordinary life. But fragments that were supposed to have been erased return like ghosts, bringing the buried past violently into the present. When one of these eruptions gets out of control, Big O rises into the story like a mechanical deus ex machina. Roger “solves” the problem by smashing the monster, meteor, or robot that has given it a body.

What these characters have lost is less memory than history. Roger makes the distinction clear. He is only in his twenties, so he cannot personally remember the catastrophe; unlike the older survivors, he did not wake to find his own past erased. Yet he has lived his entire life inside a society with no shared account of where it came from. Paradigm City has lost not only individual recollections, but also the framework that allows private memories, records, and physical remains to become a common history.

To have lost history is not the same as never having had one. The catastrophe happened; broken walls and relics of the former world remain as evidence. But the city’s residents want more than evidence. They want a complete recovered story that will explain the ruins, restore every missing connection, and make them whole. That seamless past never existed, even before the memories disappeared.

Lacan’s Thing gives us one way to describe this fantasy of completeness. The subject imagines that something essential has been taken away and that recovering it would finally close the gap within the self. The real past existed; the perfect memory that could explain everything did not. Yet this imagined missing object still organizes desire and helps produce the very subject who longs for it. *The Big O* is bold about how malleable that process can be. The past does not sit untouched, waiting to be retrieved. Action in the present continually remakes it. As the series puts it, “Imagination and memory are the same thing.”

We can therefore reverse the usual formula: history does not only create the present; the present also creates history. Consider Roger’s flashbacks. In chronological order, the remembered event comes first and his later actions respond to it. In the order experienced by Roger and the viewer, however, a present crisis comes first. It prompts the act of remembering and gives the recovered past its new meaning. Without that present event, this particular past would never appear as part of the story. The future enters too: what Roger hopes or fears will happen shapes the meaning he gives the memory, and the recovered memory changes what he can do next.

Past, present, and future thus determine one another instead of lining up as a simple chain of causes and effects. This is what the essay means by “messianic time.” In Benjamin’s terms, it interrupts the homogeneous, empty time of modern progress narratives, in which one moment replaces another as history advances in a single direction. Messianic time is not a cycle, nor does it make the future literally cause the past. It names a present charged at once by unfinished demands from the past and unrealized possibilities for the future.

But the Messiah does not come.

## IV. Late—or Already Here?

> “The Messiah will come only when he is no longer necessary; he will come only on the day after his arrival; he will come, not on the last day, but on the very last.”
>
> —Franz Kafka, “The Messiah”

Perhaps the O in Big O already contains a clue to the plot. It is a closed loop, with neither beginning nor end. At the end of the series, Paradigm City is revealed to be a fiction repeatedly staged and erased. The theatrical lights hanging over the city expose it as a literal set. At intervals, the robot Big Venus awakens, destroys the world, and resets the performance. Roger finally does what a negotiator does best: he confronts the power behind the reset and persuades it to interrupt—or at least alter—the next repetition.

Cyclical time and messianic delay both break with a simple story of historical progress, but they do not bend time in the same way. A cycle returns to its starting point and makes the past recur. Messianic delay keeps a promised future ahead of the present; what repeats is not the past itself, but the condition of waiting. Big Venus’s erasure seems to promise a new beginning, but instead it restores an existing script. It looks like absolute negation, the same refusal of the existing order that we saw in divine violence. The reset exposes the limit of that negation: instead of creating something new, it clears the old stage for another performance.

For a series that debuted in 1999, this is a distinctly fin-de-siècle Messiah: a savior defined less by arrival than by the management of apocalypse. Roger stands in for the Messiah precisely so that final transformation can keep failing to occur. Each victory keeps Paradigm City alive and renews the promise of salvation for another episode. The Messiah does not choose to postpone himself; the city and the narrative preserve their own continuity by keeping him forever on the way. But why must he always be “about to arrive”? Why can he not have “already arrived”?

Agamben’s account of messianic time offers another possibility. Messianic time is not a future age placed beyond ordinary history. It is the time remaining within the present, transformed by the way we act in it. Any moment can become messianic—but only retrospectively, once an action has revealed what that moment made possible. Kafka writes that the Messiah “will come only on the day after his arrival.” *The Big O* treats memory in much the same way: an action in the present brings the past into view, and only afterward can the event be recognized for what it was. The Messiah is no longer a person promised for the future, but a name for redemptive action in the present. That leaves a harder question. When *The Big O* reaches its ending, does its promised future actually redeem anyone? Does the world become any better?

The finale supplies no picture of a redeemed society. Big Venus walks through the city, and everything in its path vanishes. Roger negotiates; the erasure does not simply run to completion, and Paradigm City appears again. The return may take place on altered terms, but the series does not show us that the city’s institutions, relationships, or everyday life have become better. Survival and transformation remain two different things.

This is a limit that *The Big O* exposes in its own form. The series can picture the end of the world in extravagant detail, but it has only the image of a reset for what comes afterward. Roger has spent the story destroying each embodiment of crisis so that ordinary life can resume. In the finale, at least, he addresses the mechanism of repetition itself. He does not produce a purified future; he keeps the script from closing exactly as written.

A renewed world does not have to be spotless to be worth affirming, but recurrence alone is not redemption. What might make a new beginning redemptive is the possibility of acting differently within it. *The Big O* cannot guarantee that difference. It can only leave room for it.

The story continues. The Messiah remains a specter—not a savior waiting somewhere on the horizon, but the possibility that an action in the present might keep the world from ending exactly as it has before.
