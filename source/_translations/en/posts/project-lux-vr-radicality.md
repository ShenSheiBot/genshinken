On September 16, 2020, Facebook—now Meta—announced the Oculus Quest 2, its new standalone VR headset. At $299, it was priced to compete with the Nintendo Switch. The old joke that “every year is the Year of VR” seemed, at last, about to come true. VR appeared to be starting down the road to maturity, ready to become a new medium and transform the way we imagined the future. Bishōjo games made for that medium seemed to promise equally new experiences. Yet putting on the headset often brought disappointment: much of what was presented as a new experience felt strikingly old-fashioned. That disappointment forces two questions. Is VR really a new medium? And do bishōjo games in VR really offer anything new?

Or, to state these two questions more precisely:

1. Does VR necessarily entail innovation—that is, radicality?

2. Do bishōjo games in VR necessarily entail that same radicality?

This essay follows two threads—the history of how VR has been imagined and the critical history of bishōjo games—before turning to the VR bishōjo game *Project LUX* to ask whether such “radicality” is possible at all.

## Why Seek the Radical in Bishōjo Games

Before making that argument, we should set VR aside and address a prior question: why look for radicality in bishōjo games at all? The term refers here to Japanese story-driven games centered on relationships with female characters, a field that overlaps with visual novels and the adult-game market. For many players in 2020, radical innovation may have seemed beside the point. A bishōjo game could instead offer an endlessly renewed everyday life, a gentle refuge of familiar stories and character types. Historically, however, the pursuit of the radical was a real current in bishōjo games—perhaps even part of the form’s essence. We might also describe it as a self-referential movement of “criticism” that bishōjo games directed at themselves.

For players who approach the form chiefly as entertainment, “criticism” may sound remote. One bridge between the two worlds is *ErogameScape -Erogame Criticism Space-*, a Japanese database and review site for *eroge*—erotic games, a market category that substantially overlaps with bishōjo games. Although numerical scores are among the site’s most immediately visible features, its fullest expression of criticism lies in long-form reviews. That practice belongs to a Japanese tradition of literary criticism. “Critical Space” may now sound native to game culture, but the name came from *Hihyō Kūkan* (*Critical Space*), the influential critical journal associated with Kōjin Karatani and Akira Asada. It inspired names and formats elsewhere, including a “Film Critical Space” website; ErogameScape was the bishōjo-game counterpart.

The impulse toward a radical, critical engagement with bishōjo games is therefore already latent in the language players use, as well as in the form’s critical history.

## The Critical Point of Bishōjo Games

Criticism cannot be avoided in any history of bishōjo games, and Hiroki Azuma is indispensable to that history. Readers of theory may know the analyses in *Otaku: Japan’s Database Animals* and *The Birth of Game-Like Realism*.[^titles] Another major intervention was the two-volume *dōjin*—independently published—criticism series Azuma edited in the early 2000s, *The Critical Point of Bishōjo Games* (*Bishōjo Gēmu no Rinkaiten*, 美少女ゲームの臨界点).

The two volumes brought together bishōjo-game writers including Gen Urobuchi, Masaki Motonaga, and Jin Haganeya. They also published an influential account of *sekaikei*, the term that dominated criticism of otaku culture throughout the 2000s. The project fostered writers such as Satoshi Maejima and influenced Akinari Sakagami, who later wrote book-length histories of the visual-novel studios Key and TYPE-MOON. Familiar names such as Tatsuhiko Takimoto and Makoto Shinkai also appear in its production credits. This unusually dense network of writers and critics explains the zine’s later reputation within the field.

The story of the zine’s creation is equally striking. Azuma’s best-known bishōjo-game criticism may be his analysis of loop-structure games in *The Birth of Game-Like Realism*, which began as a serialization in the early-2000s light-novel magazine *Faust*. Soon after it began, Azuma had a principled disagreement with the magazine’s editor, Katsushi Ōta. The improbable fault line was fandom: Azuma favored Key, the studio behind *AIR*, while Ōta favored TYPE-MOON’s *Tsukihime*—closer, in today’s vocabulary, to a *Fate* partisan. Azuma left the magazine’s criticism section and independently produced the email magazine *Hajō Genron* (波状言論) and *The Critical Point of Bishōjo Games*. The first volume included a spectrum chart mapping the influences and relative positions of light novels and bishōjo games as Azuma understood them. The project went on to shape not only both forms, but also the criticism that surrounded them.

## The Radicality of the Critical Point

In his essay on *AIR* in *The Critical Point of Bishōjo Games*, “Before Moe: Remaining with Impotence” (萌えの手前、不能性にとどまること), Azuma sets out his criterion for bishōjo-game criticism.[^relay] He begins from a paradox. *AIR* is a *nakige*, or “crying game”—a melodramatic work designed to move its players—and neither Key’s staff nor most players need consciously intend a critical reading. Unlike works that announce their critical ambitions, such as *Urusei Yatsura 2: Beautiful Dreamer*, *Neon Genesis Evangelion*, or *Kiss the Future* (未来にキスを), written by Masaki Motonaga and released by the developer group styled “otherwise,” *AIR* remains a successful commodity shaped by the logic of consumption.

For Azuma, however, *critical* need not mean censuring a work or crediting its author with an explicit theory. It describes a creative act that pushes a genre’s possibilities to a limit and, in doing so, exposes the genre’s own conditions and constraints. A reading of *AIR* can therefore reveal something latent in the bishōjo game as a form, independently of authorial intention or the ordinary experience of play. That is the sense in which Azuma calls it critical.

Azuma’s critical-point theory performs exactly this operation: it pushes the possibilities of the bishōjo game to their limit, exposing the medium’s essence. His account of loop-structure games in *The Birth of Game-Like Realism*, with its close attention to the game’s “system”—the architecture of the medium—is another application of the same criterion. To pursue critical radicality in VR bishōjo games is likewise to push VR, and the genre built within it, to their limits so that their essence and architecture come into view.

## The Radicality of Cyberspace

Let us return to our opening question: does VR necessarily entail radicality? Put differently, what would radicality mean for VR in the first place? Although the consumer VR industry is recent, the social and literary imagination surrounding VR is much older. The quickest route into that history of the imagination is the word “cyberspace.”

“Cyberspace” is familiar, yet it is far more metaphorical than “the net.” The communications infrastructure beneath cyberspace has the form implied by *net*: not a place, but a traffic network of fiber and cable. In “Why Is Cyberspace Called Such?” Azuma argues that cyberspace became detached from this mesh and imagined as a place because of the techno-utopian hopes invested in the internet by what would later be called the Californian Ideology, rooted in the US counterculture of the 1960s. The unlikely marriage of communications technology—infrastructure built on the scale of the nation-state—with left-wing thought promised a new continent of freedom outside physical space. John Perry Barlow’s 1996 “A Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace,” modeled on the American Declaration of Independence, exemplifies that dream. Cyberpunk was deeply shaped by the same current. In its literary imagination, capital and the system control individuals through cables, traffic, and buildings: infrastructure. Resistance finds its base in a phantasmal cyberspace. These opposing representations of the same technology reveal the ideology’s simultaneous radicalism and conservatism.

In the author’s account, the hope that the net would become a base for a great left-wing counteroffensive proved naive. When Azuma serialized the essay in 1997, before the internet became ubiquitous, that judgment still required philosophical argument. By 2020, online nationalist mobilization and the extraction of data and labor had made the distance between the utopian promise and platform reality much harder to ignore.

## The Short-Circuited Symbolic and Radicality

Even after the rapid growth of the VR industry, people had not outgrown this naive image of cyberspace. In 2020, Facebook’s requirement that VR users log in with a Facebook account drew widespread criticism from players and the industry. One strand of that criticism still rehearsed the old story of freedom under threat and so, in the author’s view, remained trapped in the Californian Ideology described above. The immediate reference was “Delivery Riders, Trapped in the System,” an investigation published by the Chinese magazine *Renwu* on September 8, 2020. It described delivery platforms steadily compressing allotted times and using algorithmic dispatch to push riders toward dangerous work.[^riders] The example made platform control concrete: the “system” was not merely a metaphor for lost online liberty but an apparatus organizing labor and risk. Does that leave no radical innovation for cyberspace—or for theories of VR and the VR bishōjo game? The author’s answer is no. Radicality may lie not in the old utopian promise, but in what an unfinished medium exposes through its failures.

What does that mean? Return once more to the imagination of cyberspace. Psychologist Sherry Turkle[^1] and media theorist Allucquère Rosanne Stone[^2] argued that, in the coming age of electronic media, switching between windows would let people move among personas. This plural, persona-switching structure, they suggested, could deconstruct the Cartesian subject and give rise to a postmodern one. Žižek challenges that conclusion: freely moving among personas still implies a subject-position behind them, an empty place able to assume any mask.[^3] On that account, cyberspace’s plural personas do not abolish the unified subject; they reproduce its structure of desire at another level. Azuma nevertheless finds, within Turkle and Stone’s analysis, a radical possibility that Lacanian psychoanalysis had overlooked.

Azuma found in these accounts a peculiar logic of the surface. MUDs (multi-user dungeons) were shared, largely text-based online worlds in which players acted through written characters. A player might therefore feel personally violated when that textual character was violated. In online chat, one might provisionally accept another participant’s claim to be a woman when she wrote in feminine language, without asking what lay behind the screen. Such cases express the information age’s indifference to depth.

In psychoanalytic discourse, surface and depth form one another dialectically. In an account of cinema, for example, the spectator identifies both with the visible character on the screen—Imaginary identification—and with the apparatus outside it, such as the camera, director, or social gaze—Symbolic identification. Here the **big Other** means the impersonal order of language, law, and social recognition that makes positions intelligible. The subject’s desire takes shape between the visible image and that invisible order. In cyberspace, however, depth no longer commands the same interest. Psychoanalysis expects this weakening of the big Other to return in a displaced form. Žižek, for instance, argues that the missing authority merely invites the voluntary return of another “master” called cyberspace.

Azuma accepts the need for supplementation but argues that the information age does not simply restore the Symbolic in its old form. Signs, rules, and social positions are increasingly presented as manipulable images on the interface. He calls this the Imaginarization of the Symbolic. The **Real**, in the Lacanian vocabulary used here, is whatever cannot be fully organized by image or language but returns as a limit or disruption. The proposed “short circuit” is therefore not a proven electronic pathway from screen image to the Real; it names a theoretical claim that, when the old Symbolic depth is flattened into the interface, its unresolved limits return on that same surface. Azuma’s account of *sekaikei* follows this pattern: intimate relations are coupled directly to world-scale questions of survival and destruction, without the usual social institutions mediating between them.[^relay]

Rendering the Symbolic as Imaginary—turning depth into surface—produces a total surface, an “ultra-surface” or hyperplane. Azuma calls the mode of subjectivity born on that plane the **interface subject**.

## The Radicality of the Interface Subject

The critical stance toward Lacanian psychoanalysis in Azuma’s interface subject derives from his reading of Derrida. In Azuma’s view, psychoanalysis is broadly a product of the twentieth-century cinematic apparatus. The move from cinema’s model of viewing to the information age’s cyberspace flattens “seeing and being seen,” “the visible and the invisible,” and “image and sign” onto the same surface. Azuma invokes Derrida’s **writing** (*écriture*) for this unstable play of marks that can operate as both perceivable form and repeatable sign; the term is doing theoretical work here, not naming ordinary alphabetic writing.

Previously, the image was treated as visible while the sign—the Symbolic order—remained an invisible depth. The interface presents both as operations on a perceivable surface. Azuma’s next step is to shift the distinction away from *what kind of thing* image and sign are and toward *how a mark is encountered*: seen, heard, or felt. His phrase “the space between the eyes and the ears,” borrowed from Derrida, points to that interval among channels rather than to a literal anatomical space.[^relay] The interface subject is distributed across these different encounters with the same field of signs. Azuma links this distributed perception to Freud’s uncanny.

Freud’s uncanny is not fear of the wholly foreign. It arises when something intimate and familiar returns in estranged form; dolls and doubles are familiar examples. Following Azuma’s account as relayed by the archived article, the author offers another illustration: you begin thinking of someone you would rather avoid, only for that person to appear. The proposed explanation is that unconscious recognition occurred before conscious recognition, producing an eerie lag. This causal gloss belongs to the article’s reconstruction of Azuma, not to a located passage in Freud’s essay.[^freud]

In Azuma’s interpretation, this lag joins the interval among sensory channels in a media theory of uneven processing. Three distinct phenomena must remain separate. **Latency** is a measurable delay within a technical system. **Uneven simulation** means that one modality may be richly represented while another is absent—for example, responsive head tracking without touch on the back. **Sensory conflict** occurs when channels deliver incompatible information, as in visually perceived movement while the vestibular system reports stillness. The essay gathers all three under Azuma’s philosophical language of different “speeds,” but its argument about *Project LUX* depends chiefly on uneven simulation and sensory conflict, not on a claim that every difference is literal latency. Once vision loses its privilege, those mismatches can unsettle the stable viewing position presupposed by the cinematic model.

## Half-Dive VR and the Scrambling of the Senses

At the time of writing, Facebook’s $299 Quest 2 suggested that the VR industry was maturing, but not that the technology itself had matured. Two familiar problems were motion sickness and the gap between the demands of full visual immersion and available graphics performance.

VR motion sickness is commonly explained through sensory conflict: vision reports a moving viewpoint while the kinesthetic and vestibular senses report a body at rest. The result resembles carsickness or simulator sickness. Many VR games try to reduce it through teleport locomotion or by sharply narrowing the field of view during movement. VR aims at total sensory immersion, yet may need to restrain visual motion to prevent sickness. For the author, this paradox made motion sickness the greatest obstacle on VR’s road to maturity.

Yet precisely because VR sits between maturity and immaturity—in what I will call **half-dive**—the radicality we seek becomes possible. Vision can persuade consciousness that the viewpoint is moving, while the body’s other sensory systems report no corresponding motion. The author reads motion sickness through Azuma’s broader history of a visual culture that subordinates other forms of perception. Motion sickness does not by itself prove that history; it is the physiological conflict through which the essay chooses to make that theoretical history perceptible.

The author’s 2020 account of the performance gap begins with three demanding targets for full visual immersion: a horizontal field of view approaching 200 degrees, roughly 8K resolution for each eye at headset viewing distance, and at least 90 frames per second to reduce strain and dizziness. The archived source then sets this idealized dual-view VR workload beside Nvidia’s claim that the RTX 3090 could run many conventional games at 7680 × 4320 and 60 fps with DLSS.[^ampere] These are not like-for-like benchmarks: one is a hypothetical two-eye VR target at 90 fps, the other a single 8K output reconstructed with DLSS at 60 fps. The comparison can indicate the scale of the challenge in 2020, but it cannot quantify the gap.

Foveated rendering offered a workaround. Human vision spans a broad field, but high visual acuity is concentrated around the point of fixation and falls off toward the periphery. The source describes the high-acuity region as roughly 30 degrees and gives an idealized rendering-load estimate of 16 percent, but preserves neither a derivation nor a technical citation; those numbers should be read as the author’s working premises, not portable performance claims. Fixed foveated rendering lowers detail away from the center of the display, while gaze-contingent rendering requires eye tracking to move the high-resolution region with the eye. In 2020, the author judged eye tracking neither affordable nor supported by a broad software ecosystem. Current Meta documentation illustrates why the distinction still matters rather than simply making the old claim universal: Quest Pro supports eye-tracked foveation, whereas Quest 3 and 3S have no eye-tracking hardware.[^foveation] These figures document the essay’s historical technical premise rather than universal limits of present-day hardware.

For the author, this graphics gap meant that affordable headsets addressed only the “conscious” center of vision, not the “unconscious” periphery, even as head tracking had already become highly responsive. The result was a **conflict between continuity in kinesthetic perception and discontinuity in vision**. Visual change follows conscious head movement and produces immersion, yet the narrow, periscope-like field violates the same perceptual structure. The author calls the resulting experience an **objectification of the gaze**: one’s own vision begins to feel like a camera operated by an inept cinematographer, making the player mock their own “camerawork”—their own looking. This resembles the uncanny. Vision is one of the subject’s most intimate senses, yet the headset externalizes it as an alien object.

This objectification of the gaze most closely fits the interface subject. The next step, however, is this essay’s extension of Azuma rather than a claim Azuma himself makes about VR. What it treats as **writing across channels** is the headset’s repeatable inscription of one represented situation in several forms: a rendered scene, spatialized sound, head-position data, and—where a work supports them—tracked-controller poses. Vestibular stillness, a narrow field of view, or missing haptics are not themselves Derridean writing. They are mismatches and omissions in how that situation is returned to the player. The author’s analogy is that, when one encounter is encoded visually and spatially but not tactilely, no single sensory channel can secure the player’s position as wholly inside or outside the represented scene. That uneven inscription, rather than mere technical latency, is what the essay carries from Azuma’s interface subject into VR.

This gives us a provisional answer. As an ideal inherited from the imagination of cyberspace, VR replays the Californian Ideology and has no radicality of its own. Existing VR, however, had not yet achieved that ideal: it remained half-dive. In the author’s Azuma-inspired account, that condition also exposes the priority visual culture grants to sight over other senses. Rich visual simulation, absent touch, limited peripheral coverage, and incompatible vestibular cues are not one technical problem, but their unevenness can make the player’s perceptual position unstable. These limitations may eventually be solved. While they remain, the medium exposes a relation between subject-formation and an era’s **episteme**—the historically specific framework that determines what counts as knowledge and which forms of perception receive authority. This is the point of Azuma’s claim that psychoanalysis was a product of the cinematic apparatus.

## Project LUX

Let us turn, then, to this essay's final task: analyzing how a VR bishōjo game can embody radicality in its own expression.

*Project LUX* was the first VR work from Spicy Tails, the independent studio led by light-novel author Isuna Hasekura. The archived article dates it to 2017, while Steam’s current listing gives February 2, 2018 as its full release date.[^release] Hasekura had previously produced the *WORLD END ECONOMiCA* series, well known in independent visual-novel circles. By 2020, *Project LUX* could already feel dated: its graphics were limited, and its interaction design centered on 6DoF head movement and gaze rather than tracked, embodied hands. The current Steam page does list “Tracked Controller Support,” so the source’s categorical statement that the work had “no controller tracking” cannot stand as a platform-wide hardware claim.[^controllers] What the archived play account consistently describes—and what the later argument needs—is the absence of tracked-hand interaction and tactile response within the scene. The sparse interaction led many VR players to call the work “VR anime” rather than a “VR game.” The official English description calls it a “VR animated story,” while the Japanese site presents it as Spicy Tails’ first “VR animation.” That unstable boundary is hardly new to bishōjo-game players, who are accustomed to works that blur game and novel. It corresponds to the “games like novels” and “novels like games” discussed in *The Birth of Game-Like Realism*.

More important than the quality of its writing is the way *Project LUX* turns those technical limitations into the basis of its scenario. In this sense, it is a work of game-like realism in the fullest sense.

## The Objectification of the Gaze in Project LUX

The story runs as follows. In the future, most of humanity lives in cyberspace. Cyberbrains allow people to share memories and even personalities. The young artist Lux alone refuses cyberization and lives in her biological body, in a cottage on a deserted shore. As cyber technology blurs individual boundaries, the creative capacity of people in cyberspace atrophies, making Lux an ideal producer of cultural goods for them to consume. To reach her and commission a work, they dispatch the protagonist: an agent who enters a prosthetic body, visits Lux in the physical world, and delivers the request.

Most of the game consists of Lux and the agent talking about everything under the sun and trading flirtatious banter. In that respect, *Project LUX* resembles a conventional slice-of-life bishōjo game. Yet the player is told from the outset that this intimacy will end in tragedy.

The game opens not by placing us directly in the agent’s sweet routine with Lux, but at a trial over her death. The agent is the defendant; the player is a juror using cyber technology to relive his memories and determine what happened. That reliving plainly mirrors the VR headset on the player’s own head. The game thus gives the player two roles at once, in a metafictional structure that also serves as a metaphor for VR: the agent flirting with Lux, and the juror silently reliving it all.

At first glance, the structure resembles the psychoanalytic dialectic: inhabiting the agent is Imaginary identification with the visible character, while inhabiting the juror is Symbolic identification with an invisible gaze. As the reliving proceeds, however, that boundary blurs because VR makes the scene spatially embodied. *Project LUX* provides no tracked-hand embodiment or tactile response in these encounters, but it does have free 6DoF head tracking, and its scenes with Lux are choreographed around movements of the head. The player selects topics by looking, turns to locate Lux’s voice in spatial audio, and even leans into her outstretched hand. These actions do more than make the agent’s experience vivid: the player’s own head and gaze become inputs that perform the agent’s responses, even while the trial frame defines the same player as a juror reviewing those responses after the fact. One bodily action is thus assigned to both narrative positions. That double ownership makes it harder to preserve the juror’s abstract distance from the agent’s embodied encounter.

## The Scrambling Between Two Identifications

This collapse of the juror’s critical distance—the objectification of the gaze and scrambling of the dialectical subject—intensifies as the case unravels. The people who commissioned Lux belong to one side of an otherwise unnamed conflict within cyberspace. They intend to harvest from her art the energy of individual passion and turn it into a weapon. The agent was not only a commissioner: through shared memory and reliving, he was also an instrument for surveilling Lux on their behalf. The archived plot summary does not identify the opposing camps in greater detail; what matters for this argument is that either side’s victory would lead to Lux’s forced cyberization. She would become electronic livestock, endlessly milked for creative energy. Electronic copying would destroy her singularity, distributing her self for others to share and relive. Rather than lose what makes her irreplaceably herself, she decides to let death inscribe that singularity into history.

Lux chooses suicide. Before shooting herself, she embraces the agent—and therefore you, the player. What she finally cannot accept is not only the destruction of her own singularity, but the destruction of the singularity of her memories of meeting you, of a relationship that might be called love. Those memories are unique and unrepeatable; once the agent returns to cyberspace, however, others will inevitably share them, **experiencing them as if they were their own.**

Before she dies, Lux says in substance that she cannot bear the thought of other people looking into a feeling that belongs only to the two of them.[^relay] Her objection to shared memory therefore turns back on the player, who is doing exactly that as a juror. The scene forces a question on every player moved to tears: **you who are crying—are you the agent, or the juror?**

The structure is ingenious. First, it places the player on a meta-level as a juror coolly observing Lux and the agent. Then, through interactions not reducible to the flat image—head movement, positional audio, and bodily orientation—and through Lux’s flirtation, it draws the player into an intimate everyday life. The juror’s task recedes and the player slips down into the agent at the object level. Once that intimacy takes hold, the story turns tragic, deepening the player’s emotional bond with Lux while self-referential dialogue recalls the juror’s task. Between ordinary physical closeness and tragic love, the player **cannot decide whether the emotions welling up belong to the agent or the juror.** The juror does not cause Lux’s already-recorded death. The ethical implication is different: by reliving and judging the memory, the juror participates in the very system of access to private experience that Lux fears.

The self-reference lies in the game’s making the player review a memory about the danger of memories being reviewed. Lux’s objection addresses the juror’s act at the same time that her intimacy addresses the agent. The retrospective frame and the embodied scene therefore cease to form cleanly separated levels: both positions bear on the same feeling. That overlap gives form to the radical character this essay finds in VR.

## The Narrative Trick in Project LUX

*Project LUX* does not stop there. The player makes conversational topic selections earlier, but the suicide scene contains the work’s sole route-determining choice: attempting to stop Lux unlocks a hidden ending in which, after the trial, the agent returns to his long-frozen biological body and reunites with the “revived” Lux in the seaside cottage. The choice functions as a route condition that grants the player a later revelation; the archived source does not claim that the juror literally changes the already-recorded past.

The Lux in the suicide scene was a prosthetic body operated remotely by the real Lux. Although she resisted cyberization, life alone in a biological body had become too lonely, so she had prepared the prosthetic body required for cyberization without taking the final step. The agent learns of her substitution without exposing it through shared memory because, while embracing him in the suicide scene, Lux writes the plan on his back. The form of memory sharing presented by the game—and thus by VR—does not include, within touch, the experience of **being touched**.

*Project LUX* thus turns VR’s immaturity into a narrative trick. Head tracking, gaze selection, and spatial audio produce embodied spatial immersion without tracked hands or tactile response; on hardware and builds that accept tracked controllers, controller input still does not supply the missing touch on the player’s back. This half-dive state creates a blind spot around being touched—and makes possible a device particular to VR.

## The Asymmetry of Touching and Being Touched

The trick is particular to VR because it depends on an asymmetry between touching and being touched. Yet *Project LUX* offers no tracked-hand embodiment or haptic feedback in this encounter, so where does its “touch” come from? The touch at issue is not vibration. Just as ASMR can evoke a tactile sensation through sound, another sense can induce touch: here, a **visually induced touch**.

Return, then, to VR and Azuma. More than a decade after “Why Is Cyberspace Called Such?”, Azuma reinterpreted its argument as a theory of the touch panel. At its center is the inseparability of vision and touch, a property he calls **tactile visuality** (*shokushisei*, 触視性). Azuma notes that early proponents of the graphical user interface (GUI) often described it as a way to “touch” the computer’s core—its code. Code has no body and cannot literally be touched, but the metaphor reveals how vision and touch supplement one another. Here the logic of the interface subject returns. Invisible depth—code, the Symbolic—is flattened into visible surface—image, the Imaginary—so code can be manipulated intuitively. Dragging a desktop file into a trash icon, for example, presents a filesystem operation as a visible object moved by a gesture; it is not a literal picture of any one shell command. Mouse and keyboard made this tactile visuality less conspicuous; the touch screen embodies it directly. Touch, for users raised on such screens, need not require vibrating feedback. Vision can complete the sensation.

VR is absent from Azuma’s theory because he regards it as the culmination of visual culture. On this essay’s account, however, VR scrambles several modes of perception and therefore embodies tactile visuality even more fully than the touch screen. By 2020, Oculus had implemented camera-based hand tracking on the $299 Quest 2, and compatible applications were beginning to appear. The experience of **“touching” in midair**—without even the physical panel required by a phone or tablet—shows how touch can be induced visually. By the same principle, the relevant *Project LUX* scenes do not require tracked hands: leaning into Lux’s hand through head tracking, moving close to her rendered body through positional tracking, and “touching” in midair all depend on vision’s completion of touch.

Yet while *Project LUX* can produce a kind of touching, the VR systems in view here cannot reproduce touch on the player’s back—the sensation on which the narrative trick depends. Haptic suits may eventually do so. The point is how the work reflects and exposes the medium’s immaturity. Visually induced touch arrives before the medium can reproduce being touched; that imbalance makes the technology feel more complete than it is. The game can depict the agent embracing Lux, but it cannot transmit Lux’s writing on the agent’s back to the player. This asymmetry—this **division of tactile processing across channels**—is where *Project LUX* embodies VR’s radicality.

## The Critical Point of the VR Bishōjo Game

We can now answer this essay’s final question. Are bishōjo games in VR inherently radical?

VR remained in an immature state of half-dive, and *Project LUX*—an early work by an independent studio with limited technical resources—was more rudimentary still. Yet it is acutely aware of both the medium’s condition and its own limitations. It turns those limits into metafiction and narrative trickery, satisfying the player’s desire for closeness with Lux while throwing the player’s subjectivity into disarray. This intricate and radical structure exposes the essence and limits of the VR bishōjo game as a genre. It also brings to the surface a VR-specific, asymmetrical tactile visuality that Azuma’s own theory did not address.

Taken together, these features justify reading *Project LUX* as a critical point in the history of bishōjo-game criticism and as an early test case for VR-game criticism. The title toward which this essay has been moving is therefore not simply praise, but a description of method: *Project LUX* pushes the VR bishōjo game far enough for the genre’s limits to become visible.
