*Flavors of Youth* is a Sino-Japanese animated anthology released in August 2018. One reason it seemed so promising was that **Japan’s CoMix Wave Films**, which had worked on several of Makoto Shinkai’s films, was handling the film’s mid-stage production. The company had long dazzled viewers with its meticulous background art and its ability to re-create real places. The trailer looked gorgeous. I, at least, had high hopes.

![Shanghai cityscape in *Flavors of Youth*](attachments/roof-archive/cv1530117/01-shanghai-skyline.jpg)

The film consists of three stand-alone segments. “The Rice Noodles” follows Xiao Ming, now living in Beijing, as food draws him back into memories of his childhood in Hunan and of the grandmother who raised him. “A Little Fashion Show,” set in Guangzhou, centers on the model Yi Lin and her younger sister, Lulu. In Li Haoling’s “Love in Shanghai,” Li Mo remembers Xiao Yu, their friend Pan, and the shikumen neighborhood where they grew up. Judged only by their plots, I would rank the three stories on something close to an exponential curve. The box-office data and Douban rating also made the wider public’s lack of enthusiasm plain. Yuan Lei’s headline for *The Beijing News*—[“*Flavors of Youth*: Neither Fish nor Fowl—a Sino-Japanese Co-production’s Vision of Youth”](https://www.chinawriter.com.cn/n1/2018/0806/c404076-30210227.html)—sums it up rather neatly. The film never solved the problem of how to tell Chinese stories well while speaking to a Chinese audience.

My argument is simple: physical spaces make relationships possible; remembered relationships turn those spaces into landscapes; and animation tries, with uneven success, to make such landscapes recognizable to people who never lived in them.

## 1. The Modern Concept of Romantic Love

Because the film tells stories of young love, I want to begin not with the animation but with the modern Chinese idea of romance, and with the spaces that allow a social life to take shape.

By *lian’ai* (恋爱), I do not mean love as a private feeling. I mean dating as both a practice and a publicly intelligible relationship. Once such a relationship exists, “lovers” exist too: the couple can understand themselves, and be understood by others, as people who occupy a particular social position. In premodern China, at least within the mainstream forms of legitimate relations between men and women, that category did not exist. **Between a young woman “waiting in the boudoir to be married” and becoming a wife, there was no generally recognized place for the relationship we now call dating.**

![An unidentified illustrated scene from a classical romance, with a scholar and two women meeting indoors](attachments/roof-archive/cv1530117/02-classical-romance-scene.jpg)

Of course, we cannot know whether “love” passed between a poor scholar on his way to the imperial examinations and a young woman who spent one passionate night with him. We may reasonably suppose that people in different periods shared certain forms of affection. But feeling is not the same thing as an available social relationship. The **practice of dating familiar to us today is modern**. It is difficult to imagine a man in premodern China introducing the woman beside him by saying, “This is my girlfriend.”

Around the turn of the twentieth century, Shanghai’s “progressive women” helped bring romantic relationships into public recognition and advocate for their legitimacy. Many forces drove that change. At the level of ideas, Western influence plainly mattered. But ideas did not work alone. A quieter transformation in **physical space—including the institutions that organized who could meet whom—did just as much.**

[fig] One institutional example: [Fudan University emerged in 1905 after Ma Xiangbo and a group of students broke with Aurora Academy](https://www.fudan.edu.cn/2019/0426/c427a96140/page.htm), also known by its French name, Université l’Aurore. [Fudan began admitting women in 1927; its first female cohort included Yan Youyun (Juliana Young Koo)](https://alumni.fudan.edu.cn/3c/11/c14600a146449/page.htm), who later married the diplomat V. K. Wellington Koo. Coeducation placed young men and women within the same legitimate social space.

![Commemorative medal of Université l’Aurore, the institution also known as Aurora Academy](attachments/roof-archive/cv1530117/03-universite-laurore-medal.jpg "=33%")

It is no accident that “free love” was so often associated with students. Modern Shanghai acquired many new-style schools, while parks and inns opened in both the foreign concessions and Chinese-administered districts. Schools and parks gave young men and women legitimate places to spend time together; inns gave their relationships somewhere else to unfold. The shared identity of “student” brought young people of similar ages into sustained contact over several years. Perhaps, then, “puppy love” was not a late corruption of romance but its earliest modern form. (Laughs.)

Once a custom becomes widespread, what first looked startlingly new begins to seem timeless. That is why I find so many romantic plots in historical films and television dramas hard to watch. They take the modern sequence—“feel an attraction → date and fall in love (repeat as necessary) → marry and start a family”—and project it backward as though people in antiquity had followed it all along. This is romantic fantasy masquerading as historical continuity. Instead of searching for some hazy sameness between ancient and modern life, we should ask what conditions allowed a particular feeling or behavior to become possible. The more I think about social life, the more sharply I feel how physical space limits even our conceptual imagination.

![Shikumen residential space in “Love in Shanghai”](attachments/roof-archive/cv1530117/04-shikumen-courtyard.jpg)

Li Haoling, director of the third segment, “Love in Shanghai,” has said that his feelings about first love resembled his feelings about the shikumen, the traditional Shanghai lane-house neighborhood where he once lived. In those cramped homes and crowded lanes, neighbors gathered to talk and laugh and came to feel almost like family. He had assumed that this “family” would always remain together and look after one another. Instead, people gradually moved away into new apartments. First love, as he saw it, works in much the same way: it brings people close and then lets them drift apart.

The bond among the segment’s three characters is not just a school friendship or a romance that happens to grow out of one. It belongs to the shikumen itself: **a distinctive space now unfamiliar to many people, and to the intimate neighborhood relationships that space sustained**. Contemporary urban discourse often reduces such familiarity to the petty calculation and meddling associated with old neighborhood life. From the 1990s onward, however, the spatial form and the relationships bound up with it gradually disappeared, taking their story with them.

As cramped traditional homes gave way to spacious new apartments, the social ties among city dwellers thinned out as well. Li wanted **to render shikumen architecture in animation so that viewers could feel the warm, crowded mixture of lives that once filled those spaces and now survives only in memory.** A relationship made possible by a place had turned, in recollection, into a landscape.

## 2. The Discovery and Reproduction of Landscape

For this film, “telling Chinese stories well” might mean giving artistic form to lives Chinese viewers recognize as their own, especially the lives of their youth. The three segments vary in narrative force, but all draw on ordinary work and daily routines: Xiao Ming’s memories stretch between Hunan and Beijing; Yi Lin and Lulu inhabit Guangzhou’s fashion world; Li Mo’s recollections belong to Shanghai’s shikumen. Each offers some version of a Chinese everyday. Why, then, did so many Chinese viewers say in the *danmu*—comments scrolling across online video—and in reviews that they felt nothing? Had the film reproduced a shared everyday, or only three local ones?

![Two men seen through a rain-streaked car window in *Flavors of Youth*](attachments/roof-archive/cv1530117/05-urban-anime-scene.jpg)

But that question hides a larger one: what counts as a shared “everyday”? In *Origins of Modern Japanese Literature*, Kōjin Karatani describes the “discovery of landscape.” To paraphrase his argument, **landscape is not simply given in advance. Writers discover it—or, put another way, produce it through signification.** Landscape does not just sit there waiting to be recorded; a perceiving subject has to bring it into view. A place becomes a landscape only after someone teaches us how to see it.

Japanese youth offers an obvious example. Trains, attractive school uniforms, school festivals, and similar motifs form a rich but instantly recognizable landscape of everyday life. For those of us who encounter Japanese youth from outside Japan, that repertoire owes much to the makers of anime, manga, games, and related media. They did not merely record a landscape; they helped discover it.

[fig] The colorful school-day landscape of *Hyouka*—hardly a picture of “gray” high-school life.

![High-school students in *Hyouka*](attachments/roof-archive/cv1530117/06-gray-high-school-life.jpg)

Sometimes the image does more than produce a “landscape” in the mind. **It can feed back into the way people build and present the place itself.** Consider Hollywood’s symbolic construction of Vienna. On this account, the films did not merely teach international audiences to see Vienna through Hollywood’s eyes; decades later, Vienna had come to resemble more closely the city those films imagined. A landscape supplied from outside can become a form of cultural hegemony.

Deliberate acts of representation are only part of the story. The relative uniformity of the physical settings of Japanese social life may be a precondition for this shared landscape. From Kyushu to Hokkaido, in large metropolises and smaller cities alike, Japanese people of the same age pass through broadly similar scenes. Everyday life changes from one generation to the next, but not so radically as to make one generation unrecognizable to another. **This makes it possible to generalize an image of “everyday life” or “youth” that resonates widely.** Even more distinctive groups receive works devoted to their particular routines. *Tamako Market*, for instance, turns life in a *shōtengai*, a neighborhood shopping street, into its own landscape. That local everyday has joined the larger constellation of signs called “Japanese everyday life.”

![Residents of the shopping street in *Tamako Market*](attachments/roof-archive/cv1530117/07-tamako-market.jpg)

For Chinese viewers and creators, the corresponding question is much harder: what is “the everyday life of Chinese youth”? Is there a landscape shared by students at elite Shanghai High School, students at the famously exam-driven Hengshui High School, and students at a typical school in a less prominent regional city? Is the distance between the youthful memories of someone from Beijing and someone from Sichuan as vast as the physical distance between the two places?

**The problem may not be any poverty of imagination among cultural producers**, and there is no need to wring our hands over a defective “education system.” As a field of shared experience, China may simply remain “fragmented.” At the time this essay was written, subways and light rail had yet to become ubiquitous across Chinese cities, while milk tea and coffee were still minority habits. There were shared landscapes, of course. The baggy, tracksuit-like school uniform was instantly recognizable, as was the nationwide ordeal of cramming for the *gaokao*, the college entrance examination. But recognition did not make either image easy to romanticize. The uniform seemed too drab for the grand stage; the *gaokao* seemed too brutal for nostalgic youth drama. For many ordinary high-school students, romance remained hormonal longing rather than a socially possible relationship. Audiences could recognize these signs without agreeing that they deserved to be idealized, and creators hesitated to build a common everyday out of them. Everyday life was abundantly present, but as a shared landscape it was stillborn.

Under such conditions, asking one animated film to represent “the everyday life of Chinese youth”—much less **to create cross-cultural recognition through longing and a shared Asian sensibility, as the interviews suggest—may be impossible.** The difficulty is already visible in the film’s structure: three independent stories by three directors from different regions, each telling a story set in a different part of China. Reconciling diversity and unity within the film is hard enough. Overcoming the fragmentation of audiences, social landscapes, and ideas beyond it is harder still.

The Japanese team worked painstakingly, “making repeated location trips to China and striving for perfection in every detail, from the layout of spaces and the way buildings should be drawn to the growth patterns of plants… To convey how delicious the rice noodles really were, they would eat thirty or forty bowls in a single day.” The passage is reproduced in [Donghua Xueshupa’s behind-the-scenes interview](https://www.sohu.com/a/246023205_482993). Yet seriousness and craft could not by themselves produce a single representative landscape of Chinese youth. The decisive constraints lay outside the film. It is understandable that many Chinese viewers found *Flavors of Youth* unequal to the task.

![Shikumen street in “Love in Shanghai”](attachments/roof-archive/cv1530117/08-shikumen-street.jpg)

Let me stop before this sounds like an accusation. It is not. I mean only to point to the enormous differences in lived experience across Chinese generations and regions. Those differences leave any cultural work addressed to “everyone,” or even to young people as a whole, in a predicament. Chinese viewers and creators still struggle to name the everyday they possess and might wish to reproduce. In animation about ordinary life, we have grown used to standing outside the shop window and admiring Japanese-style imports. But within this “fragmented”—or simply diverse and differentiated—world of experience, can Chinese creators discover a landscape of their own, an everyday life of Chinese high-school students that Chinese audiences will recognize together? We shall see.
