## It Showed Me What the Job of “Direction” Actually Is

**—The final title is episode 8 of *Futari wa Precure* (hereafter *Precure*).**

**Furukawa:** It’s a legendary episode. After I graduated from university, when I was just drifting around, I decided, “I’m going to work in anime,” and as part of that I thought I should go back and rewatch Toei’s animation. At just the right time I heard that a new morning anime was starting, so I began watching *Precure*, but at first I watched it pretty casually. Things like, “The drawing style feels kind of nostalgic,” or, “Wow, this is an anime where the girls really do throw punches and kicks.” I didn’t have any sisters, so I had never properly watched *Sailor Moon*. Maybe because of that, those were about the extent of my reactions. I kept half-absentmindedly watching every week, and then, with episode 8, it suddenly felt as if a special episode had begun.

**—What made it feel special?**

**Furukawa:** It was easy to watch, and the characters’ emotions came straight through. I don’t mean to be rude, but in the first seven episodes, all I had really taken in was the plot. Episode 8 was directed by Takuya Igarashi, and for me—someone who only vaguely felt that I wanted to work in episode direction or become a director—it was as though the screen itself properly showed me what kind of job “direction” actually was. With Anno, Ikuhara, and Oshii, I could sense that there was a creator on the other side of the screen, but I couldn’t imagine what their work actually consisted of. Somewhere in the back of my mind I thought things somehow worked out through some mystical, almost miraculous power possessed by the auteur. (laughs) But after watching episode 8 of *Precure*, I clearly felt that there was concrete work being done behind the screen. I became distinctly aware of the concrete techniques and actions of a professional episode director. …So, after watching episode 8, what do you think was the first thing I did? Take a guess!

**—What, it’s a quiz!? …Did you print out every shot with a video printer?**

**Furukawa:** Wrong. The answer is: “I paused the video recording and reconstructed the storyboard one shot at a time.” My knowledge of storyboards was still pretty hazy then, and I had to look things up as I went, so I think it took about a week. The strange thing is that, around the same time, I watched episodes of *Digimon Adventure* directed by Mamoru Hosoda and thought they were amazing too, but they didn’t make me want to reconstruct the storyboards. I love Hosoda’s work, and I’ve bought every one of his storyboard books, but something was different. It’s hard to put into words, but Igarashi’s episode 8 of *Precure* stays, fundamentally, inside the framework of the *Precure* series. Hosoda’s *Digimon* had already spilled outside it.

**—Whether it’s *Digimon* or *Ojamajo Doremi*, Hosoda’s episodes are wonderful, but they depart from the shared texture of the series and turn completely into “a Mamoru Hosoda film.”**

**Furukawa:** Exactly. That’s a wonderful thing, but … by my standards, that’s something a movie is allowed to do. That’s why I love *Digimon Adventure: Our War Game!* It connects to what I said about *Saint Seiya: Legend of Crimson Youth*: a film can put the director’s signature as an auteur out front, but I think an episode of a TV series should remain, first and foremost, one episode within that series. What makes Igarashi’s episode 8 of *Precure* so good is that, in that sense, it serves the work. This is just my own arbitrary opinion, but without that episode 8, I don’t think *Precure* would have become a series that continued for more than ten years. What is so good about Igarashi’s direction is a kind of clean restraint: he has a strong self, but he doesn’t push that self too hard. The way he stands one step back from the work is wonderful. And yet whatever he makes is unmistakably “a Takuya Igarashi film,” while still being easy to watch. I think that is extremely important. Though I’m sure plenty of people will be thinking, “Huh? Furukawa, *you’re* saying that?” (laughs)

**—Hahaha.**

**Furukawa:** Another thing … when I reconstructed the storyboards, I realized he was cutting out connective beats. For example, a character enters a scene—meaning they appear in that location—but there is no shot actually showing them coming in. The part where Nagisa tries to repair her relationship with Honoka and speaks to her at school is easy to understand. “Yukishiro, want to have lunch together?” “Sorry, I have a student council meeting now …” The two of them repeat these little missed connections. None of the shots uses exactly the same setup, but the cels stay in the same position and the camera stays fixed in the same side-on, no-perspective view. The repetition goes pon, pon, pon … at a crisp tempo, and before you know it, it’s after school. You can watch it without thinking anything of it, but I found myself wondering: how was this written in the script?

The story moves with that much speed, and time inside the episode just *poof*—jumps ahead. Who is controlling that? Is it the script? Or is Igarashi simply that good? When I reconstructed storyboards from finished animation for the first time—in other words, when I “traced the act of designing animation”—the answer I arrived at was that this is only possible because someone deliberately controls it through the storyboard. I understood that by drawing it myself. So I don’t do half-measures like inserting an “explanatory entrance” shot. Drawing a storyboard is about how you extract only the necessary elements and eliminate what you don’t need. How do you cut out the connective beats and give the characters’ emotions speed and rhythm? I came to see episode 8 of *Precure* as an episode where all of those techniques are concentrated.

**—I see.**

**Furukawa:** Meeting that episode was what made me keep watching *Precure*, of course, and I also went back and rewatched all 200 episodes of *Sailor Moon*. I found my way back to Toei Animation, you could say. Before that I’d been in this mindset of, “From now on, anime is gonna be Production I.G and nobody else!” It was right around the release of *Innocence*, so that was the kind of animation I admired.

**—The absolute extreme of the high-quality, hand-drawn approach.**

**Furukawa:** Toei Animation was what taught someone like me how formidable it is to keep making animation all year long, and also something like the sheer “breadth” animation can have. Episode 8 of *Precure* was the trigger. The *Precure* series—especially the first season of *Futari wa Precure*—is a special, genuinely important work to me. So when I got to draw Cure Black as an animator on *Precure All Stars DX3*, directed by Takashi Otsuka, I thought, “Okay, I can retire as a key animator now.” I had entered the industry because I wanted to become a director in the first place, so after that I shifted toward directing work. That’s how important *Precure* is to me.
