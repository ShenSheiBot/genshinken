> This essay was honored in two categories of Lab on Roof’s 2022 open call for essays.

André Bazin challenged animation to get away from realism. As Makoto Sumii explains, Bazin prized work in which the artist’s hand reinvented the medium instead of borrowing the concepts and techniques of live-action cinema wholesale.[^bazin] That challenge sits awkwardly beside the industrial scale of Japanese animation, so much of which pursues what Bazin called pseudorealism: drawn figures serve conventional stories and imitate a camera-made world so persuasively that their handmade construction disappears. For Bazin, this quest for illustrative likeness narrowed both what animation could do and what it could think.

Yet a television anime or original video animation (OVA) can still produce an instant of panic and wonder, when every reassuring answer to “What is animation?” falls apart. The works considered here preserve that shock from inside commercial production. They turn its most familiar devices—held drawings, repeated backgrounds, limited movement, shot/reverse shot—against our habits of looking.

## I. Takashi Anno and Mourning Time

### 1. *Magical Emi, the Magic Star: Semishigure*

The broad, heavyset Koganei rushes downstairs to question his subordinate Kokubunji about a proposal. He has forgotten that only moments ago he rejected it and demanded a rewrite. Flustered, he turns away and recites a nursery-rhyme mnemonic. Sweating inside his heavy summer clothes, he pats each pocket in turn, checking off the objects named in the rhyme. Another thought comes to him. He hurries back, calls for Kokubunji—and forgets what he meant to say. “Nothing? Nothing at all …” Still muttering in disbelief, he wanders away again.

The scene is drab and easy to miss, which makes it perfectly at home in *Magical Emi, the Magic Star: Semishigure* (1986; hereafter *Semishigure*), an OVA composed almost entirely of minor incidents. Again and again, a pause or hesitation severs the expected link between perception and action. A lapse may feel like an omen, as though word of an approaching change has arrived early. Koganei’s lapse points in the opposite direction: a past he cannot recover holds him back. It feels like déjà vu stripped to its barest form. I cannot remember what happened, but I know that something did.

*Semishigure* unfolds over several summer days before the television series finale, when Mai relinquishes the magic that turns her into the idol Emi. The OVA steadily foreshadows her separation from the fairy Topo. That is its plain narrative direction. But when Mai’s mother sends her on an errand, director Takashi Anno builds a second pattern around the trip. Long shots reduce Mai to a speck in a vast, sun-bleached town, while glimpses of other residents interrupt her progress. Repeated places and parallel routines make the town seem to be filing this summer away before it has ended. Mai is still walking through it, but the images already anticipate the trace she will leave behind.

That may sound grand for an errand, so begin with the route itself. Mai puts on a hat and a little dress and sets out. On the way home, Anno reuses the background paintings from her outward journey (Figure 1a–d). A round trip ought to restore her to familiar ground. Instead, after a long stretch without music, a melody starts and Mai loses her way on streets she has walked countless times (Figure 1e).

The errand gives way to aimless wandering. Mai has slipped out of her usual route and into an empty quarter where the cicadas make summer seem endless. She fumbles with a hopelessly tangled cat’s cradle, passes a broken warning sign (Figure 1f), and finally reaches a wall marked *dead end*. The melody stops (Figure 1g).

Why does Anno cut from this dead end to Koganei reciting the rhyme meant to jog his memory? The juxtaposition unsettles without explaining itself. Successive shots make separate lives seem to unfold at once: Mai walks away laughing and humming, her reflection shrinking in the warning sign’s convex mirror, while Koganei straightens the papers on his desk and repeats the rhyme. He knocks over a cup. It falls and shatters, scattering pieces across the floor like petals.

Koganei crouches and picks up the pieces one by one, gathering them in his palm. The gesture is peculiar enough to hold our attention. He then arranges the shards carefully on his desk. Outside, rain begins to fall across the town. Anno cuts through a series of empty rain shots (Figure 1h–k), revisiting Mai’s detour in reverse order (Figure 1c–f). A shower occurring everywhere at once becomes a journey through editing: the rain retraces Mai’s route until it reaches the park, where she is sheltering beneath a tree.

Shō gives Mai a tiny, glittering shell ornament. Looking past it, she notices the rain washing away a picture of Emi that she drew on the ground. The shower ends; light floods the air; the world appears to wake. The moment has the force of a revelation.

Mai sees a rainbow above the town and runs toward it, leaving Topo behind. The sight recalls the little rainbow made by spray from a garden hose in the title sequence. Anno likes tightly arranged constellations of motifs, but here a detail that could have remained decorative becomes part of the OVA’s structure.

Mai calls to Topo, who lies motionless on a branch. The cicadas return, sparser now and faintly desolate, as though summer were already ending. The shot puts us beside Topo: Mai faces the camera and points excitedly at the rainbow. Wind stirs the ginkgo leaves and the rainbow-colored drops along the railing. One drop falls toward the wet patch where Emi’s chalk portrait used to be. An essay titled “Fragments from Takashi Anno’s Direction: The Transcendence of Everyday Life” catches the effect: “Even the least attentive viewer becomes like Topo: hearing and vision sharpen, the sense of touch grows keener, time seems to stretch without end, and every movement around us stands out …”[^anno-fragments] Mai has already moved ahead, while Topo and the viewer remain absorbed by the minute changes of the present—the leaves, the water, the cicadas. At first we cannot even hear her. Only after a pause does her voice reach us: “If you don’t hurry up, I’m going to leave you behind!”

The line lands like a threat. Mai freezes, fades, and dissolves into night. Images that had seemed loosely scattered now disclose the temporal design of the whole OVA. In Book XI of the *Confessions*, Augustine describes three presents: a present directed toward what is coming, a present attending to what is happening, and a present retaining what has passed.[^augustine] Mai leans toward the future. Koganei is caught by a past he cannot retrieve. Topo occupies the vanishing present between them, and for this suspended instant the viewer joins him there. We are always falling behind change, or just barely keeping pace with it. Topo will disappear; being left behind is the story’s ending. It is also ours. We too will leave this present and survive, if at all, as someone’s memory of it.

The same pattern runs through *Semishigure*: signs of what is coming appear alongside the feeling that something has already been lost. Mai’s wandering is intercut with Koganei’s forgetfulness. Just after he summons Kokubunji and loses the thought he meant to voice, Emi—Mai transformed—stops and turns; several shots break up her long gaze at the summer sky. Before the title card, everyone watches Emi’s magic show. Mai’s grandfather imagines a music box crowned by an Emi figurine. Koganei, shaken by the same performance, murmurs something we never hear. He seems trapped in a waking dream, unable to assemble its fragments into a memory that could return him to the present. Later, he sees Mai accidentally snap the string of her cat’s cradle. His eyes widen and he goes still, as if the break had filled his entire field of vision. Omen and déjà vu arrive together again. What can bind the broken string to both future and past? The answer the OVA presses on us is death—not necessarily literal death, but the instant when a living present turns into an irrecoverable trace.

A warm, faintly melancholy instrumental version of the opening theme continues. Koganei holds out his broad palm to catch a spark from Mai’s sparkler just before it dies. Everyone rushes out, afraid he has burned himself, while the camera—and we with it—remains in the empty room. The figurine in the music box turns until it stops. Then, with a cruelty almost too quiet to bear, the Emi figurine becomes a photograph. Time jams. We cannot move.

The adult Mai turns the pages of an album whose photographs are, in this severe Barthesian sense, an array of corpses: each one insists that what it shows was once there and is there no longer.[^barthes] We already know we will be left behind, but Anno withholds something else. He never gives us a proper look at the grown Mai. Did she become the idol she hoped to be? We cannot see her whole face or hear her speak. She barely moves in the dark room. The omission leaves a dull, oppressive unease.

One common interpretation treats the albums at the beginning and end as the trigger for Mai’s memories, making the entire OVA her subjective recollection. The film itself resists that account. These summer days include scenes from other people’s lives that Mai did not witness. More importantly, the reading mistakes what photography does in Anno’s work. Here a photograph does not revive memory; it arrests it. Anyone who has stared too long at an old photograph knows its coercive power. One instant becomes so fixed that the living sequence before and after it is difficult to recover. The past no longer returns as movement in the present.

Koganei’s failed recollection comes closer to this experience. The circuit will not close; only the obscure certainty of déjà vu remains. Cold, hard fragments lie in his hand. The faint burn in his palm carries the distant pain of loss. An old, blurred photograph can hurt in much the same way.

Anno returns to these concerns in another OVA, *Yokohama Kaidashi Kikou* (1998). The adaptation follows its manga closely, but the material fits his preoccupations with uncanny precision. In the first episode, Alpha tries to photograph a sunset changing over the sea. No single exposure can isolate the change itself. Back home, she can only remember the passing moment in silence. Where, then, does the experience of photography reside—in the picture, or in the duration that escaped it?

In the second episode, Alpha looks out at night over an electrical grid still glowing in a town submerged beneath the water. She weeps for ruins whose emptiness only intensifies her attachment. Mamoru Oshii describes three tenses within a ruin: it once stood whole; it is gone now; what remains will disappear in turn.[^1] Former splendor has vanished, yet the remnant proves that it existed. We long for what has passed while knowing that we cannot recover it. Such is love’s powerlessness.

The submerged lights no longer serve their original purpose. They simply flicker together in the dark. Animation lingers on that flicker long after its purpose is gone. From there, the thought returns to photography: light reflected from living people once altered the silver salts of a negative; years later, that trace sends light into our eyes. The people in the photograph were alive. Now they are dead. We will die too.

### 2. Episode 27 of *Maison Ikkoku*

Lu Xun once wrote, “When I am silent, I feel replenished; as I open my mouth, I sense emptiness.”[^lu-xun] Imagine an animator taking the line as a private confession. Before the blank sheet, everything remains possible. The first touch of the brush reduces that abundance to a few definite figures, each burdened with a job in the story. Intention suddenly looks embarrassingly blunt.

The complaint might continue. Once finished, the drawing no longer feels like an image in its own right. Its marks have become signs pressed into a story, clamoring where the page was once silent. The animator can complicate the surface and resist one-to-one meanings, but the plot keeps pulling every unruly detail back into line. A character is made to embody one idea, as if neither figure nor story could ever have been otherwise. The more completely the figure is made to signify, the less life it seems to possess apart from that meaning.

Anno’s work suggests another possibility, though stating it risks sounding ungenerous: his achievement does not rest on exceptional draftsmanship or lavish movement. He directs by patiently arranging unshowy, concrete images. *Semishigure* feels unmistakably his in its sparing use of music, its empty spaces and extended takes, its attention to small gestures. But an inventory of mannerisms cannot explain the work’s force.

That force comes from recurrence. Details that first look incidental gather into a pattern running against the story as well as alongside it. At a decisive moment, emptiness and fullness trade places: the interval between images lends the surrounding images their intensity. Anno approaches time through that interval—the largest thing animation must express and the one thing that can never appear on its own.

Episode 27 (1986) of the long-running *Maison Ikkoku* television series marked a major reorganization of the production staff. From this episode on, Takashi Anno served as chief director, and episode 27 was the only installment he personally storyboarded. Kazunori Itō took charge of series composition—the overall supervision of story and scripts—while Akemi Takada took over character design. Color designer Michiyo Yasuda and composer Kenji Kawai also joined the staff.

Naohito Takahashi, one of the animation directors, loved the heroine Kyōko Otonashi, manager of the Ikkoku-kan boarding house, so much that he worked under the pen name Ryūnosuke Otonashi. He later recalled: “That first change was astonishing; even the character designs changed. Conditions at the studio were extremely chaotic. But I think the upheaval taught everyone on the staff how to make everyday acting more expressive.”[^2] By “acting,” Takahashi means the performance built from drawn poses, gestures, and shifts of expression. For all the disorder of its production, episode 27 reveals Anno’s method with unusual clarity and shows how much animation can draw from the smallest gestures.

The first shot after the opening theme places a plump persimmon on a branch in the foreground. Far across the sky, a contrail lengthens behind an unseen plane. The shot holds two forces together: the fruit is still, heavy, and settled; the contrail records slow movement. Something appears about to happen, yet nothing does. Nothing seems to change, yet change is already under way. This tension governs both the episode’s plot and the cycles of everyday life around it.

The same pairing marks a seasonal turn in episode 22 (1985) of the *Magical Emi* television series, often treated as a prototype for *Semishigure*. Anno stages a tiny incident that alters the episode’s tone more than its plot. Yukiko washes vegetables, puts her hands beneath the tap, and flinches at the cold. She notices that the persimmons outside the window have ripened. The image dissolves to a contrail rising diagonally through the sky.

The autumn fruit gives narrative and memory a common frame. Despite the chaotic handover and a punishing schedule, Anno returns to persimmons eight times in a little over twenty minutes, always in a different form and always as part of the landscape. In *Magical Emi*, signs of the changing season confront Yukiko and make her notice time. In *Maison Ikkoku*, the persimmons stay outside the story and appear to watch it. They make one plain, uncanny declaration: they are here.

After flowers beside a slope stir in a light breeze, Kyōko walks toward us with Ikuko, the niece of her late husband, Sōichirō. In the manga, Kyōko says plainly that she still misses him but that her grief has lately grown less painful. The anime cuts the line. When Ikuko says she is going home to eat with her grandfather, a well-timed close-up instead catches a thought crossing Kyōko’s face. The manga then has Ikuko ask why the dog shares Sōichirō’s name, prompting Kyōko’s recollection. The anime lets the association surface without explanation. Kyōko laughs, calls “Sōichirō,” and runs toward Ikuko, who is playing with the dog.

A lyrical melody begins, and the image cuts to an unfamiliar town in memory, red with sunset. A sudden close-up follows: the younger, short-haired Kyōko, who knows nothing of what awaits her, has noticed something. Dazed, she reads the shopping list in her hand: “Green peppers, chunks of pork … I feel as though I’ve forgotten something …” On her way home to cook, in the middle of this unmistakably Anno-like lapse, she sees the silhouettes of a man and a dog climbing a steep hill against an enormous sun.

She softly repeats Sōichirō’s name and smiles with a happiness we can only envy. Then she calls out and runs toward them. Man and dog look back together. At that decisive instant, their backlit silhouettes freeze like a photograph, and the episode title appears: “Sōichirō Is Gone?! Memories of Yakitori.”

The title plays on the name shared by the man and the dog. The corresponding manga chapter, “Sōichirō Looks Back,” emphasizes their simultaneous backward glance. The anime title does more. It joins the silhouetted pair beneath the sun to the omitted line about Kyōko’s fading grief. Memory becomes both enchanting and treacherous. Has Sōichirō gone? Which Sōichirō?

The enormous sun complicates the image. The effect is not quite that of a telephoto lens; Anno is using freedoms specific to drawing. Distant objects need not diminish consistently, and the buildings do not have to obey a single system of linear perspective. The houses behind Kyōko seem to radiate outward rather than recede. The town presses close around her. By flattening near and far onto one plane, Anno makes the distant buildings feel dense with a history larger than any one character.

The effect recalls the submerged streetlights and spreading electrical grid in *Yokohama Kaidashi Kikou*. Near and far occupy the same drawn surface. Light moves across it not only as illumination but as a material shape. Anno’s technique and his imagined world fit each other perfectly.

The huge sunset and the town behind the figures recur in past and present. They resemble the sound of the river in *Semishigure*, which also crosses temporal boundaries. The OVA opens by making sound supply the depth that the flat image withholds. Through an unseen window comes the river’s low roar. Nearby, we hear a wind chime and Mai turning the pages of an album; farther off, children play and a train passes. Behind the image, the river runs through the town and through the accumulated memories of those who have lived beside it.

Can animation give scent the same reach across space and time? It cannot photograph an odor, but “Memories of Yakitori”—grilled chicken skewers—makes smell a through-line. The dog noses among scattered objects and recognizes the dead man’s scent on a wooden clog. In the past, the aroma of yakitori led it to Kyōko’s home. Years later, it leaves again, following the same smell across a gulf of time. Godai finally finds the dog in another red dusk. He has found “the vanished Sōichirō,” who disappeared in pursuit of the past’s scent. The metaphor anticipates Godai’s future: to love Kyōko, he must live beside her love for her dead husband and bear the presence of the dead.

Watch these works closely and Anno’s towns begin to resemble sites of private ritual. Editing and mise-en-scène turn useful, ordinary places into stages for actions that accomplish nothing. Mai’s errand in *Semishigure* ends at a wall. In *Yokohama Kaidashi Kikou*, Alpha watches distant streetlights switch on one by one, although they have no purpose left except to shine. The small miracle has a solitary witness but no beneficiary.

The pattern is already present at the end of *Magical Emi* episode 22. Susumu has run away after a failed magic trick. From high ground in a park—the park in *Semishigure* is also elevated—he looks toward lights scattered across the town. Suddenly, he remembers the second half of a nursery rhyme that had escaped him. He leaps from a swing as if performing the magic of flight. In a bird’s-eye view, the town slowly grows larger and for an instant seems to move closer.

At the end of *Maison Ikkoku* episode 27, the town’s transformation produces an experience of sweetness bordering on cruelty. It may be the fullest realization of Anno’s method. The camera tracks slowly right past flowers moving in the breeze. Far below, Kyōko appears at the foot of the slope, backed by innumerable household lights beneath the darkening sky. A side view shows distant houses soaked in sunset. Then, against the giant sun, she sees the silhouettes of a man and a dog climbing above her. The present has reproduced her memory so exactly that the pair might be Godai and the dog—or the dead Sōichirō returned. Kyōko calls the name they share. Man and dog turn together, black in the afterglow, and freeze like a photograph.

A gale suddenly roars. The camera now tracks left past flowers bent by the wind, reversing its earlier motion. Kyōko’s long hair and dress stream in the same direction. Far across town, a train travels left. Even the close-up of her astonished eyes tracks left. The movement also reverses a close-up in the remembered scene: there, facing right, Kyōko tells the dog—then searching for her husband’s scent—that “Sōichirō is gone.” Now the whole dusk has become a ritual in which the dead might return.

The pair of silhouettes is so familiar, so perfectly lifted from memory, that it gives Kyōko one impossible hope. Does that contradict what *Semishigure* taught us about photography? Here the town seems to summon a photograph and restore it to life. The irretrievable past crosses time and space to enter the present.

During the long look, the clatter of a passing train and the streetlights switching on from far to near beat against the stillness like a racing heart. In the manga, this suffocating ritual occupies a single panel accompanied by a heartbeat. We do not truly believe Godai will become Sōichirō. Even so, everyone holds their breath, waiting for Kyōko to say the name she has carried for years.

At last, the streetlights reach Kyōko and the flowers beside the slope. Her hair flies in the wind; her astonished profile is magnificent. This time, we know, she is about to address not the dog but the man. At the decisive instant, however, the name catches in her throat. A lamp spreads its light across Godai’s face.

Only then do we understand: this was never a resurrection. It was the cruel development of a photograph. Sōichirō’s face cannot appear. As soon as the image develops, all its other possibilities disappear; what has been fixed can no longer change. The discontinuity of cel animation lets Godai hold unnaturally still without seeming out of place, while the streetlamp develops his face like a latent image—an effect beyond anything the manga panel could produce.

The image before the light reaches him and the image after are almost identical. Their repetition nevertheless exposes the difference between a living man and a dead one. Once again, everything happens in the narrow interval between two images. Emptiness becomes fullness; hope gives way to shock. Kyōko folds inward and cannot move. At the one moment when any other song would be unthinkable, “Alone Again (Naturally)” begins.

Alone again. Alone once more. Godai stands before the sunset and looks toward Kyōko, who has mistaken him for a dead man. He dissolves gently, desolately, into the Milky Way pouring down the night sky. The camera follows that flow to the clock on Ikkoku-kan, the boarding house. The dusk of returning memory has ended. Beyond a window in the deep-blue night, a radiant river opens through the town like a wound.

Then the persimmon returns. It began as a seasonal detail framing the memory and has remained present throughout the episode. Now the same material object brings us back to the beginning. The fruit never receives a fixed symbolic meaning. It simply endures outside the plot, asking with every recurrence what has changed, what has not, what happened, and what might have happened in this circular story of loss and return.

A slowly rotating high-angle shot makes the few warm lights of Ikkoku-kan look frail beside the town’s countless lamps. This story is only one among many. The song’s final “Alone again, naturally” coincides first with Godai upstairs and then with Kyōko below. She softly says “Sōichirō” to the dog, though the name still carries the dead man within it. A faint smile returns to her face.

The episode that began with a persimmon and a contrail ends there. The final images of the closing theme seem to answer that opening pair. Godai walks beneath the pouring Milky Way. The dog named Sōichirō appears by the road and falls into step with him. They exchange glances, then recede together into the boundless night.

## II. Naohito Takahashi: What a Look Can Do

### 1. The Surface That Will Not Open

Anno asks what repetition can do to time: a place, object, or pose returns almost unchanged, and the interval reveals what has been lost. Naohito Takahashi starts from the same formal fact but strips it down more severely. He repeats a drawing until we can no longer treat it as a transparent window onto the story; we have to confront its surface. The question changes with it. If an image will not open and yield an interior, what can a look passing between two such surfaces do?

In episode 12 (2002) of *Figure 17: Tsubasa & Hikaru*, an empty northern sky carries extraordinary emotional weight. Hikaru, Tsubasa’s inseparable double, knows they must eventually part and has begun avoiding her. Tsubasa stands alone in the snow. A low-angle tracking shot recalls background art director Shichirō Kobayashi’s way of setting stark branches like dark ink against a pale, nearly contourless sky.

A boy sees Tsubasa waiting at a bus stop. They stand beside each other without meeting one another’s eyes. “I have important business today, so I’m taking the bus,” he explains, reaching for words comically grand for an elementary-school child. He glances at the girl, whose head is bowed in dejection, and immediately looks away.

The image returns to a sky ringed by branches, almost identical to the one before. A lyrical melody begins. This time the camera does not track; it slowly closes in on the blank sky, as if drawn upward. The children wait without speaking. Wind roars in the distant woods. Snow settles on their hats, more thickly on Tsubasa’s hat and scarf because she arrived first. At last the bus comes, its tires scraping over ice and snow. They sit together in the back row while mountains drift past the windows. Even inside the bus, low camera angles keep the vast sky in view.

“I hear you’re going back to Tokyo.” Once again, the boy breaks the silence. Only now do the bus stop, the winter landscape, and their brief time together disclose themselves as the setting for a farewell. Although he speaks, he does not look at her. She turns toward him, then drops her eyes just before he finally looks back. Their gazes keep missing, and the near encounters pass quietly beneath the dialogue.

Takahashi knows how to stage a conversation. He places bodies with exacting care and calibrates every turn of the head, every shift in a sight line, every instant when someone looks away. A glance can therefore carry a scene with remarkable economy. Only when the boy half rises to press the stop button do the two children, seen in a wide shot as if each were stealing a look, come close to meeting each other’s eyes.

Three times during the conversation, the low-angle sky fills a pause with a blank surface. This is Takahashi’s negative mode of direction: he works by withholding action, depth, and explanation. Yet restraint alone does not account for the force of his repeated surfaces. Near the episode’s end, Hikaru and Tsubasa stand in the woods and notice a few fine snowflakes. The music fades, leaving only wind. As they look up, a quick tilt reveals a gray sky enclosed by branches. Is it the same sky as the one over the bus stop? The glimpse is too brief to tell. Then the identical drawing appears twice more. Takahashi deliberately breaks ordinary spatial continuity.

No matching tilt had established that this was the sky above the children at the bus stop. If Takahashi had asked why he needed to draw another one for the woods, there might have been no practical answer. Yet only after the same sky returns does Tsubasa find the courage to ask Hikaru about the separation she has begun to sense. In a fixed shot, the firmament watches the tiny pair embrace and sob in the snow. Because the drawing belongs equally to the bus stop and the woods, it no longer represents either place alone. It becomes the sky under which people part: mute, flat, and impossible to enter.

Animation has always synchronized images and music through rhythmic editing and movement. Takahashi is just as exact with silence and stillness. Repeating a quiet surface creates another kind of movement: almost nothing changes within the frame, yet feeling gathers from shot to shot. He needs neither musical excitement nor a fresh turn of plot to propel the scene. His sensibility lies in the succession of particular images, not in montage as an abstract formula.

In *To Heart* (1999), Akari and Hiroyuki’s largely unspoken attachment forms one side of a triangle with their friend Shiho, who also cares for Hiroyuki. Episode 8 quietly sustains the distance across which Akari and Hiroyuki look at one another. They barely touch. The space between them makes the gaze possible, and their gentle looks make that space palpable in return. Each body registers the other across the room. Their calm exchange confirms a private space and a stretch of time that they share.

Shigehiko Hasumi’s writing on the gaze begins from a paradox: sight depends on distance, while touch abolishes it; the closest union would occur only when the eye ceased to function.[^hasumi-gaze] The repeated shot/reverse-shot sequence at the end of episode 8 pushes that conflict between looking and contact to its limit. It is not merely a romantic delay, another promise that something may happen in an ordinary life where nothing quite does.

Takahashi has the nerve to place identical still drawings only seconds apart. The scene nearly stops. In another chest-up shot over Akari’s shoulder toward Hiroyuki, she straightens so slightly that the composition hardly changes; the shot simply lasts a little longer. Gentle music begins. Hiroyuki raises his head. Only now do we understand that Akari has been watching him. The camera slowly moves closer to her silent gaze.

By bringing space and time almost to a halt, Takahashi separates this exchange from the countless glances of daily life. At first, Akari’s look means only that she is waiting. Silence and immobility make it opaque, and therefore inexhaustible. Her waiting seems to expand beyond the instant and contain her whole history with Hiroyuki. Or perhaps the movement runs the other way: because this is one ordinary glance among many, its sudden intensity travels backward and changes all the earlier ones. Either way, the series has singled it out.

There is a hard problem here. Takahashi can show two people looking at each other in profile, or divide their simultaneous encounter into alternating shots. Even if the camera circles their shared sight line forever, as it does in Mamoru Oshii’s *Urusei Yatsura 2: Beautiful Dreamer* (1984), no position can show both faces head-on at once. Any camera filming two people who look at each other faces an unhappy choice: stay outside the encounter and see both in profile, or move between them and break their simultaneity. Hasumi treats this as a death sentence for the filmed meeting of gazes.[^hasumi-gaze] Drawing does not make the problem disappear.

Conventional direction often passes over the difficulty, cutting from face to face and changing angles to disguise repetition. Oshii’s advice to eliminate useless shot/reverse-shot footage from dialogue scenes answers the same danger. Takahashi takes the opposite risk. He repeats the composition so doggedly that it can seem merely blunt, then makes minute differences emerge from near-total stillness. Change occurs because the image stays the same.

Episode 13 pushes the conventions of romance anime to an extreme by returning obsessively to exchanged looks. In the gym, rain patters outside. From Akari’s place against the wall, we follow her gaze toward Shiho, who stands in a distant line of girls. The camera continues moving in on Shiho as she faces right. A close-up of Akari midway through the movement reminds us that her gaze has not relented.

After a long stillness, Shiho either senses the look or becomes unable to bear it. She turns slightly toward Akari. A cut catches Akari’s mild surprise. Shiho quickly turns back and lowers her head; the angle remains unchanged and the image goes still. After another cut, a matching move toward Akari begins.

Takahashi will not seek relief in a new angle. The move toward one girl gives way to stillness; stillness on the other gives way to a move toward her. The shots remain sequential, but the exchanged motions register what has changed between them across the gym. Akari’s expression does not explain that change. After one flicker of surprise, her face looks as it did before.

The repeated surface becomes still more powerful when Shiho visits Akari midway through the episode. The room is extraordinarily beautiful. The low, wavering sound of a kettle stretches through the scene. Akari sits on the bed; Shiho sits beside it. They face almost the same direction at a shallow angle. When either turns toward the other, window light catches her cheek. When she turns away, her cheek enters shadow. As Akari speaks, fine bands of shade tremble along her ear, cheek, and neck.

Hasumi distinguishes between two ways of seeing a filmed face. We can treat its light and shadow as evidence of an unseen psychology, or we can attend first to the face as an arrangement of light, color, and contour on the image. He calls the first a “psychological face” and the second a “face of the image.”[^hasumi-face] The distinction does not deny that Akari and Shiho have inner lives. It asks us not to make the visible shadows disappear into an explanation of those lives. Shiho twice denies that she likes Hiroyuki. The first time, she avoids Akari’s eyes and turns into shadow. The second, she stands, faces Akari, and says she does not like a guy like him at all. Light falls on both faces. From then until the end of the scene, neither one enters shadow again.

The change carries sadness and relief. Lyrical music begins as the kettle fades from the soundtrack. The length of the scene, and the accumulated history of Akari’s bedroom as a setting, make Shiho’s defeat unavoidable without forcing her expression to spell it out.

Then Akari asks outright whether Shiho likes Hiroyuki. A silent shot/reverse-shot passage makes their mutual gaze dangerous (Figure 2a–d). Akari’s eyes do not open a window onto her thoughts. They remain visible, opaque marks that Shiho can neither meet for long nor wholly escape. The gaze gains force because the editing sustains its pressure, not because one pupil somehow penetrates another person or reveals a hidden soul.

In the middle row, from Tai Katō’s *Blood of Revenge* (1965; a′–d′), and the bottom row, from Norifumi Suzuki’s *Girl Boss Blues: Queen Bee’s Challenge* (1972; a″–d″), comparable live-action exchanges vary the faces, angles, and framing from shot to shot.

Such exchanges are common in live action, as the middle and lower rows show. The actors’ expressions, camera angle, and framing shift slightly each time a face returns. That continuous variation would require new drawings in Takahashi’s form of limited cel animation. It can, however, reuse one drawing exactly.

Godai’s unnatural stillness looked natural in *Maison Ikkoku*. Here the arrested gaze exposes a characteristic stiffness of limited animation. Takahashi repeats the drawings exactly and refuses the relief of a change in angle. The breaks between them become visible. He turns a production constraint into an expressive resource and pushes the repeated surface—blank yet charged—to an extreme.

At crucial moments, Takahashi stops replacing drawings and leaves a held pose suspended outside ordinary time. Episode 28 of the wordless *The Adventures of Mini-Goddess* (1998) begins with a girl sitting alone on a veranda, staring into the rain. Her eyes meet a crow’s above her. The sequence twice returns to the same shot of her calm, motionless gaze. The crow suddenly turns its head with mechanical precision. Stranger still, the girl shows no surprise, as though she had expected it. When the bird flies away, she almost seems to wish it would stay.

She wanders through an empty city in the rain, and the camera keeps returning to her silent gaze. Rain, a flooded river, and ocean surf fill this lifeless world; only the wingbeats of crows and gulls escape the watery soundscape. On the shore she finds a rotting tree trunk and a doll abandoned by an unknown owner. The same shot of her contemplating these forsaken objects appears twice, echoing her look at the crow and inviting a connection among them.

She then approaches a flock of gulls standing unnaturally still on the ground. They beat their wings and rise all at once. At that instant she reaches toward the sky, but the film refuses to show what she sees. Where does she want to go?

The episode offers no settled cosmology and never says that the girl wants to die. Still, its pattern of ominous birds, dead wood, an abandoned doll, and repeated looks makes a deathward reading difficult to dismiss. The crow may seem to invite her away; her calm gaze may seem to accept. Because the film leaves that possibility suspended rather than confirming it, the encounter is all the more chilling.

At the end, a gust tears away her umbrella, apparently the only bright color in the world, and the crow returns. Her slow, determined steps toward it make the heart sink. Then her companions arrive, and the world warms and brightens at once. The turn moves us because the same device that kept two gazes apart has made an ambiguous meeting with a bird feel like a summons toward solitary death.

### 2. From Untouchable Bodies to Impossible Sight

> **Content note:** This section discusses explicit sexual imagery, incest, sexual violence, and bodily fluids.

The argument now reaches its hardest claim. The kind of line-drawn cel animation considered here can depict bodies, wounds, and sex. What it cannot do is turn pigment and contour into the photographed presence of human flesh. This is a philosophical distinction, not a technical law for every kind of animation. Stop motion, paint on glass, CGI, rotoscoping, and lineless animation each pose different problems. Takahashi’s OVAs belong to a narrower tradition, one that assembles bodies from discrete drawings, black contours, colored trace lines, and photographed cels.

To name what exceeds such a body, I turn to a deliberately extreme theoretical lineage. In *Francis Bacon: The Logic of Sensation*, Gilles Deleuze describes flesh as a zone where the distinction between human and animal begins to fail.[^deleuze-flesh] Georges Bataille’s *Story of the Eye* tears an eye from the body and forces it into obscene contact with another organ.[^bataille-eye] Even when a live-action image is staged, it can confront us with the indexical fact of an actor’s vulnerable body. A volume on director Tatsumi Kumashiro attributes to him the claim that a close-up of Taiji Tonoyama’s withered penis in Nagisa Ōshima’s *In the Realm of the Senses* (1976) surpasses acting because it expresses human existence as a whole: comic, shameless, and sorrowful at once.[^kumashiro] On those deliberately narrow terms, cel animation contains no penis—only a graphic construction that represents one.

The OVA *Stainless Night* (1995) stages a kind of intercourse between black contours and colored trace lines, the production lines used to give selected boundaries a color other than black. They interlace and caress one another. The eroticism belongs to lines on a surface. Folds in clothing, patterns of light and shadow, and even the tiny creases in a sheet crushed beneath a body acquire erotic charge through contact with that body. They are not incidental decoration. They are where the image locates sensation.

In this kind of cel animation, contour does not cover a body that already exists; it constitutes the body we see. Hair, clothing, shadow, and bedding then layer further textures over that first graphic boundary. This order produces a peculiar erotic arrangement. The image promises a body beneath its surfaces, yet every variation that makes the promise—each strand of hair, fold of cloth, or band of light—also attracts the eye in its own right. The implied body can become a generic sign. The textures stay restless and specific. Once again, the surface entices the eye without opening onto actual depth.

This helps explain why Naohito Takahashi lights the central sex scenes in *Stainless Night* and *Kagami* (1997) so fiercely. Light and shadow flicker across the bodies without settling into stable shapes. The drawn penis cannot acquire the material weight of photographed flesh, yet it becomes translucent. Light passes through it while semen moves inside. The two OVAs share other features, but their clearest connection is this use of shifting light to make drawn flesh translucent and reveal the semen within it. *Kagami* is the second OVA unit within *Yūwaku Countdown* and unfolds across three episodes; every episode number below refers to *Kagami* itself.

The cel image cannot pass through its contours into tactile flesh. Nor does the viewer encounter the physical cel directly. Photography, compositing, and duplication turn any dust, uneven paint, or scratches that register at all into optical noise within the release image; the flat color fields, not the cel as a handled object, dominate what we see. A fixed background painting imposes another limit. Unless the production supplies new art, the camera can only travel across that painting, move nearer or farther away, or hold on it. *Kagami* turns both constraints into a painful test of the medium’s freedom.

I will call the result decadent in a precise formal sense. The OVA indulges in the luxurious surfaces and erotic excess of cel animation until those pleasures expose their own artifice. Its images promise touch, then make their inability to deliver touch part of the experience. That is why *Kagami* deserves a place in animation history.

Placed beside *Belladonna of Sadness* (1973), *Kagami* approaches the medium’s limit from the opposite direction. Under art director Kuni Fukai, *Belladonna* abandons conventional character animation for lines that proliferate and freely change shape. *Kagami* stops replacing drawings, freezes its characters, and holds the camera still. Its everyday scenes expose the fiction of animated touch through discontinuity and estrangement. The barrier here belongs to the medium, not to the plot: lines must simulate the flesh that those same lines keep out of reach. When movement stops, the contradiction can no longer hide in the scene’s flow.

The still everyday scenes anticipate contact and repeatedly postpone it; the sex scenes answer that delay with sensual motion. Even then, Takahashi never shows the exact point of penetration. The lines that define vagina and penis remain far removed from tactile flesh. At the end of episode 2, the bodies stop moving and only a transmitted-light effect—the photographic backlighting used in cel production to make an area flare or glow—travels across the lower half of the heroine Kaname’s body. Takahashi does not depict ejaculation. In the final scene, that omission becomes conspicuous enough to register as an absence within the sexual act.

Dense fields of long, scratch-like lines appear in the backgrounds most often when the protagonist, Kagami Amayo, is with his older sister. Their texture gives a visible form to a tactile relationship built since childhood. More plainly, the scratches mark his sister’s power to initiate and control touch between them. In episode 2, she pierces Kagami’s ear and initiates a kiss. In episode 3, she stands over him as he sits and places her palm against his cheek. Each time, the background becomes a field of fine lines multiplying at furious speed.

*Kagami* depicts a man trapped within the tactility of lines. The image grants him neither Deleuze’s unstructured flesh nor the intimacy of matter touching matter; all contact remains represented by surfaces. Watching people across the city lose themselves in physical encounters, Kagami says, “In the end, only bodies ever connect.” The line opposes bodily contact to any assured meeting of minds. It also names the sadness of a body that can connect only as a pattern of lines.

Kagami’s name means “mirror,” and mirrors recur throughout the OVA. They place him in a circuit of reflected images with the sister he resembles, unable to turn from his double and see another person. A mirror also offers apparent depth behind glass that no hand can enter. That barrier is physical and optical, distinct both from cel animation’s lack of photographed flesh and from the story’s sexual taboos. Kagami reaches into empty air but cannot touch the fullness promised beyond the mirror. Instead he caresses his sister’s school uniform, an empty garment that once touched her body.

A production interview says that the animated adaptation did not retain the source manga’s incestuous conclusion.[^3] Read through Bataille’s account of eroticism, taboo, and transgression, that omitted ending presents a severe theoretical demand.[^bataille-eroticism] The brother and sister, already alike in appearance, would have to escape their mirrored bodies of line and enter the disorder of flesh. Crossing the social and sexual taboo of incest would turn union into a violent loss of separate selves: pain, ecstasy, self-offering, and a momentary rehearsal of death without literal dying. This account of their union reconstructs what the theory would demand; it is not an argument that the adaptation ought to have depicted incest or sexual violence.

Bataille’s theory drives transgression toward abjection, where the boundaries between clean and filthy, living and decaying, self and other begin to collapse. Push that logic to its hypothetical extreme—decay, excrement, urine, semen—and a drawn scene might still do no more than illustrate an idea. This is a theoretical test case, not a description of either the manga or the OVA. Explicitness alone cannot give a moving drawing the sense of embodied presence that literature can evoke without showing a body at all. *Kagami* therefore changes the problem. Instead of making touch ever more graphic, its final movement asks whether sight can reach where touch cannot. Phallic penetration loses its organizing role; animal coupling gives way to an angelic orbit; the represented body gives way to the eye.

The opening of episode 1 has already established sight and touch as twin themes. In a church at the town’s highest point, colored light and shadow from a stained-glass saint fall across Kagami’s pupil in close-up. He looks up, but the saint inclines its head and does not meet his gaze. Kagami cannot see where the saint is looking. Then the camera reveals its colored image cast on the floor behind him like a second incarnation. From that impossible position, the projection appears to look directly at Kagami.

The sight line exposes a precise blind spot. Kagami can face the saint before him or turn toward the projection behind him, but no ordinary viewpoint lets him see both the saint and its projection at once. He reaches into empty air and says, “I’m so thirsty.” The line names a desire that the smooth cel surface cannot satisfy.

In the interval between images, Takahashi creates what I will call a “third eye.” It is neither an extra organ nor a grand synonym for consciousness, and it does not belong literally to Kagami or Kaname. It names an experience that editing produces for the viewer: we register another person’s presence and response even though no single shot can contain the reciprocal encounter. Repeated shot/reverse-shot passages at decisive moments in episodes 1 and 3 make that relation palpable (Figure 3).

Lower row, episode 3 (α–γ): the frontal composition holds while hands rise into the frame, letting touch and response register without placing both sides of the encounter in one image.

In the upper sequence, Kagami’s unchanged face appears before and after Kaname. The cut tells us that he sees her, but the repeated drawing gives no visible sign that the encounter has changed him. The lower sequence reverses the emphasis. No single shot shows the person seen, the touch, and the answering response together. Even so, the held composition, the approaching hands, and the body’s reaction make another presence felt. The sequence culminates in orgasm, but the edit leaves cause productively uncertain: gaze, touch, and response cannot be reduced to a simple chain. The “third eye” is this experience of reciprocity across the cut.

The OVA presents Kagami as male. In the final encounter he appears outside the church in feminine clothes, his long hair loose; Kaname recognizes him at once and never treats the appearance as either a transformation or a mistaken identity. Takahashi reserves for Kaname and Kagami alone the gazes that briefly detach themselves from ordinary bodily action and exert pressure across a cut. Kaname’s former boyfriend never receives such an independent gaze; in the OVA’s terms, he has no eyes.

A photograph of Kagami’s sister stands on his bedroom desk. A drawing lies on Kaname’s. It depicts two angels mirroring each other—or an angel and its reflection—about to kiss. The photograph belongs to Kagami’s closed circuit with his sister; the drawing anticipates a different affinity, between Kagami and Kaname, that does not depend on outward resemblance. Their looks no longer preserve a simple division between the person who sees and the person who is seen.

What remains here of penetration, of one sexual organ entering another? Almost no trace of the phallus. Kagami never removes the feminine clothes he is wearing; his skirt covers his penis from beginning to end. The organ is not merely hidden but denied the power to organize the scene. In silhouette and movement, the embrace presents two feminine figures, yet the narrative has not changed Kagami into a woman or asked Kaname to mistake him for one. Their union no longer resembles intercourse in the ordinary sense and is not structured by genital penetration.

Gustav Fechner’s *The Comparative Anatomy of Angels* imagines angels as spherical beings, turning the sphere’s geometric perfection into a speculative anatomy.[^4] *Kagami* joins that conceit to its image of the eye. If these lovers are to couple as angels rather than animals, Takahashi gives them planetary movement: orbit and rotation instead of thrusting. Kagami and Kaname circle one another and turn on their own axes. Only through this nonpenetrative rotation does Kagami reach orgasm for the first time.

Editing grants us the “third eye,” a mode of sight that still cannot carry us through the glass door separating us from the lovers’ angelic coupling. Snow falls on a holy night; the glass gives back our reflection; the hand reaching toward the scene is itself a pattern of lines. We can register the encounter across cuts, but the image will not let us enter it.

This is what it means for Takahashi to work at cel animation’s limit. He does not close the gap between drawings, clear the opacity of a pupil, or turn contour into flesh. He makes each failure visible and asks it to carry feeling. A repeated drawing becomes an encounter; a body that cannot acquire flesh finds another intimacy in orbit and exchanged looks. Animation does not escape its construction. For one astonishing moment, that construction learns to see.
