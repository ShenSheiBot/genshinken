## Foreword: The Working Body and the Bound Soul

The zombie is one of popular culture’s most durable figures. Films, novels, and games repeatedly enlist it to trigger crisis and turn ordinary space hostile. Yet the monster did not arise from nowhere. Anthropological and sociological readings of zombie traditions allow us to sketch some of its earlier forms, though no single “original zombie” can account for everything the figure later became.

This essay follows one thread through those transformations: labor. How did a creature associated with sorcery—a body condemned to work while its soul remained in bondage—become an emblem of modern fear? And might the walking corpse of contemporary fiction still contain, metaphorically, a captive soul?

## I. Transformations of the Zombie Image

### 1. The “Enemy of the Open Society”: The Zombie Horde

If one were mischievous enough to search Karl Popper’s *The Open Society and Its Enemies* for a portrait of the zombie horde, the match might seem surprisingly close. Popper gives special prominence to tribalism and opposition to cosmopolitan life.[^1] Unless we count going “planetward”—my deliberately warped term for a horde spreading across the planet—as a form of globalization, what could look more hostile to an open society than a massed and merciless Other?

The analogy is playful, but the history behind it is not. Eric Kurlander has discussed Nazi rhetoric that cast the Weimar Republic’s modernity as a force that had stupefied or “zombified” Germans. Across the Atlantic, *Revenge of the Zombies* (1943) imagined a Nazi scientist creating an undead army in the Louisiana bayou.[^2] The early zombie cycle was still small, but the figure was already being recruited to picture political domination. Was this merely coincidence, or did the zombie carry an unresolved association that invited such uses before its screen image had stabilized?

Answering that question requires us to look beyond contemporary film and games. Folklore did not supply popular culture with a finished monster. It supplied stories, fears, and social memories that modern writers and filmmakers repeatedly rearranged. The zombie’s history lies in that process of rearrangement.

### 2. Icelandic Revenants and Haitian *Zonbi*: Body and Soul

If we isolate such motifs as “corpse,” “resurrection,” and “cannibalism,” folklore offers countless beings that look vaguely zombie-like. Most are near misses. The returning dead are common enough; even Snow White is mistaken for dead and later revived. What matters here is not resurrection alone but the loss of human agency: a corpse reanimated as an instrument rather than a person, and not simply a spirit returning to complete unfinished business.

Icelandic legends offer a useful comparison, though not an alternative origin story for the modern zombie. Among Jón Árnason’s categories of magic tales are *uppvakningar og sendingar*: raised corpses and “sendings.” In such stories, a magician may raise a dead body and send it to attack someone or perform an errand.[^3] The resemblance lies less in cannibalism than in compelled work. A body serves an assigned purpose without a will of its own—or at least without a will anyone else recognizes.

That resemblance becomes historically charged in Haitian traditions of the *zonbi*. Outsider accounts have often flattened Haitian Vodou into “evil sorcery,” obscuring the cultural logic of the stories themselves. The vocabulary also requires care. The *zonbi kò kadav* is a corporeal zombie: a body deprived of an essential part of the soul and made to serve. The *zonbi astral* is a captured spirit or soul. A *zonbi jaden*, meanwhile, is a zombie made to labor in a field or garden; it is not another name for the spirit-zombie.[^4] A *bokor* may be said to bring about this condition, but the terror belongs above all to the victim’s loss of freedom.

The word’s deeper etymology is contested. Scholars have proposed cognates in several West and Central African languages, including terms related to spirits and the dead; it is too simple to derive every use from a single Kongo word. What matters for this essay is the division of body from soul and the body’s continued capacity to work.

In the strand of Haitian lore foregrounded here, becoming a zombie is more frightening than encountering one. The punishment is not merely to hover between life and death, much less to become a cannibal. It is to lose one’s agency and continue laboring, often on a plantation, under another’s command. Colonial slavery thus gives the working corpse its deepest historical terror. The body remains useful; the person to whom it belonged has been dispossessed.

### 3. Monster Rally: Modern Rewriting and Modern Fear

The zombie looks ancient, but its familiar image is modern. So are many supposedly timeless monsters. The tales collected and rewritten by the Brothers Grimm, and the literary fairy tales of Hans Christian Andersen, did not simply preserve medieval fears intact; they helped give older materials new forms for modern readers. As forests ceased to be unknown spaces, the beings inside them acquired different meanings. Writers, folklorists, and filmmakers did not merely inherit monsters. They remade them for their own *Zeitgeist*.

The vampire’s modern career makes the pattern especially clear. Bram Stoker’s *Dracula* (1897) and F. W. Murnau’s *Nosferatu* (1922) turned a folkloric predator into a richly coded modern figure. Count Orlok remains a foreign, contaminating body who brings plague and threatens both Ellen and the city, while later screen vampires often become articulate, urbane, even sympathetic. Scientific confidence did not eliminate the specter; it made the specter available for subtler forms of characterization. Kurlander likewise shows how Nazi rhetoric appropriated werewolves and vampires through a *völkisch* language of forest, soil, land, and people.[^5]

The zombie underwent a comparable reconstruction. Early films invoked sorcery and drugs; later ones added radiation, contamination from space, and eventually viruses. These causes are not interchangeable. George A. Romero’s 1968 film leaves the exact mechanism ambiguous, and its revenants were not originally explained as victims of a zombie virus. But the pathogen became dominant because it offers a distinctly modern horror: it reproduces, spreads, and transforms its host.

Later biomedical knowledge also made older taboos available for new readings. Prion diseases, for example, lent “scientific” dread to the already ancient fear of consuming human flesh. David Cronenberg’s body-horror films often ask us to imagine disease not only as destruction but as transformation—a body reorganized for another purpose. Read this way, the virus-driven zombie returns us to enslavement in mechanized form. A human body becomes a machine captured by an infection and assigned an endless new task: reproduce the infection and consume its own kind. Modern pathology supplies the medium, but the older terror remains recognizable. The body works; its former subject has vanished.

## II. The Zombie Image in Film

### 1. Radical Times and Romero’s Specter

The screen zombie assumed its most familiar form only after the alien and radiation cycles of the 1950s and 1960s. One stage in that transformation was the loss of an ordinary human appearance. Another was the disappearance of the master who commanded the zombie’s labor. In Hammer’s *The Plague of the Zombies* (1966), the decaying dead still work in a Cornish tin mine under the control of the local squire. The setting changes, but the structure recalls the coerced plantation labor of earlier zombie films.

Then came Romero’s *Night of the Living Dead* (1968). Romero called its creatures ghouls, and the film moved them decisively away from Caribbean Vodou into the American backyard.[^6] Later audiences and filmmakers made them “zombies.” To put Romero’s intervention playfully, he broke their chains. As Peter Dendle writes, Romero gave the zombie not a job but “a drive (eating flesh).”[^7] By combining the zombie with the flesh-eating ghoul, Romero loosened its defining connection to commanded labor. Compulsion remained, but no human master issued the orders. If the zombie was a working machine, the machine had acquired a terrible kind of “freedom.”

That freedom became a specter of its own. *Dawn of the Dead* (1978), made in collaboration with Dario Argento, reached Italy in September 1978 as *Zombi* while the country was living through the Years of Lead.[^8] Neo-fascist bombings, the state’s contested role in the Strategy of Tension, and violence by armed left-wing groups—including the Red Brigades—had made political terror part of ordinary life. The film’s besieged shopping mall could readily speak to viewers living amid that anxiety.

I do not mean that *Dawn* supplied the Italian left with an actual political program, or that politics alone explains the country’s zombie-film cycle. My narrower claim is interpretive: the image of a horde controlled by no one yet trapped in repetitive compulsion could resonate with a society preoccupied by mass politics, consumerism, and violence. Lucio Fulci’s *Zombi 2* (1979), titled to capitalize on the Italian release of *Dawn*, drove the zombie’s bodily terror further. The cycle then waned in the early 1980s for many reasons, including changing markets and production conditions as well as the defeat and repression of radical movements. The parallel is not proof of causation, but it is more than an empty coincidence: the films and their political moment shared a vocabulary of order breaking down.

### 2. The Reversal of Abjection and Nobility: Human No More

Romero’s successors enlarged the zombie image, sometimes while refusing the undead category itself. The enemies in Danny Boyle’s *28 Days Later* (2002) are living people infected by the Rage virus, not corpses restored to life.[^9] Yet the film uses the grammar of zombie cinema: infection, hordes, deserted streets, and survivors expelled from the spaces they once inhabited.

London—the administrative heart of the former British Empire—loses its human circulation and becomes a machine left running without a purpose. Traffic lights still change and car alarms still sound, but there is no traffic to direct and nobody protecting the cars. The city remains physically intact while its social function dies. In this sense, the infected do not merely occupy London; they “zombify” it.

Horror often introduces what a living body and an orderly society must expel: blood, waste, filth, germs. Julia Kristeva calls this disturbing remainder the abject. The infected are abject twice over. Their blood transmits contagion, and their bodies have been cast outside the social order. Yet they are also the force that expels the survivors. The reversal turns the former human subject into what I will call the “Human Abject”: the person ejected from the city as the body ejects waste.

This does not establish a noble new order. The human and the monster merely exchange positions. Read through the anti-colonial concerns associated with Frantz Fanon and Homi Bhabha, the reversal raises a harder question: does overturning a binary opposition free anyone, or simply invert its terms? Zombie cinema gives no stable answer. It does, however, make the fantasy of human mastery suddenly precarious, which helps explain the figure’s usefulness to post-apocalyptic fiction.

### 3. “Zombies”: The Answer of Post-Apocalyptic Fiction

What, then, is an apocalypse? The word means an unveiling or revelation, not destruction alone. In the *Book of Revelation*, the end of ordinary historical time opens onto judgment and, for the saved, the New Jerusalem: an invulnerable city described in spatial rather than temporal terms. The apocalyptic promise is therefore not only catastrophe but final safety.

That promise helps explain a basic tension in post-apocalyptic fiction. Its characters almost never possess safety, yet they remain oriented toward its mirage: a shelter, a settlement, a new society. The cause of the catastrophe follows them along the road and generates conflict at every stop. Zombies are particularly effective because their persistence resists the forward motion of the survivors’ quest. As Steven Shaviro writes, “The slow meanders of zombie time emerge out of the paralysis of the conventional time of progressive narrative.”[^10] The survivor wants history to begin again; the zombie keeps returning.

I use *chronos* here simply as a name for measurable, sequential time, not as a classical synonym for the apocalypse.[^11] Zombie time seems to survive the end of that sequence. The dead decay slowly, disperse through space, and remain after any single human life has ended. They are ideal apocalyptic objects: beyond the timetable of progress, yet present everywhere within the landscape.

Whether the “New Jerusalem” exists or not, it remains a possible destination. Can a new world be built after catastrophe? Every yes or no discloses an author’s political imagination. Survivors may find temporary shelter, but the monsters keep coming. Futurity begins at the point where safety collides with that stubborn remainder.

## III. Reading the Zombie Image

### 1. The Return of Frankenstein’s Monster

Before pursuing that reading, it is worth returning to Mary Shelley’s *Frankenstein* (1818). Frankenstein’s creature and the zombie share more than a stitched or decaying exterior. Both are imagined as dead matter brought into motion, and both are denied an ordinary place among the living. Across endless adaptations, a Frankenstein-like monster returns in the zombie—now multiplied into a crowd.

Neither monster possesses a secure name. Shelley’s creature is repeatedly reduced to “it”; the screen zombie to moans, a staggering gait, and compulsive movement. I read that impaired, repetitive motion through the psychoanalytic ideas of repetition compulsion and the death drive. But this is an interpretation of how the monster has been represented, not a claim that bodily impairment is itself monstrous. The process by which other characters identify the grotesque, the savage, or the filthy is also the process by which they manufacture “the monster.”

The deeper resemblance lies there. Victor’s creature resembles its maker but is forced to accept the world’s definition of it as an unnameable evil. Its guilt appears settled in advance: sin, Satan, death. Yet those labels belong first to Victor and the society that rejects the creature. Conscious of that rejection, it returns violence for violence and extends the tragedy. The supposed “innate filth” is thus not innate at all. It is a judgment imposed upon a body. When zombie fiction reverses the positions of the abject and the human, Frankenstein’s creature returns with the question it was never permitted to settle: who made whom monstrous?

### 2. Why Are There So Many Zombies on the Lawn?

For a long time, the zombie played the part of an emotionless monster. That role can be challenged. But before asking why so many zombies are on the lawn, we should ask another question: why did zombies so rarely seem endearing before *Plants vs. Zombies* (2009)? Games had already let players control zombies; what sort of experience did that offer?

Since *Resident Evil* (1996), video-game zombies have been tightly connected to action and shooting. Slow zombies with particular weaknesses make readable targets. The faster enemies in *Left 4 Dead* (2008) intensify the pressure, but still operate as physical obstacles that players must overcome. Communication appears impossible. The game’s tagline makes the arrangement explicit: “Curing the infection … one bullet at a time.”

Even competitive modes rarely change the structure. In *Left 4 Dead*, players control distinctive Special Infected rather than inhabiting the indistinguishable mass. They understand that they are playing the monster, but the role is largely tactical. *Plants vs. Zombies*, by contrast, turns the horde into comic individuals. Its zombies are a hybrid of Romero’s flesh-eating dead, graveyard ghouls, and the older image of the compelled laborer. They can speak, write notes, wear costumes, devise rules, and climb out of graves. None of those traits belongs to a single “original” zombie tradition.

They remain enemies, but the softened design gives them character. They still invade your garden, yet now you know that *they* know they are invading it. Thought, rules, and rudimentary communication begin to loosen the fixed image. Have they escaped the compulsion that has always made zombie bodies work? Not yet: they still march toward the house. But the joke creates the possibility of another kind of zombie, one that may eventually speak back.

### 3. “Just Another Monster”: The Future of the Fallen Other

Most zombies nevertheless remain Romero’s monsters: tireless cannibals apparently incapable of pain, fear, reverence, or negotiation. That construction is entangled with a much older colonial vocabulary. European colonizers imposed the category of “cannibal” on Indigenous Caribbean peoples and used it to distinguish supposedly redeemable “natives” from irredeemable “savages.”[^12] The distinction was never a neutral description. It helped make conquest, forced conversion, and enslavement appear legitimate.

The zombie perfects that fantasy. Colonial discourse could imagine an Indigenous person as someone to be “civilized”; the zombie is denied even that violent promise. It is cast as a “barbarian” that already confronts civilization and can only refuse it. I call this figure the *fallen Other*: a being defined as having fallen from—or failed to reach—the position occupied by the person doing the defining. It is made powerful enough to justify violence yet weak enough to be killed without remorse.

In *The Conquest of America*, Tzvetan Todorov argues that Columbus repeatedly treated Indigenous languages as an absence of language. Columbus took captives to learn Spanish and serve as interpreters, rather than recognizing an already existing field of speech. Zombie fiction repeats the structure in purified form: the zombie “cannot speak.” The colonial subject’s voice was ignored or translated into the conqueror’s terms; the zombie is designed without a voice at all. Within this fantasy, the subaltern is not merely unheard but prevented from producing any discourse that the human survivor must answer.

One might object that fictional monsters can safely be kept at a distance. There is no one-to-one correspondence between zombies and any historical people. Yet fictional images draw on existing ways of seeing. Shelley famously described a waking dream that helped inspire *Frankenstein*, but the social materials of the novel did not spring from a dream alone. In the same way, the zombie has no single real-world referent, but the category of the fallen Other has a history.

Our path from *zonbi* to “zombie” has therefore returned us to a being declared monstrous, denied a voice, and made to labor. The zombie echoes colonial images without being reducible to any one of them. Its future remains uncertain. Will it continue to serve as the constitutive outside of human progress, or can the monster cease to be an Other? I cannot promise that every hostile division will disappear. The more modest hope is that the figure might remove its mask long enough for us to see the social judgments underneath it. Perhaps that could become the zombie’s final strategy.

## Conclusion: “Planetward”—The Dawn of Monsters

Do monsters have a dawn of their own? In the real world, protesters have sometimes dressed as zombies, using the horde’s massed body to represent a multitude. Humans can play zombies—but whom do zombies play? The question takes us beyond film history, yet the discussion was never about the screen image alone. To speak of reversal and resistance is to place a hope beyond the monster.

How might those designated, insulted, and injured from the beginning reclaim their souls from the sorcerer? The *bokor* may have vanished, but the structures represented by that figure remain. The fallen Other’s dawn may lie in going “planetward”: not in consuming the world, but in overturning the division between those entitled to name and those forced to bear a name. One day even the abject may dare to say: “When the city wakes to an unpeopled dawn, this will be the dawn of monsters—and also the dawn of the multitude forced to bear the name ‘fallen.’”
