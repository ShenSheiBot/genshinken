Here I would like to take stock of the learning paths I have followed while expanding the dimensions of my appreciation and criticism of animation, and compile the resources I encountered along the way. Given my limited knowledge and abilities, what follows mostly concerns Japanese animation, or animation in a Japanese style. I offer it only for reference.

First, a disclaimer: enjoying animation or live-action films does not necessarily require an assortment of “skills in appreciation and criticism.” In primary school, I knew nothing about any of this—not even anime references—and still thoroughly enjoyed *Hayate the Combat Butler*. But some people may want to explore further dimensions of appreciation. We should also begin with some generosity toward interpretations that approach things from other directions; hence the title’s “expanding the dimensions.” No one can truly exhaust every dimension, but that does not stop us from enjoying what we watch. Nor are these abilities useful only for Japanese animation. So, to learn and regularly put what we learn into practice—is that not a pleasure? Enough about justifying the title. Let’s get to the point.

## 1. Industry-Side Knowledge of Animation Production (a Heading I Just Made Up)

![A production meeting in Shirobako](attachments/roof-archive/cv2318316/02-shirobako-meeting.jpg)

Understanding how animation is made matters to our grasp of its characteristics as an artistic medium and of the styles of the people who create it. If you do not know the basics of animation production, you can easily make a fool of yourself as a critic. These are often matters of agreement within the community of creators—that is, the animation industry—and critics should respect that knowledge. This information is also an important source of material for arts criticism.

For many people, watching *Shirobako* sparks an interest in this knowledge. What next? I began with introductory video lectures on drawn animation, or *sakuga*, by two pioneers I greatly respect:

- [An Introduction to the Basics of Japanese-Style Animation: Complete Version](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV17s411D75e)
- [Animation Lecture: Japanese-Style Animation and the Animators Behind It (2013 Version)](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1Qs411o7w8)
- [One Video to Explain Exactly How Animation Is Made—Anitama’s New-Series Analysis Special, Episode 1](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1jb411e75F)

After that, I kept building my knowledge and experience by regularly watching Anitama’s sakuga showcase compilations, or MADs (and keeping up with its [*Jie* (“Explained”)](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1Sx411v79r/) and *Xinsheng* (“New Voices”) features), reading translated articles from Western sakuga blogs, and so on. So far, though, I can only claim a rudimentary understanding.

Beyond drawn animation, there is also direction:

All of this is very helpful for appreciating and writing criticism of animation.

## 2. Visual Art and the Philosophy of Art

Animation is a distinctive art form, and we need to treat its particular characteristics with care. Conversely, exploring the wider artistic world will help us understand what distinguishes animation and where it fits within art’s broader categories. There are all sorts of art, of course. Apart from the live-action films discussed below, let’s single out visual art here.

[fig] The composition in *Revue Starlight* is influenced by Russian Constructivist art.

![Composition in Revue Starlight and the influence of Russian Constructivism](attachments/roof-archive/cv2318316/06-revue-starlight-composition.jpg)

Of course, most people working in animation are not trained art historians. But we should remember a familiar point: when people make art, they are always influenced, consciously or otherwise, by the general spirit of their age and by past and present society and history. That is enough of a defense. My own knowledge of art history, which I began studying last year, is actually very shallow. Here are a few resources I like:

## 3. Philosophy and the Social Sciences

Generally speaking, ideas from these fields are used to analyze both what lies within the text of an animated work and what lies beyond it—and a “text” need not consist of written words.

What? You can do that too? Seeing is believing (?), so here are a few articles that show what animation criticism along this dimension can do:

- [Toward an Analysis of Relational Form: Love Triangles in Erciyuan Culture and “Byakuyakō”](/books/relational-form-analysis/chapters/love-triangle-biyakuya)[^erciyuan]
- [The Parallax View | An Essay on the Ontological Aesthetics of Erciyuan Culture—The Return of the Sacred](/posts/parallax-view-ontology-aesthetics-sacred-return)

Hang on, you say—how could the creator possibly have thought of all that…? Creators’ own ideas deserve respect; the author does not leave the stage. But once a text comes into being, it has relative independence and multiple meanings. And, as I said at the beginning, the information creators convey remains an important source of material for criticism, but not the only one. Whether an interpretation is any good must, of course, be tested by fellow critics, readers, and history.

What an enormous field of study this is… Everyone has to choose areas that interest them and suit their abilities. I am only an ordinary soul who started late and has put in too little work. All I can say is: study hard and improve every day. Given this article’s partly introductory purpose, here are some introductory works on literary theory, the history of sociological thought, and the history of philosophy, respectively:

## 4. Film Theory and Film History (Leaving Television Drama Aside for Now)

As the most popular product of mass culture, film is an important part of social and cultural life. Animation creators may be influenced by cinematic techniques in how they work—for example, in storyboarding and direction. To heighten realism, they might imitate a live-action shot with shallow depth of field, blurring the distant background to simulate out-of-focus bokeh. Animation does not produce this effect automatically—though, conversely, its use in live action need not be unintentional either.

![A Shurou Zatan video explains realism through an animated shot](attachments/roof-archive/cv2318316/15-realism-analysis.jpg)

[“Sound! Euphonium: In Search of the Most Romantic Realism” (Bilibili search)](https://search.bilibili.com/all?keyword=%E7%86%9F%E8%82%89%E6%9D%82%E8%B0%88%20%E4%BA%AC%E5%90%B9%20%E8%BF%BD%E5%AF%BB%E6%9C%80%E6%B5%AA%E6%BC%AB%E7%9A%84%E7%8E%B0%E5%AE%9E%E4%B8%BB%E4%B9%89)

We must be careful here! Animation and live-action film are two different arts. If we mechanically transplant film theory into animation criticism, it probably will not travel well. Yet only after becoming familiar with both can we make specific judgments about whether borrowing a concept is appropriate in a particular situation. Beyond creative techniques, it scarcely needs saying that animation creators, as a group within society, are influenced by the content and styles of celebrated films and filmmakers. This is part of the wider interplay of influences throughout society.

## 5. Japan, Japanese Animation History, and More

If we are discussing Japanese animation, the enormous usefulness of knowing Japan’s social history surely needs no defense. For example, the sarcastic remarks of Hachiman—the fans’ “Great Teacher”—often contain references to Japanese local culture. His story and that of a certain teenage “Rascal” from [*Rascal Does Not Dream of Bunny Girl Senpai*](https://aniplexusa.com/pdf/PR_092218_Fall2018.pdf) are also unmistakably steeped in Japanese school culture:

![Three characters from My Teen Romantic Comedy SNAFU](attachments/roof-archive/cv2318316/18-oregairu-trio.jpg)

Another example is *Concrete Revolutio*, a lovingly made, underappreciated series from Bones:

![An animated sequence from Concrete Revolutio](attachments/roof-archive/cv2318316/19-concrete-revolutio.gif)

Through metaphor, this series practically tells the greater part of Japan’s history in the second half of the twentieth century, mixed with a great many references to *tokusatsu*, Japan’s live-action special-effects tradition. Anitama has a series of articles exploring this in depth. Without some knowledge of Japanese social history and tokusatsu, viewers will recognize much less of what the creators want to communicate through the work. (After all that, I really just wanted to recommend *Concrete Revolutio*.)

There are many other versions of animation history, of course. I am only talking about the ones I know. Animation history naturally leads us back to watching animation itself. Suppose we want to review Director A’s new work: familiarity with Director A’s earlier works gives us a grasp of their distinctive style, and some expectations and knowledge to bring to the new one. I generally follow four approaches in my animation viewing:

- The auteur approach: follow the directors, episode directors, series-composition writers—who oversee the overall script structure—and source creators I like.
- The studio approach: follow the animation studios I like.
- The genre approach: follow the genres I like. Tokusatsu, for example—wait, that doesn’t count, does it?
- The ratings approach: find things to watch through rankings drawn from large datasets and the recommendation roundups based on them. One famous example:
  - [Data Updated July 2021: The All-Time Top 500 Japanese Anime by Reputation—Combined Ratings from Chinese, Japanese, and US Websites; Recommendations for Catch-Up Viewing](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV18x411T7UT)

## 6. Learning from Others’ Practice and Trying It Yourself

If sections 1 through 5 are about building up a reserve of ideas and knowledge, section 6 is **watching others put them to work and having a go yourself**.

I have done quite a bit of editing, layout, and publication work on articles released by Lab on Roof, discussing their critical content with the writers. It is work, but it has also let me read many pieces of criticism more closely and attentively, and learn a great deal, both in knowledge and in critical methods.

Here I would particularly like to applaud two articles:

- [Animation Metacriticism I.i: On the Ebbing of the Sublime and Private Experience, with *Zombieland Saga* and VTubers as Examples](/books/meta-animation-criticism/chapters/i-i-sublime-tide)

Its conceptual analysis gave me a clearer understanding of what animation criticism should describe.

> It is easy to imagine that when a critic writes, “Such-and-such a work/plot development/shot creates a vivid character,” without further clarification, readers’ ideas about why the character is “vivid” may differ from the ideas the critic has left unclear.
>
> Yet in practice, this difficulty does not seem to arise—at least from an ordinary, commonsense point of view. If we are not to abandon our everyday confidence in animation criticism, we must answer the following question:
>
> (P) When an animation critic writes, “Such-and-such a work/plot development/shot creates a vivid character,” how can readers understand what the critic means?

[fig] “Optimal” criticism offers both unfamiliar perspectives and some depth. It conveys the greatest amount of useful information to readers.

## 7. Among Any Three People, I Can Find a Teacher

Human beings—there is only so much one person can do… What I mean is that everyone has different strengths and specialisms. Being good at everything is difficult, perhaps even unnecessary. Coming together to learn, raise doubts, and question our reasoning is therefore another good way to learn and improve.

So there you have my modest learning experience, **one thing mentioned, ten thousand left out**, as I have **persisted in “expanding the dimensions of animation appreciation and criticism.”** Honestly, now that I am nearly finished, I feel quite uneasy. What if someone who really knows their stuff points out a serious flaw? What if I lead beginners astray?

Still, I am willing to take those risks, to embarrass myself by taking stock of the routes—and detours—I have followed and laying them out here. If any of you can benefit, nothing could be better. **And if you can put my meager achievements beneath your feet and stride beyond them**, isn’t that unsparing act of surpassing me precisely what I most hope to see? All I can do is gear up again: study, study, and study some more, and struggle to catch up with you all between bouts of slacking off.

Let me close with words from Weber’s lecture:[^weber]

> Every scientific “completion” brings new questions; science asks to be surpassed, to be shown inferior by comparison. Anyone who wishes to devote themselves to science must face this fact… The constant overtaking and surpassing that takes place in science, let me repeat, is not only the fate of each of us but our common goal. We cannot work without wanting others to do better than we have. In principle, this progress has no end. Here we come to the question of the meaning of science.

(In German, “science” probably means something like systematic knowledge; hence the sciences of history, the humanities, and art.)
