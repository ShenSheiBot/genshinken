> **Abstract:** In isekai reincarnation, a protagonist dies in one world and is reborn in another. The premise became a major current in Japanese light novels as user-posted fiction flourished on sites such as Shōsetsuka ni Narō. Much commentary dismisses these works as commodities that gratify *otaku* fantasies, but that approach rarely asks what the stories themselves make of death, work, failure, and starting over. Why must the protagonist die rather than travel to another world alive? Does rebirth imagine a life beyond coercive labor and competitive success, or does it merely offer the same contest with better starting conditions? This essay follows that question through Japanese fantasy and isekai, then sets reincarnation beside time-reset stories and a strand of Chinese transmigration fiction.

## I. From Fantasy to Isekai

I first began to think of isekai as a problem in its own right while following a Chinese internet argument usually framed as “web fiction versus Japanese light novels.” The categories are not parallel. *Web fiction* names a medium of publication, while *light novel* names an illustrated mass-market publishing category associated mainly with teenage and young-adult readers. Yet the argument treated them as national teams in a race. Depending on the speaker, priority proved superiority—Chinese web fiction had sent heroes to other worlds and given them extraordinary “cheat” powers first—or a longer publishing history and larger professional output proved superiority, which favored Japanese light novels. In either case, literary value became a matter of who arrived first or whose industry had produced more.

The same dispute generated a second hierarchy: “good” isekai invents an original world, while “bad” isekai borrows the conventions of video games. From there it is a short step to the story of decline: fantasy was once good, but contemporary isekai has become derivative and crude. That complaint points toward a better question. What distinguishes the isekai boom of the 2010s from the fantasy and other-world stories that came before it? What, exactly, changed?

One answer concerns who could write and circulate fiction. Ao Jyumonji makes the importance of games to his own career explicit in the afterword to Volume 1 of *Grimgar of Fantasy and Ash*:

> *Dragon Quest*, *Wizardry*, *Final Fantasy*, *Megami Tensei*, *Metal Max*, *Romancing SaGa*, *Breath of Fire*, *Live A Live*, *Chrono Trigger*, *Arc the Lad*, *Tactics Ogre*, *Suikoden*, the *Tales* series, *Wild Arms*, *Final Fantasy Tactics*, *Star Ocean*, *Atelier Marie*, *SaGa Frontier*, *Xenogears* … Console RPGs like these, or games containing strong RPG elements, were always a kind of salvation in my life.
>
> […]
>
> People sometimes ask why I started writing novels. I think games were probably the biggest influence. If I had never encountered RPGs, I almost certainly would never have begun writing fiction. In particular, if I had not become so absorbed in online RPGs, my debut work *Bara no Maria* could not have come into being, and neither could this book. And if I had never read novels built around role-playing games—Ryō Mizuno’s *Record of Lodoss War* or Benny Matsuyama’s *Tonari Awase no Hai to Seishun*, for example—then even if I had somehow become a novelist, I might not have written the novels I write now.

—Ao Jyumonji, afterword to *Grimgar of Fantasy and Ash*, Vol. 1[^1]

Jyumonji did not simply borrow the surface furniture of an RPG. Games formed part of his education as a novelist. As social media and fiction-posting platforms lowered the barrier to publication, more readers steeped in fantasy and RPGs could become writers. The uneven craft of user-posted fiction is real enough, but it cannot by itself explain the new form. Nor can “gamification”: Japanese popular fiction had been circulating among novels, games, manga, and animation for decades.

Eiji Ōtsuka’s *How to Make Character Novels* divides light novels into three broad lineages: youth literature that predates the 1970s, the girls’ novels that emerged in the 1980s, and “game-like novels” shaped by tabletop role-playing games in that same decade. A tabletop role-playing game, or TRPG, is a collaborative game in which participants play characters and produce a story through rules, conversation, and chance. Hiroki Azuma’s key example of game-like fiction is *Record of Lodoss War*—one of the fantasy works that nostalgic accounts often place on the respectable side of the fantasy/isekai divide:

> *Record of Lodoss War* began as a “replay”: a serialized record, published in a specialist magazine, of a group playing a well-known tabletop role-playing game. The creative group Group SNE produced the replay; the project was planned by the group’s representative, and the novelist Ryō Mizuno was one of its members. The serialization proved highly popular, but Group SNE did not own the game on which it was based. The group therefore novelized the replay while also adapting its setting into an original tabletop RPG. The novel became *Record of Lodoss War*; the game followed in 1989 as *Record of Lodoss War Companion*.

—Hiroki Azuma, *The Birth of Game-Like Realism: Animalizing Postmodernity 2*[^2]

This is a media genealogy, not a claim that every later isekai plot reproduces a TRPG session. A replay records people playing characters; a transfer story usually sends a character into another world as the same person. The connection lies instead in a shared imaginative infrastructure. Fantasy supplied worlds and roles to games; games made those worlds participatory; and writers raised on that participation carried game-like settings, progression, and reward systems back into fiction. None of this yet tells us why entry into another world would come to require death.

## II. Four Ways of Starting Over

The word *isekai* simply means “another world.” I will use it as an umbrella for stories in which another world is central, not as a synonym for reincarnation. Four related mechanisms need to remain distinct:

- **Living transfer** (*ten’i*) carries the same living person into another world. The protagonist keeps their body and social identity, and return may remain possible.

- **Isekai reincarnation** (*tensei*) begins with death in the original world. The protagonist is reborn with a new body, age, and social position, though memory and point of view may continue across lives.

- **Time reset** sends a protagonist back to an earlier point in the same life and world, usually with foreknowledge. It requires neither another world nor rebirth.

- **Transmigration**, as I use the English term for a broad family of Chinese web-fiction premises, moves a person or consciousness across a world, body, or period. It is an umbrella rather than a single mechanism: a transmigration story may resemble transfer, reincarnation, possession, or time reset. It therefore cannot serve as a national synonym for Japanese isekai reincarnation.

Shōsetsuka ni Narō, the user-posted fiction site closely associated with the isekai boom, distinguishes reincarnation and transfer in its keywords. That distinction gives us a more precise historical claim than “fantasy turned into isekai.” Among the works central to this essay, the prominent 1990s and 2000s precedents use living transfer: *The Twelve Kingdoms*, *Magic Knight Rayearth*, *The Familiar of Zero*, and *Kyo Kara Maoh!*. Their protagonists cross into another world without first ending one life and beginning another. This is not an exhaustive history of Japanese fantasy, and death-and-rebirth plots certainly exist outside this selection. What changes in the later reincarnation current is the prominence of a complete biographical restart.

*Record of Lodoss War* offers the fantasy of inhabiting a heroic role in a game-like world. Transfer isekai turns that imaginative participation into a plot: a person from the ordinary world enters the fantastic one alive. Reincarnation goes further. The protagonist does not merely enter the adventure but begins an entire life there. The subtitle of perhaps the most influential example, *Mushoku Tensei: Jobless Reincarnation*, states the promise plainly: *Isekai Ittara Honki Dasu*, or “If I Go to Another World, I’ll Get Serious.” The rough sequence looks like this:

> Fantasy draws worlds from myth, history, and folklore.<br>
> ↓<br>
> TRPGs and video-game RPGs turn fantasy worlds into spaces for participation.<br>
> ↓<br>
> Game-influenced fiction carries settings, roles, progression, and reward systems back into novels.<br>
> ↓<br>
> Living-transfer isekai sends the protagonist into such a world as the same person.<br>
> ↓<br>
> Isekai reincarnation makes that entry the beginning of a whole new biography.

Death changes the relation between the two worlds. In a living-transfer story, the protagonist can still imagine going home. The pull between ordinary life and adventure often drives the plot, as it does in *The Familiar of Zero*. *Kyo Kara Maoh!* makes repeated movement between worlds a continuing source of conflict. Even when return is difficult, the old life has not been declared over.

Several adjacent genres clarify this point without collapsing into isekai. In school-supernatural stories, an encounter with danger may nearly kill the protagonist, awaken a hidden power, or draw them into a previously invisible struggle. Yet home, school, and the supernatural realm usually remain entangled; the plot changes ordinary life rather than replacing it. *Sword Art Online* is another boundary case because its virtual game spaces function like other worlds. Analytically, however, its premise remains closer to transfer than reincarnation: the protagonist enters as the same person, and the possibility of leaving still matters. Death can appear in all these stories. What distinguishes isekai reincarnation is not death as spectacle but death as a one-way door. It closes the old biography and makes the other world the only life left to live.

That is why *Mushoku Tensei* spends an entire chapter approaching its protagonist’s death. The thirty-four-year-old unemployed *otaku*—a deeply invested fan of anime, manga, and games—has been evicted from his family home. He dies violently, “crushed like a tomato between the truck and the concrete wall.” The ugliness matters. Rebirth is not an exciting detour from an ordinary life waiting safely in the background. It follows the destruction of a life that can no longer be resumed.

Azuma’s “game-like realism” asks how a game system can generate multiple possible stories; works such as *All You Need Is Kill* and *Ever17* make repetition visible by killing or resetting their protagonists again and again. My concern here is the opposite structure: one irreversible death that ends the possibility of another attempt in the original world.

## III. What Death Makes Possible

After reincarnation, continuity survives through memory, but almost everything that fixes a person’s place in society has changed: body, age, family, status, and world. Why is that rupture necessary? Could the protagonist not recover in the life he already has? Coming-of-age stories and novels about young adults in crisis are built on exactly that possibility. *Mushoku Tensei* refuses it.

Soon after his rebirth, the protagonist tells himself:

> Yeah. This isn’t bad. I’m far too old to be this excited, but how could I not be? I’ve been reborn with my memories intact in a world like this. Anyone who couldn’t get excited by that would never have wound up a NEET. All right. I’ve decided: I’m going to take this life seriously. I’m going all out, so I never have regrets again.

—*Mushoku Tensei: Isekai Ittara Honki Dasu*, web version, Chapter 1[^3]

NEET means “not in education, employment, or training,” a bureaucratic label that popular usage often turns into a judgment on the person it describes. The line does not tell us that a dream of reincarnation caused the protagonist to withdraw. His withdrawal followed severe bullying at school. What the joke does reveal is the association he makes between two parts of himself: the susceptibility that led him to retreat from society and the intense attraction to fictional, or “2D,” worlds of anime, manga, and games. Fantasy did not cause his exclusion. It became the place where a life narrowed by exclusion could still expand.

The novel also refuses to make him secretly competent at navigating ordinary institutions. After his parents die and he is thrown out, he tries to imagine looking for work. The result is a grimly comic chain of partial knowledge, shame, and defeatist speculation. The physical details are deliberately degrading:

> I don’t know how to look for a job. Well, I know, more or less, that I’m supposed to go to Hello Work. But I didn’t spend more than ten years shut in my room for nothing. Of course I have no idea where the nearest office is. And from what I’ve heard, Hello Work only refers you to an employer. Then I would have to take a résumé there and sit for an interview. An interview in these clothes, with the sleeves crusted stiff with jizz and blood spattered here and there? No chance. I would never hire a guy who showed up looking this crazy. I might feel for him, but I definitely wouldn’t hire him. I don’t even know what kind of shop sells blank résumé forms. A stationery store? A convenience store? I might find a convenience store if I walked long enough, but I don’t have any money. Fine—suppose I cleared all those hurdles. Suppose I somehow got lucky and borrowed money from a bank or somewhere, bought fresh clothes, and picked up a résumé form and something to write with. I’ve heard you can’t fill out a résumé without an address. Game over. At that point I realized my life had become completely unwinnable.

—*Mushoku Tensei: Isekai Ittara Honki Dasu*, web version, prologue[^4]

Hello Work is Japan’s public employment service. The protagonist knows just enough to foresee the sequence of gates between him and a job: find the office, obtain presentable clothes and a résumé, secure an address, survive an interview. He does not know how to pass those gates, and he lacks money, housing, confidence, and practical familiarity with the system. Worse, he has learned to see himself through the imagined employer’s eyes. Before anyone rejects him, he performs the rejection himself. The passage dramatizes neither mastery nor simple ignorance. He recognizes the terms of inclusion while being unable to meet them.

Had he found shelter, employment, and a way back into social life, the result might resemble the hopeful young-adult fiction associated here with Hiro Arikawa: stories in which people knocked off course recover through persistence and human connection. *Mushoku Tensei* instead makes the predicament terminal. The protagonist dies while trying to save someone else and awakens in a world of swords and magic. Death does not force him to work hard, and it cannot prevent another withdrawal. What it does is remove return as an option. The old institutions that shamed and excluded him no longer control access to food, shelter, respect, or adulthood.

The new world is also legible to him in a way the old one was not. It resembles the games and “2D” fiction he already understands: abilities can be practiced, progress can be observed, and effort tends to produce recognizable results. In game-derived slang, a “cheat” is an ability so powerful that it seems to break the ordinary balance of play. Such gifts do not abolish work; they make the relation between work and reward unusually clear. Rudeus’s resolve to “get serious” therefore contains an ambiguity. He may be learning to care for people and pursue a life he values. He may also have found a meritocracy finally calibrated to let him win.

That distinction matters because *work* has several meanings here. Coercive labor is work imposed under conditions that destroy the worker, as an office does to Azusa Aizawa in *I’ve Been Killing Slimes for 300 Years and Maxed Out My Level*. Exclusion from work is Rudeus’s position at the end of his first life: employment remains the price of social existence, yet he cannot gain entry. Self-directed routine is something else again. After Azusa literally dies from overwork, a goddess reincarnates her as an ageless, immortal witch. She chooses a modest daily practice—killing a fixed number of slimes—and builds a quiet life around it. Her extraordinary ability makes that life secure, but its value lies in sufficiency rather than promotion.

Azusa’s death is thus necessary in two separate senses. It is first a biological fact: her employer works her to death. It is also the story’s way of removing her from a social order that recognizes only one respectable model of adult labor. The banking drama *Hanzawa Naoki* offers a harsher version of that institutional judgment when an unwanted outside posting is treated as the end of a banker’s career. To leave the hierarchy is to become professionally invisible. Azusa’s reincarnation does not reject effort, discipline, or routine. It changes who controls them, what they are for, and how much is enough.

This is why “escape the city” or “return to the countryside” cannot quite perform the same work in the argument. Those plots may offer distance from an employer or a metropolis, but not necessarily from a society that equates a worthwhile life with rank, income, and ceaseless advancement. Another world allows a more complete separation. Yet that freedom remains only a possibility. If the new world merely makes advancement fairer, faster, or easier for the protagonist, reincarnation has preserved the old standard of success.

## IV. Restarting under the Same Rules

Time-reset stories expose this ambiguity because they grant a second chance without leaving the original society. *Remake Our Life!* follows a twenty-eight-year-old who dreams of making games. He enters the industry after studying an unrelated subject but never receives the chance to prove himself. When his employer goes bankrupt, he returns to his family home in despair and wakes ten years earlier, at the moment when he chose a university. Armed with foreknowledge, he enrolls at an arts university and pursues the career he missed the first time.

The spin-off *Remake Our Life! Ver.β*, in which he never travels back in time, belongs to a familiar kind of inspirational fiction: the protagonist must keep faith with his aspirations and struggle within the conditions he has inherited. The time-reset premise does something different. It does not challenge the institutions that sort winners from losers; it sends the protagonist back to make the correct choices sooner. In the terms of this essay, that makes it a still more troubling—and purer—restart fantasy than reincarnation. The hero does not need a different account of the good life. He needs foreknowledge and another run at the same goal.

A recognizable strand of Chinese web fiction makes this restart structure especially explicit. Here *transmigration* is the broad category defined above, not a specifically Chinese version of isekai reincarnation. Across *xuanhuan*—fantasy built from Chinese mythic, martial, and cosmological materials—and *qihuan*—fantasy more visibly indebted to Western-derived settings and conventions—as well as some historical and urban stories, transmigration can give the protagonist knowledge, status, or powers unavailable to everyone else. The world may look radically different while preserving a familiar measure of success: the protagonist climbs, dominates rivals, accumulates resources, and reaches the top.

This claim concerns a recurrent structure, not “Chinese web fiction” as a whole and still less a national character. The Japanese works discussed here do not all escape that structure either. Rudeus receives a world he can read and capacities he can develop; Azusa receives immortality and overwhelming power. The decisive question is not where a story was written but what the second chance is for. Does it release the protagonist from a life organized by ranking, or merely reverse their position within the ranking?

The “restart” and “be reborn” memes that circulated online in China give that desire a blunt everyday vocabulary. Their appeal need not prove a single cause or represent every user. They do, however, name a fantasy produced by stratification: perhaps the hierarchy itself is immovable, and only one’s starting position can change. The danger lies not in wanting another chance but in accepting the premise that only the strong, successful, or properly ranked deserve an ordinary life.

Game language makes the structure easy to picture. In *League of Legends*, `/remake` calls a vote to restart a match when a player has disconnected. The command is a useful analogy, not evidence that every restart story descends from the game: nullify the failed opening and begin again before the result counts. Reincarnation can do more than that, because death can remove the protagonist from the old contest altogether. But it can also turn another world into the same match with kinder matchmaking and better statistics.

## Conclusion: A New Life, or an Easier Meritocracy?

The formal power of death in isekai reincarnation is modest but decisive. Death does not redeem the protagonist, compel effort, or guarantee a better society. It forecloses return. Living transfer remains an excursion, however long; time reset replays the same biography; transmigration may use several different mechanisms. Reincarnation alone makes the other world the protagonist’s whole remaining life.

That break can support a genuine refusal. Azusa does not stop working; she escapes labor that consumes her and chooses a routine governed by sufficiency. Her new life changes the purpose and ownership of effort. Rudeus presents the harder case. His second world frees him from the institutional dead end of his first, gives him intelligible paths to action, and lets his abilities produce visible results. Yet those advantages may amount to meritocracy repaired for one exceptional person rather than meritocracy transcended.

Isekai reincarnation, then, does not escape the work ethic or competitive success by definition. It creates the space in which a story might do so. Its most revealing question is not whether the protagonist receives a cheat, works hard, or wins. It is whether rebirth changes what counts as a life worth living. If the answer is still power, status, and victory, death has only restarted the old contest under more favorable rules. If the new life makes room for chosen effort, reciprocal ties, and the right to have enough without defeating anyone, then the one-way door opens onto something genuinely new.
