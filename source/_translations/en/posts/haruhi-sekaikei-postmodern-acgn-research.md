## 1 Is the Haruhi Series Sekaikei? Yes—and No

We are forever attaching labels to works, on the theory that classification will reveal what different works have in common.[^2] One label often attached to the Haruhi series is *sekaikei* (セカイ系). The term emerged from online criticism and has never had a single settled definition. A widely repeated minimal account, associated with Hiroki Azuma, describes stories in which the intimate “you and me” relationship is connected directly to an abstract crisis of the world, without concrete social institutions mediating between the two.[^3] In *The Birth of Gamelike Realism* (*Gēmuteki riarizumu no tanjō*, subtitled *The Animalizing Postmodern 2*), Azuma includes *The Melancholy of Haruhi Suzumiya* among works displaying features of the “sekaikei character novel.”[^54]

Satoshi Maejima’s history *What Is Sekaikei?* (*Sekaikei to wa nani ka*) shows how unstable the category has been. Some accounts stress only the missing social “middle”; others add a stronger claim that nation and society have been replaced by the protagonists’ feelings, or that the imagined world remains thin because it serves mainly to magnify the central relationship.[^4] Those are related ideas, not interchangeable rules. In this essay, *sekaikei* is therefore a **working critical label**, not a universal genre definition. Its minimal structural core is the direct link between intimacy and world crisis without social mediation. The stronger claim—that the world is abstract and subordinated to the relationship—will be treated as a historical and interpretive tendency, not a necessary condition every critic accepts.

When critics say “Haruhi is sekaikei,” title a review of the *Disappearance* film “A Sekaikei Love Story,”[^6] or call Nagaru Tanigawa “an author with an extremely strong sekaikei consciousness,”[^7] they may mean different points within that range. My question is not whether one rigid definition can police them all. It is whether the Haruhi series directs readers toward the cluster of relations that made the label useful in the first place.

One purpose of this essay is to decide whether Haruhi really is sekaikei. Yutaka Yamamoto reportedly addressed the question at Otakon 2009: “I don’t want to go deep into the definition of sekaikei, but Tanigawa holds that it [Haruhi] is not sekaikei. The SOS Brigade is a concept opposed to sekaikei; it stands between the real and the illusory and adds a distinctive seasoning to the story. I agree with the view that Haruhi is not sekaikei.”[^8] Yet even if this accurately represents Tanigawa’s view, authorial intent alone will not persuade readers who classify the series otherwise. Some might also argue that the novelist’s opinion has little bearing on the anime. As we shall see, however, the novels and anime do not differ greatly on this particular question.

For the sake of analysis, I will ask three questions. The first and third track the minimal structural account; the second tests the stronger interpretation just described:

(1) Does the story center on the relationship between the protagonists?

(2) Does “the world”—usually a crisis threatening it—serve mainly as a vehicle for dramatizing that relationship, rather than as a concrete object of inquiry in its own right? Some definitions describe sekaikei settings as comparatively undeveloped; a hostile version complains that the composition “aliens invade, the world is destroyed” matters more than the aliens, society’s response, or the path to destruction.[^9][^10]

(3) Does the story leave out the social institutions that mediate between the individual and “the world”—in the usual shorthand, is it “desocialized”?

These three conditions correspond to the domains of individual, society, and world. Critics have in turn aligned those domains with Lacan’s Imaginary, Symbolic, and Real,[^11] a correspondence to which I will return.

Among the twelve Japanese volumes available when this essay first appeared in June 2021, several stories seem, at first glance, to satisfy all three tests: “The Boredom of Haruhi Suzumiya” (here and below, italics distinguish the volume *The Boredom* from its title story), the climax of *The Melancholy*, all of *The Disappearance*, and “Endless Eight.”[^56] The title story of *The Boredom* looks like the clearest example. Haruhi is disappointed by Kyon’s performance in a baseball game—the small relational problem—and that disappointment immediately causes closed space to expand. The threat to the world remains abstract: unlike *The Melancholy*, the story never depicts the closed space itself. Nagato’s powers, rather than any social institution, finally resolve the danger. Summarized at this level, the other three stories appear to fit just as neatly.

All four, however, belong to the series’ early phase. Of these four works, “The Boredom of Haruhi Suzumiya” and *The Melancholy* appeared first, followed by “Endless Eight”; *The Disappearance*, the last of the group, was published in August 2004, barely a year after *The Melancholy*. If we use *The Disappearance* as a provisional dividing line, most later stories no longer fit the three conditions.

First, a crisis on the scale of the world never again seems to occupy the foreground. The danger in “Snowy Mountain Syndrome” is confined to a mountain lodge and the members of the SOS Brigade. The student council’s attempt to reclaim the clubroom in “Editor in Chief, Full Speed Ahead!” and Asahina’s kidnapping in *The Intrigues* are still further from the end of the world.

One might object that *The Dissociation* and *The Surprise* still threaten the world. Near the convergence of the two narrative branches in *The Surprise* (Japanese Part Two), Koizumi explains that Haruhi unconsciously split reality into a world that should exist and one that should not, thereby protecting the former.[^57] Yasumi Watahashi disputes even this description: the two branches have slightly different histories but belong to the same time and world. More importantly, Tanigawa dramatizes three immediate dangers for the reader: Nagato falls ill, Asakura returns, and Haruhi’s power may pass to Sasaki. None is presented as a crisis for the world itself. Koizumi tells us that the world has split, but the narrative neither depicts that split on a global scale nor makes the split itself the problem, as “Endless Eight” does with its time loop. Under the stronger working account used here, the divided timelines therefore do not function as a sekaikei “world crisis.”

Second, the characters’ relationships are not the story’s only center of gravity. The series also investigates its world in its own right, steadily developing the incompatible cosmologies of the aliens, time travelers, and espers. “Wandering Shadow” expands the alien theory; *The Intrigues* does the same for time travel; and although the espers receive less space, *The Surprise* introduces Gnosticism, their theory’s greatest development since *The Sigh*. I discuss this further in “A Hymn to Humanity: A Framework for Reading *The Dissociation of Haruhi Suzumiya* and *The Surprise*.”[^12]

Tanigawa devotes sustained attention to the rules governing this world. What natural or supernatural laws does it obey? *The Surprise* brings that question to a head when the older Asahina and Fujiwara debate whether the future can be rewritten. The world is therefore not an abstract stage on which a crisis happens; discovering its structure is one of the series’ central projects. By contrast, the Tarsians and UN Space Army in *Voices of a Distant Star*, a canonical sekaikei work, function much more like abstract devices that cause and answer a crisis.

Finally, even “desocialization,” ordinarily the easiest condition to meet, becomes difficult to sustain. The Tamaru brothers drive a police car in *The Intrigues*; in “Editor in Chief, Full Speed Ahead!,” the Agency spends lavishly to install the student council president. “Tsuruya’s Challenge” goes further by detailing the wealth, influence, and research investments of T’s and Tsuruya’s families. Capital moves from the background to the foreground, suggesting an economic force alongside the alien, time-traveler, and esper factions. The fan story “Four Moments in Haruhi” is an entertaining elaboration of this possibility, but not canonical evidence for it.[^13] One anonymous commenter has similarly argued that the mayoral father in *Your Name* supplies enough social mediation to complicate that film’s classification.[^14] I do not take one comment as a general rule; it simply shows that the same boundary dispute arises elsewhere.

“Fine, you have a point. But on your division, we could perfectly well say that early Haruhi is sekaikei and later Haruhi is not; and since this division almost coincides with the division between ‘already animated’ and ‘not yet animated,’ why not keep it simple and just claim that the Haruhi anime is sekaikei and the novels are not!”

This is a serious countermove. Later installments cannot erase the formal structure of an earlier story. At most, they can invite us to reread what that structure was doing. My claim that Haruhi was never straightforwardly sekaikei is therefore an interpretive hypothesis, not a result forced by chronology.

Consider the film-production chapters of *The Sigh*, unquestionably an early work and one adapted for television. Asahina warns Kyon not to trust Koizumi’s account of Haruhi as creator of the world: in her view, Haruhi can alter the present, but did not create the world and cannot change its underlying structure. She carefully adds that this theoretical disagreement is not personal hostility toward Koizumi.[^15] Later, Nagato tells Kyon that no one can guarantee Koizumi is telling the truth. These scenes are readily located in the second half of the novel, during and immediately after the brigade’s filming of *The Adventures of Mikuru Asahina*.[^58]

From these two passages we can draw three conclusions:

(1) The aliens, time travelers, and espers offer genuinely incompatible theories, not merely different vocabularies for the same idea. In *The Melancholy*, their accounts of the world and Haruhi’s power may initially sound like three ways of saying much the same thing. *The Sigh* makes clear that the time travelers’ and espers’ positions, at least, cannot both be accepted. Indeed, at most one can be true: Kyon is not satisfied by any coherent or pragmatically useful account of what happened to him. He wants the correct answer.[^16]

(2) Tanigawa does not ask us to reduce this theoretical conflict to a device for expressing strained relationships. Kyon and Asahina’s conversation carefully separates disagreement over the world from personal hostility. Both kinds of conflict exist, but neither is merely the surface expression of the other’s supposed “essence” or “core.”[^17]

(3) It follows that the theories are more than background lore. Tanigawa gives us three rival accounts and asks which, if any, describes the world as it really is. This is not inert “setting”; it is the series’ deepest mystery, the final riddle posed to the reader.

Here lies the basic difference between Haruhi’s world and the world of a typical sekaikei story under my stronger working account. Haruhi never gives us its world directly or transparently, nor does it treat that world as a neutral device. Its nature must be discovered. A serious reading therefore cannot focus only on the characters’ inner lives and relationships while dismissing the world around them. Under the minimal structural definition, some early Haruhi stories can still reasonably be called sekaikei. My stronger claim is that the series repeatedly redirects attention from the intimate/world-crisis circuit toward the world’s independent structure, so that the label becomes an incomplete guide rather than an exhaustive description. A reader who insists that its world is only “a foil that carries the protagonists so that they may exist”[^18] will miss a substantial part of the series.

(One further clue lies in the series’ deliberately atypical aliens and espers. In *The Surprise* (Japanese Part One), Sasaki contrasts the communicative humanoid aliens of familiar fiction with the Data Overmind and Sky Canopy Dominion, entities that do not fit those reassuring conventions. In *The Melancholy*, Kyon asks Koizumi to prove his esper power with a simple demonstration, only to learn that it works under highly restricted conditions. The labels “alien” and “esper” therefore pose questions about what these beings are; they are not merely reusable *moe* elements, or familiar character traits designed to elicit affectionate fan attachment.[^59]

In fact, the “atypical” in the Haruhi series is a topic that deserves an essay of its own; for reasons of space I will not expand on it here.)

With that in mind, return to the opening of *The Melancholy*. Kyon recalls wanting to be swept into battles involving aliens, time travelers, monsters, and espers, while imagining that a gifted transfer student or secret organization would do the real work. The fantasy already treats those genre labels as convenient packages and gently mocks the ordinary observer who wants transcendence without responsibility. Was Tanigawa paying homage to sekaikei in 2003, or reflecting on and parodying it? Readers can now judge for themselves.

## 2 Haruhi and the Postmodern Lens

That answers the narrow question of whether Haruhi is sekaikei. But if the inquiry is to matter, classification can only be the first step. The Chinese light-novel critic known online as Kuangkuang Kuangzi (框框框子) once joked that rather than listen to people wrangle over definitions, he would define the term himself.[^19] The remark was widely mocked, but it has a point. *Sekaikei* was once fashionable and has since gone stale; Haruhi itself is no longer new. Why devote so much space to deciding whether one belongs to the other? Why should this label, coined by a small circle of critics, still matter?

For me, the answer lies less in the label’s definition than in two critical lenses often attached to it: postmodern theory and cultural studies. Haruhi sits uneasily within both. This section addresses the first; the next turns to the second.

Later critics frequently placed *sekaikei* in a postmodern theoretical context. Tsukasa Takase, relaying Azuma, connects it to a world in which Lacan’s Symbolic—the social orders of history, nation, and shared meaning—has lost authority.[^20] This is one influential genealogy of the label, not a claim that every use of *sekaikei* carries the same theory.

“Grand narrative” is a phrase of which Hiroki Azuma is especially fond. In *Otaku: Japan’s Database Animals*, he writes:

> Modernity was ruled by the grand narrative. In contrast, in postmodernity the grand narratives break down and the cohesion of the social entirety rapidly weakens. In Japan that weakening was accelerated in the 1970s, when both high-speed economic growth and “the season of politics” ended and when Japan experienced the Oil Shocks and the United Red Army Incident.[^52]

In *The Birth of Gamelike Realism*, Azuma likewise describes grand narratives as losing their universal authority and circulating as small narratives among others.[^54] Following Jean-François Lyotard’s *The Postmodern Condition*, he constructs a chain of implication: postmodernity → the decline of grand narratives → sekaikei. That chain carries postmodern theory into criticism of anime, comics, games, and novels. I will call it the “postmodern lens.” It is a conspicuous strand of Japanese and Chinese ACGN criticism discussed in this essay; I do not claim that it dominates every language community or every branch of the field.

Two words must remain distinct. I will use **postmodernity** for a historical and cultural condition: the institutional, economic, media, and social changes associated with the period after high modernity. I will use **postmodernism** for theories and artistic practices that interpret, affirm, criticize, or otherwise respond to that condition. Terry Eagleton distinguishes the historical period from its characteristic intellectual and cultural styles, and Azuma makes the same distinction for light novels.[^53][^54] A work can oppose postmodernist ideas about truth or grand narratives while still being a product of postmodernity. Nothing in the argument below proves that Haruhi somehow stands outside the period that produced it.

Azuma’s method in fact depends on that point. Writers need not consciously embrace postmodernism for their work to arise within postmodernity. His account of light novels therefore looks not only at what one novel says, but at the media environment and patterns of production and consumption among novels. That historical account cannot be disproved by finding a rationalist character inside a book.

My narrower objection concerns explanatory priority. I will argue that Haruhi repeatedly stages resistance to postmodernist suspicions of reason, truth, and grand narratives. If so, a postmodern historical account may still explain the environment in which the series circulated, but a *sekaikei* reading derived from that account should not override the series’ own investment in rational inquiry. The issue is not whether Haruhi belongs to postmodernity. It is whether the postmodern lens describes what the work asks its readers to notice.

The first piece of evidence is Sasaki’s conversation with Kyon near the end of *The Surprise* (Japanese Part One), after the confrontation among Sasaki’s group.[^60] It is unusually dense, and much of it serves no immediate plot function. I read it as a key to *The Surprise*, perhaps even to Haruhi as a whole, while recognizing that Sasaki’s speech is not documentary proof of Tanigawa’s personal beliefs. Sasaki is acutely aware of problems often associated with postmodernity, including:

**The pluralization of values.** Tanigawa links this problem to “information” (情報), the central term in the aliens’ account of the world.[^21] Sasaki asks Kyon what matters most to him and observes that, amid competing standards—money, information, feeling—neither of them can produce a common measure. More information multiplies possible choices, yet that very abundance can leave people unable to choose; a lack of information can instead push them toward whatever the majority already prefers. Diversity and conformity are thus entangled rather than simple opposites.

**The loss of meaning in individual existence.** Sasaki imagines an intelligent bacterium asking not what humanity plans to do with the planet, but what any individual human lives for. She then speculates that aliens, time travelers, and espers may all be seeking acknowledgment that they exist and will be remembered. The passage can be read in plot terms: Haruhi lives in this era, and therefore this era matters. Yet it enters the conversation so abruptly that some readers treat it as a clue to Fujiwara’s motives. I am more inclined to hear Sasaki thinking aloud about the present.

Sasaki answers these problems in emphatically modern terms: rational inquiry, the pursuit of meaning, confidence in progress, and an attraction to grand narratives. She defines her life through thinking and continuing to think. What she hopes to leave behind is not merely genetic inheritance but a word, concept, invention, work of art, or theory that later people might receive. She insists that the thought must be her own, reached without aid from aliens, time travelers, or espers. I paraphrase here because the essay’s Taiwanese relay is unpaginated and because the Japanese text itself calls DNA an “amino-acid double helix,” a scientific error rather than a translation problem.[^60]

In this scene, Sasaki meets problems of postmodernity by combining Cartesian and Enlightenment rationalism with the kind of heroic, goal-directed narrative that Lyotard describes as losing authority.[^22] Her manifesto sharply opposes postmodernist currents suspicious of reason and grand narratives. Azuma can reply that ambitious story content does not disprove the historical decline of grand narratives.[^23] I agree: the scene cannot remove Haruhi from postmodernity. It does, however, stage an explicit resistance to one influential account of that condition. Earlier details point in the same direction: *The Indignation* names Sakanaka’s dog Rousseau and includes Hegel’s *Phenomenology of Spirit* on the published list commonly called “Yuki Nagato’s hundred books.”

This is my deeper objection to filing Haruhi under *sekaikei*. A postmodern historical account can illuminate the series’ environment, but a reading that treats postmodernist suspicion as the work’s own horizon risks missing the rationalist answer it repeatedly stages.

An obvious objection remains: these are Sasaki’s views. At most, they prove that one character articulates an anti-postmodernist position. Why extend that claim to the whole series, much less to Tanigawa? I have argued elsewhere that Sasaki’s position is closely connected to Haruhi’s central idea,[^24] but a skeptical reader may still see me generalizing from one character’s speech. I will therefore make the case from another direction: the series’ investment in detective fiction.

As Lu Ye says in the essay “The Dialectic of the Legendary and the Everyday: ‘Golden Age’ Detective Fiction and Modernity,” detective fiction “is at once one of modernity’s new products and its symbol and its supporting evidence,” and “the so-called ‘defense of the detective story’ is, in substance, a defense of ‘modern life.’” Concretely:

> To enchant everyday life and to disenchant it are both acts of modernity. A mystery arises in the first place from an asymmetry of information, yet the process of solving it brings people to discover that every answer had been lying before their eyes all along. The “dialectic of the everyday and the legendary” forged the Golden Age’s narrative principle of “absolute fairness”: the author may not conceal information from the reader; reader and detective alike stand within the restricted narration, on the same starting line. Plainly, this dialectic of the everyday and the legendary embodies the potent self-confidence of Enlightenment reason: however many readings there may be, where there is a question, there is an answer.

The structure of *honkaku*—fair-play, puzzle-centered—detective fiction models confidence that facts and reasoning can disclose a unique answer. That convention does not by itself refute postmodern thought; a postmodern work can contain a solvable puzzle. My claim is narrower: Haruhi repeatedly makes the convention into an argument. In “Wandering Shadow,” Koizumi says that gathering the available information and combining reason, imagination, and a touch of intuition can lead to the one truth. The series then tests that confidence in three major mysteries: “Remote Island Syndrome,” “Where Did the Cat Go?,” and “Tsuruya’s Challenge.”

“Remote Island Syndrome,” the series’ first mystery, begins traditionally: a locked-room murder in an isolated mansion cut off by a storm. Its second half is anything but conventional. Koizumi first announces a false solution, Haruhi invents a second, and Kyon finally reveals that the Agency staged the entire affair. The story thus offers three successive solutions. Perhaps that unusual structure led Kyoto Animation to turn its adaptation into a parody of *honkaku* mystery anime.

But was parody Tanigawa’s real purpose? Sixteen years later, “Tsuruya’s Challenge” invited a fresh reading of “Remote Island Syndrome.” Its opening has Koizumi and T discuss the **Late Queen Problem** at length. Put simply, a detective inside a story cannot know with certainty that the available clues are complete or accurate; from evidence available within the fictional world alone, the detective therefore cannot prove that one suspect is the logically unique culprit.

The history of the term matters. Rintarō Norizuki articulated the underlying problem in “Early Queen Theory,” published in the February 1995 issue of *Gendai Shisō*. Kiyoshi Kasai then generalized it and coined the Japanese label *kōki Queen-teki mondai*—literally, the “later-Queen-like problem”—in a 1996 essay later collected as “The Late Queen Problem” in *Tantei shōsetsu ron II* (1998).[^25] Norizuki thus proposed the problem; Kasai supplied the name by which it became widely known.

Norizuki situated his argument amid the late-1980s *shin honkaku*, or “new orthodox,” revival of fair-play puzzle mysteries. He also invoked deconstruction and **New Academism**, the Japanese 1980s publishing and intellectual phenomenon that brought figures such as Akira Asada and Kōjin Karatani—and poststructuralist vocabulary—into a wider reading public.[^61] His point was not that every *shin honkaku* novel endorsed postmodernism. It was that the genre’s confidence in formal deduction emerged while Japanese intellectual debate was questioning the grounds on which such systems rested. The Late Queen Problem turns that doubt back on the detective’s claim to a unique solution.

Azuma later gave this history a psychoanalytic reading in “Rintarō Norizuki and the Problem of Love.” He focuses on Norizuki’s detective series, where the fictional Rintarō lives with his police-inspector father, and reads their dependence through the figures of an absent father and an engulfing mother. Azuma identifies Yōko Kubodera, who helps Rintarō recover in *Futatabi akai akumu* (*Another Red Nightmare*, 1992), as an outside relation that interrupts this closed pair.[^26] This is Azuma’s interpretation of Norizuki’s fiction, not Norizuki’s own solution to the logical problem.

Tanigawa takes a different route. Koizumi and T argue that when a character issues a “challenge to the reader,” the author is really addressing the reader outside the book. The tacit compact between author and reader guarantees that a unique solution exists and that the supplied clues make deduction possible. “Tsuruya’s Challenge” thus answers the Late Queen Problem at the level of genre convention, rebuilding the game on the triad of puzzle, clue, and answer. In my reading, this is another case in which the series answers a postmodernist doubt with a modern confidence in reason; the puzzle form alone would not establish that claim, but the characters’ explicit appeal to the author-reader compact does.

This perspective changes “Remote Island Syndrome.” Its first two solutions are true or false in the sense of a model answer: they either match the solution set by the author or they do not. The third solution is true in a stronger sense: it matches what actually happened in the story’s world. By stacking the three, Tanigawa grounds the author’s model answer in an objective world and a unique truth. “Where Did the Cat Go?” makes the principle clearer still, because everyone knows from the outset that the Agency has designed a detective game and reasons from that assurance.

“However many readings there may be, where there is a question, there is an answer”: the movement from question to solution is a creed running through Haruhi. In “Nagaru Tanigawa’s Challenge: A Complete Analysis of *The Intuition of Haruhi Suzumiya*,”[^27] I argued that Tanigawa wants readers to solve the series’ many buried riddles. I now think that commitment extends beyond fiction to the world itself. As “Tsuruya’s Challenge” suggests when it locates the impossibility of deduction, if anywhere, in the real world, neither the Late Queen Problem nor its answer stops at the boundary of detective fiction. Critics confined to a postmodern framework will struggle to recognize such a world.

## 3 Haruhi and Cultural Studies

“Cultural studies” is the name of an academic field, not simply a phrase for research about culture. Emerging in Britain in the 1960s through figures such as Richard Hoggart (1918–2014), Raymond Williams (1921–1988), and Stuart Hall (1932–2014), the field expanded “culture” beyond the fine arts to include working-class and youth leisure, mass entertainment, and broadcasting. It studies how cultural forms interact with political, economic, and social change.[^28]

Here, however, I use **cultural studies in a broad sense**: research that treats a class of objects as an integrated culture and studies individual works from that wider vantage. That broad category includes studies of *guichu*, the Chinese rapid-cut remix form built from intense repetition; *danmaku*, timed comments overlaid across a video; cosplay; and the ACGN field at issue here. **ACGN** is an East Asian umbrella acronym for anime, comics, games, and novels; the shorter **ACG** omits novels. Criticism of this kind treats a work—or a type of work—as a case within that field as a whole, or within such subfields as light novels and romance-oriented visual novels. The method inevitably shifts attention from a work in itself to a work embedded in a cultural network. Seeking a synoptic explanation of many tangled phenomena, it tends toward theory, whether borrowed or newly coined. *Sekaikei* is one such theoretical construction.

“Theory” needs clarification. I use it to mean any systematic explanation of phenomena, not only intimidating bodies of thought such as Lyotard’s account of postmodernity or Lacanian psychoanalysis. A passage by the Chinese light-novel critic 歧路先知 (Qilu Xianzhi) illustrates the point. **Tieba** is Baidu’s network of topic forums, while **Zhihu** combines question-and-answer threads with long-form user essays:

> What a work’s Tieba forum does day in and day out is essentially epigraphy and exegesis. You can’t call this useless, of course; when you forget some setting detail or plot point of a work, they are the ones to consult. But… in my humble view, if a setting detail or plot point could actually be forgotten by you, the person writing the essay, that thing probably wasn’t very important in the first place.
>
> The Tieba style of combing through plot must assume that the work is sacred and inviolable. Posts written on that foundation naturally cannot be called “criticism.”
>
> The “Lacanians” on Zhihu who cannot open their mouths without citing Žižek are the other extreme. The essays they write are all too reasonable—so reasonable that I begin to suffer the illusion that they could have written their criticism without reading any light novels at all.[^29]

Qilu Xianzhi plainly dislikes light-novel criticism that reaches reflexively for Lacan. But he does not reject cultural studies or theory as such. His target is academic theory, not the concepts he develops himself. His “pendant heroine” names a heroine who hangs from the protagonist’s plot without driving it;[^30] his “three unities of the progress bar” describes the alignment of romantic, character-development, and narrative progress;[^31] and his “attributes versus humanity” thesis asks whether stock character traits overwhelm the person they are meant to compose.[^32]

His perspective therefore operates at the scale of light-novel culture, even the broader Chinese idea of **erciyuan** (二次元), literally “two-dimensional”: anime-, comics-, and game-centered subculture and the imagined worlds associated with it. “A Methodology of Light Novel Reviewing (VII),”[^33] which he recommended as a statement of his camp’s principles, asks about the essence and future of the light novel as a form, not merely the merits of one book. Except in casual reading notes, his criticism is therefore no less theoretical. His concepts differ from Lacan’s or Azuma’s in scale and refinement, not in kind.

The danger in this theory-driven cultural criticism appears at both stages of its method: phenomena → description → theoretical explanation. The claim here is limited to ACGN cultural studies, not theory in every field. Critics who range across a vast number of works, or devote most of their time to abstract theory, may never learn any one work in detail. Their initial descriptions can therefore be partial or simply wrong. Azuma, for example, calls Haruhi a mid-2000s bestseller and representative sekaikei, then claims that works of this kind ignore social description and treat alien invasion with astonishing carelessness.[^34] Qilu Xianzhi similarly writes that Haruhi possesses nearly all the series’ “discursive power,” that every plot exists to develop her, and that Kyon matters only as a viewpoint from which to observe her.[^35]

Theory necessarily abstracts from particulars, so no theory will match every phenomenon perfectly. A rough fit may be enough. But when ACGN critics import theories developed for other objects and disciplines, the fit can become dangerously loose. Any distortion in the initial description then passes into the explanation. If a critic cannot describe a work accurately, how can a theory built on that description explain it?

This criticism has a history. In late 2014, a Zhihu user asked, “Where is the frontier of ACG cultural studies today? What is the state of ACG cultural studies in China?”[^36] The respondent nbht attacked the whole field, calling Azuma “an anime fan who doesn’t watch anime” and accusing scholars of hiding from the present, peering through a window, canonizing bad books, and repeating inherited errors. Guo Wenfang (@Macro kuo) replied that this attack privileged production technique—storyboards and key animation—while dismissing cultural analysis. After surveying Eiji Ōtsuka, Hiroki Azuma, and Tamaki Saitō, Guo urged critics to show less contempt for unfamiliar theories and do more writing of their own.

The argument over cultural studies is therefore not new. In the seven years before this essay was written, the Chinese ACG scene produced more self-directed study and theory-driven essays alongside continuing suspicion of theory. As one participant in that scene, I want to ask what the “DIY writing” Guo advocated has produced.

Consider “The ‘Two-Dimensional Watchers’ of the Post-‘Otaku’ Era: The Poetics of the ‘Creation-kei’ and the Left-Wing Face of ‘Sekaikei’”[^37] by Lin Yunke, a scholar of literary theory then affiliated with Nankai University. I found it while researching whether Haruhi is sekaikei. The essay calls *CLANNAD* “*Dango Daikazoku*” in Chinese, although the latter is the anime’s ending theme, and dates Kyoto Animation’s *Hyouka* to 2017 rather than 2012. Its discussion of Haruhi reads:

> **This work has almost no plot to speak of**; its content amounts to nothing more than how the male lead and the espers satisfy Haruhi’s various absurd “world-creating urges” so as to avert the collapse of the present world. The work sets radically different attitudes toward “worldview” side by side: on the one hand, it resolutely affirms the absolute legitimacy of fiction and world-creation, issuing the famous “two-dimensional” declaration that “anyone who looks for a sense of reality in a made-up story must have something wrong with their head”; on the other hand, all the fiction ultimately serves Haruhi’s emotional claims on the ordinary person Kyon. This anime series—arguably the one that best fits “database consumption”—expresses this resolution: only when the “world-creating urge” is fully liberated, and the fictive potential within the everyday brought fully into play, is the world of Dasein at its most vivid and most whole.

The claim that the Haruhi series has almost no plot is contradicted by the mysteries, cosmological conflicts, and temporal structures already discussed. Nor is this unfamiliarity an isolated case. Gao Yuanyuan’s “From Hayao Miyazaki to Makoto Shinkai: What Has Happened to Japanese Animated Film”[^38] summarizes *The Disappearance* as “a romance between an ordinary person and a time traveler.” When the work itself is known so poorly, terms such as “Dasein” and Azuma’s “database consumption” hover above it without explaining it. I struggle to see what such research offers either Haruhi or its readers.

If it is said that the examples above are confined to the Haruhi series and are therefore unrepresentative, then let us look at an example beyond the Haruhi series: “The Core and Contradictions of EVA (*Neon Genesis Evangelion*)”[^39] by @kokeihou, who describes himself as “working mainly in cultural sociology and cultural studies; formerly a cram-school teacher in Japan.” At the very opening the author writes:

> Though it borrows surfaces such as robots and Angels, and uses seemingly complex concepts such as theology, whether or not you understand things like the story’s chronology or the shapes of its weapons is in fact immaterial to interpreting the story’s core. … The core of *EVA* was never based on any theology; theology, if anything, was added in order to explicate the story’s setting. Its core is very simple—but before that, we must first understand the philosophical and intellectual themes of our age, above all the heart of this work: Lacan’s psychoanalytic theory. Once we have understood that, everything will be readily solved.

The premise is clear: read *EVA* through Lacanian psychoanalysis. By 2021 this was hardly novel; “Flowers in the Mirror, Moon in the Water: Rei Ayanami and the Mirror Stage,” posted to Baidu’s *EVA* forum in 2014, is one earlier example.[^40] But *EVA* is too complex for Lacan alone to exhaust, and @kokeihou concedes that the theological material falls outside his scheme. His answer to this mismatch is simply to declare whatever Lacan cannot accommodate “immaterial.” The interpretation produces new meanings by erasing existing ones.

I once encountered the inverse maneuver in a conversation with a Haruhi fan. When I asked why Tanigawa spends so much time asking what the world is actually like and writing elaborate mysteries if the series is sekaikei, he called those elements the author’s personal taste. But we cannot extract the material that fits *sekaikei* and call it “Haruhi,” then dismiss the much larger remainder as taste. @kokeihou goes further still, treating Lacanian psychoanalysis as the privileged route and dismissing alternatives as “subjective,” “devoid of substance,” or “irresponsible”:

> Without understanding the phenomenology of the body, it is hard to appreciate the intellectual bedrock of *Kill la Kill*; without knowing the currents of feminist thought, one cannot feel what is moving in *Sailor Moon*, or even in *Little Witch Academia*. Without knowing postmodernism and the currents of philosophy, one cannot so much as touch the intellectual underpinnings of the science fiction of Satoshi Kon and Katsuhiro Ōtomo, or of works such as *Puella Magi Madoka Magica* and *EVA*. Of course, there is nothing wrong with not understanding any of this—you can simply enjoy the rush and excitement. What is to be feared is that some people use their own pleasures and explanations to delimit the meaning of an entire work and, through the media’s reach, confine everyone else’s imagination and sense of self. … There is nothing wrong at all with having no intellectual substance! You are entirely free to lose yourself in the story and arrive at your own distinctive reading. But if you want others to agree with you, first study until you have an intellectual grounding, and a grasp of the historical context, equal to the author’s!

This is cultural criticism’s elevated viewpoint at its most extreme. From the scale of “ACGN culture,” the critic looks down on individual works, creators, the culture, and finally ordinary readers. But what does this altitude actually provide beyond essays that read like Lacanian coursework and panoramic schemes that readers mistake for the whole? @kokeihou’s “The Overcoming of Sekaikei: A Brief History of Literary and Artistic Thought in Japanese Anime,”[^42] for example, compresses anime’s intellectual history into “grand narrative → sekaikei → everyday-kei,” a familiar sequence I had already encountered in “Farewell to the Apocalypse: A General Review of Japan’s Age of Fiction.”[^43]

Return, finally, to Haruhi. When I began studying the series seriously about a year ago, my reading produced a growing list of questions. If the time travelers and espers disagree about whether Haruhi is a god, why do most essays simply assume the espers are right, sometimes even likening her to the biblical God?[^44] Why do introductions rarely venture beyond *The Melancholy*, the 2009 anime’s “Endless Eight,” and the *Disappearance* film? Why do they praise the franchise’s former popularity and later influence without explaining what makes the work itself extraordinary? And why are so many basic descriptions simply wrong? A Chinese allusion distinguishes “I annotate the Six Classics,” in which canonical works correct the reader’s ideas, from “the Six Classics annotate me,” in which the reader makes the canon serve a prior interpretation.[^45] The latter can defend an openly personal reading, but not a false description.

In my essay “Is Haruhi Suzumiya a God? With Remarks on the Science-Fiction Interpretation and the School Interpretation,”[^46] published the previous October, I traced these problems to a clash between science-fiction and school-story readings. Critics accustomed to treating high-school life as the essence of the light novel had imposed that assumption on Haruhi. I now see a deeper cause in the two failures discussed here: insufficient attention to the series and excessive attachment to theory. Labels such as *sekaikei*, *everyday-kei* (stories organized around ordinary life), *atmosphere-kei* (works that privilege mood and character interaction over plot), and “club fiction” (school stories structured around a shared extracurricular club) keep returning discussion to the same familiar opposition between the everyday and the extraordinary. No wonder we arrive at “everyone praising it without knowing where it is actually good.”[^47]

Strong works exceed any single database of reusable *moe* elements—traits such as character designs, speech patterns, and archetypes that elicit affectionate fan attachment. Some critics insist that a literary critic must also be a philosopher.[^49] I would begin one step earlier: what kind of ACGN criticism do we actually need? The method modeled here is modest but demanding—describe the work accurately, test the theory against what the work leaves unexplained, and let close reading revise the framework rather than forcing the work to fit it.
