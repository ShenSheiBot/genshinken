## 0. How Should We Interpret Part 2 of FGO? Or, What Kind of Reading Does Dystopian Fiction Demand?

As one of the most influential cash-grab mobile games of recent years, *Fate/Grand Order* has become an important—and perhaps uniquely contentious—part of the wider TYPE-MOON franchise. By the midpoint of Part 2, the pattern behind its seven Lostbelts had come into focus: each is a dystopian present built from a possible history of the TYPE-MOON world.

For readers coming to the game cold, a few terms will help. Chaldea is the protagonist’s organization. To restore Proper Human History, its members enter each Lostbelt and cut down the Tree of Emptiness that anchors it. The protagonist is a Master who directs Servants, summoned heroes whose signature powers are called Noble Phantasms; a Command Spell is the magical mark through which a Master can issue an extraordinary command. These mechanics give Part 2 its repeated ethical problem: Chaldea must destroy whole worlds in order to recover its own.

The public disputes around these scenarios are therefore part of the text’s life. When Lostbelt No.3, the Chinese Lostbelt, first appeared on the Japanese server, I saw it celebrated in public discussion as “an expression of China’s strength.” When it reached the Chinese server, I saw it accused of “insulting China.” These phrases survive here as examples of reception, not as attributable survey evidence. It would be easy to dismiss them as vulgar misreadings of a none-too-accomplished commercial dystopia. But merely grasping a dystopian premise and enjoying the story built from it is hardly an adequate response either.

Slavoj Žižek once described *The Handmaid’s Tale*, using Fredric Jameson’s phrase, as an exercise in “nostalgia for the present.” I am paraphrasing the argument rather than citing a particular publication here. On this account, the work does not truly pursue the threat of religious fundamentalism. Instead, it uses the prospect of a religious-totalitarian future to flatter a present imagined as “pluralist, open, and progressive.” Graham Harman, a leading figure in speculative realism, once joked that Žižek was probably the first Western philosopher since Giordano Bruno who could have made a living as a stand-up comic; I recall the joke here without a source attached to it.

To my mind, Žižek’s charge applies even more forcefully to Part 2 of *FGO*. The game repeatedly presents the felling of the Trees of Emptiness as an unavoidable war for survival. It also stresses the Lostbelts’ “wrongness” in relation to Proper Human History, sparing players too much guilt about “doing evil.” The effect is to celebrate the world that already exists: “How wonderful that we were born into Proper Human History, where hope still exists!” It resembles the familiar Japanese refrain, “How wonderful it is to live in Japan!” The conflict with a Lostbelt then becomes a contest over which history is worse, while dystopian critique gives way to an affirmation of a reality whose dominant values combine neoliberal ethics with multiculturalism.

There is one apparent improvement. The legitimacy of Chaldea’s struggle does not rest only on a totalitarian-sounding “correct history” guaranteed by the Counter Force, the world’s own mechanism for preserving that history. It also rests on each Lostbelt’s oppression and unsustainable contradictions. Even so, this shift brings the story into closer alignment with prevailing ideology rather than breaking from it.

Must such texts do nothing but reproduce ideology? If *The Handmaid’s Tale* were only “nostalgia for the present,” Žižek would have no reason to push the discussion further. After arguing that its plot caters to the obscene, patriarchal enjoyment of its audience and ends up celebrating the present, he asks a better question: why does a United States imagined—in the terms I am paraphrasing—as “free, progressive, and respectful of women” turn into Gilead, a religious totalitarian state that brutally oppresses women? Whenever we pine for “the good old days,” we should likewise ask why those days produced a present that can be endured only through nostalgia.

Dystopian fantasy does not confront reality with a wholly alien world. Its ideological machinery is already at work here. The danger is not simply that our quiet, harmonious present might someday slide into horror; we already live among the forces that make such a slide possible. Precisely because a dystopian work may lack any radical politics of its own, we have to read it radically and recover whatever critical force it contains.

## 1. The Final Dark God—or the Final Dark Human?

Asked which Lostbelt most clearly reflects our own world, many players would instinctively name Lostbelt No.3, Synchronized Intellect Nation, SIN. I would instead choose the most important of the four Lostbelts then available on the Chinese server: [Lostbelt No.4, *Samsara of Genesis and Terminus, Yugakshetra - The Final Dark God*](https://fate-go.us/yugakshetra/). Its setting is more overtly mythological, but it exposes a crucial ideological structure and shows where that structure leads.

The chapter’s subtitle is *The Final Dark God*. In light of Section 19, however, “Arjuna as god” can also be read as “the final dark human.”

The story offers only an educated guess about the point at which the Indian Lostbelt broke away from Proper Human History: it appears to have diverged around the end of the Kurukshetra War in the *Mahābhārata*. Disgusted by the war, Arjuna attributes its bloodshed and misery to the persistence of human evil—to everything he deems “defective and unnecessary.” He resolves to unite the powers of the gods and use repeated yuga cycles to build a perfect world without evil.

Arjuna, the demigod son of Indra, continually seizes the Divinities of other gods. He eventually absorbs the entire Indian pantheon and becomes the sole deity, “the Final Dark God.” Drawing on the Divinities of Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Śiva, the nameless god who was once Arjuna compresses the four yugas—traditionally 1,728,000, 1,296,000, 864,000, and 432,000 years long—into periods of four, three, two, and one day. Creation and destruction now complete a full cycle every ten days. Each destruction removes from the next world whatever he has judged “defective and unnecessary.” The inhabitants spend the first four days in the paradise of Kṛta Yuga. The world then decays through Tretā and Dvāpara Yuga before reaching Kali Yuga on the final day, when monsters called kali attack without cease. The world resets, and whatever god called evil is missing from the new age.

In Section 19, Karna, fighting alongside Chaldea, refutes Arjuna with one question from the game’s official English script: “Would a perfect god be so fixated on my name, as you have been thus far?” After absorbing every Divinity in the Indian pantheon, Arjuna has lost almost all of his humanity. He resembles an automaton that exists only to maintain creation, destruction, and judgment. Yet the core of this machine is not divine at all. The sole god remains governed by the human Arjuna’s obsession with the Kurukshetra War. Divine will rests on Arjuna’s desire, and that desire returns obsessively to the war as proof of evil—an evil that god can judge but never erase. A justice that needs evil is itself evil at the core.

The game treats this surviving human desire as proof that Arjuna’s divinity is incomplete. I want to go further. The sole god’s law is not divine law at all, but human law. The judgment of evil is not something sacred and categorically separate from humanity. It is a human ideological structure. The sacred realm is made for human beings; the machinery that identifies and expels sin is human, all too human. Giorgio Agamben gives us a way to discuss this machinery in Chapter 9 of *The Open: Man and Animal*: the anthropological machine.

## 2. God…Has Cast His Gaze Downward

In the Lostbelt, Arjuna as sole god occupies what psychoanalysis calls the position of the big Other: not merely a powerful individual, but the imagined authority whose rules determine what a society can recognize as lawful, normal, and meaningful. Every social activity in this world is organized around him. People pray and act according to his demands, seeking protection through virtue and devotion. Those who fail to conform to divine goodness may die in the kali’s attacks as the cycle approaches Kali Yuga. An injury may also cause god to mark them as “defective” and erase them from the next world. Arjuna thus mediates and judges every permitted or forbidden relation among his subjects.

Yet even this god cannot see every evil. Indeed, a social order often depends on violations that it cannot openly acknowledge, even while its judgments are triggered by whatever it suddenly catches sight of. We can hear that contradiction in God Arjuna’s words before he unleashes his world-ending Noble Phantasm:

> “God…has cast his gaze downward. These defects are unnecessary…and therefore…evil. Begone, evil… You have no place in the new yuga…the new world. I shall bring it down… The sword of the god of terminus. I will cut off the world. From the rift in the world…made by the blade…clear purification will burst forth. Terminus and Genesis…will cycle anew… Sword of dissolution, let your blade fall. Mahāpralaya…”

Before following that machinery any further, we need three plain distinctions. They will remain stable for the rest of the essay:

1. **An unseen blind spot.** Something exists outside the order’s categories, so the authority cannot recognize or judge it. Jinako’s concealed survival across Arjuna’s cycles is the game’s clearest example: what god cannot see exposes a limit in god’s supposed perfection.
2. **A named outcast.** The order identifies a group as an enemy or inferior exception and uses that exclusion to define everyone who counts as normal. In the analytical model of Nazi ideology discussed below, the figure of “the Jew” is made into the enemy against which “Aryan” identity is produced.
3. **A tolerated stereotype.** The order says that an excluded person belongs after all, but accepts only a harmless, diminished image of them. The NEET who is told “you are human too,” while remaining fixed as a pitiable and socially useless shut-in, is the example that will matter later. I will call this **exclusion through inclusion**.

The second category is not an accidental blemish on an otherwise complete order. In the Žižekian model of ideology I am using, the named outcast helps hold the order together. Applied to the Third Reich, this is an analytical account of Nazi ideology, not a literal claim that the regime was physically incapable of murdering every Jewish person. The model says that the fantasy of “the Jew” as a permanent internal enemy helped Nazism produce and police “Aryan” identity. If the regime had eliminated every person it marked as Jewish, the exclusionary logic would not therefore have ended; on this account, it would have sought another internal enemy, potentially among those previously accepted as Aryan.

Agamben’s anthropological machine names a related mechanism. It produces “the human” by repeatedly locating something supposedly nonhuman within humanity and casting it out. The exclusion of *homo sacer*—the person placed outside ordinary political protection—helps constitute the community that excludes them. If that outcast were erased completely, the machine would need another. This is a theoretical account of how an exclusionary order reproduces itself, not a substitute for the particular history of any persecuted group.

Arjuna therefore cannot identify and destroy every evil in a single cycle. Whenever he uses his Noble Phantasm, he eliminates everything that the current world allows him to identify as “defective and unnecessary.” In the next cycle, however, new evil appears among people who previously counted as good. Agamben’s point in describing the anthropological machine is to ask how it might be stopped, not how its classifications might be perfected. I recall the Chinese political theorist Wu Guanjun giving the danger a brutal formulation—“This machine’s meat grinder is about to turn on humanity itself”—although the work in which the line appeared is not identified here. Karna and Arjuna’s final battle makes the machine turn against Arjuna, forcing the sole god toward self-negation.

Endless division does not purify humanity; it consumes humanity and its world. The fate of Asha, the young girl the party first encounters in the Lostbelt, condenses that process. The commemorative Craft Essence awarded for clearing the chapter—a piece of collectible game equipment with its own illustration and text—resembles the imaginary wedding portrait at the end of Kim Ji-hoon’s 2007 film *May 18*, about the Gwangju Uprising. In that portrait, the unsmiling bride is the only survivor among those gathered around her; the smiling others have been killed in the violent suppression by the martial-law forces of Chun Doo-hwan’s regime. Here, everything that made Asha smile has been obliterated by the cycles. Only the girl herself still smiles, in an impossible dream.

No society can endlessly cut away part of itself without eventually destroying itself. Nor can the people selected for erasure, or those who love them, accept the machine’s judgment. William Tell, a Servant summoned by Arjuna, discovers that the cycle erased his memory of being forced by a governor to shoot an apple from his son’s head. The memory interfered with his service to Arjuna and was therefore deemed “defective and unnecessary.” When the party witnesses the world’s destruction for the second time, Tell rebels and attacks his god.

Koyanskaya’s calculated cruelty produces another rebellion. Then presented as an emissary of the Foreign God, and Chaldea’s enemy since the bleaching of the Earth, she arranges for a woman in Lakshmi Bai’s rebel army to discover a letter from her vanished lover. Faced with one of Arjuna’s Divinity-bearing composite Servants, the woman cries out: “My lover is gone now. How dare you say they were defective? How dare you say they deserved to disappear!?”

The Servants who join Chaldea have their own reasons to fall under god’s judgment. Lakshmi Bai, the Rani of Jhansi, leads the villagers in rebellion against Arjuna. She is also fused with Alakshmi, the elder sister of Lakshmi and the goddess of bad luck and misfortune. Because the Indian Rebellion of 1857 was suppressed, Lakshmi Bai—the ruler of a fallen state—regards herself as an unfit queen. Ganesha’s power presents no obvious defect, but the vessel whose consciousness dominates that Servant is Jinako Carigiri, Karna’s Master in *Fate/EXTRA CCC*. She is a NEET, someone not in education, employment, or training, and therefore an unquestionably “useless” person under a capitalist work ethic.

After a second remaking of the world, Chaldea concludes that undermining the people’s faith will not shake Arjuna’s authority. Anyone who ceased to conform to divine goodness has already been deprived of existence. At Aśvatthāman’s suggestion, the party—now defined by god as “evil”—uses his Noble Phantasm, *Mahākāla Shakti*, which draws on Śiva’s power over time. Two allies travel back to the moment when Arjuna first began ruling this way and remain concealed through every subsequent cycle. If something god has identified as evil can survive unseen by him, then his perfection is conceptually broken. Lakshmi Bai becomes the outer shell that hides them; inside her, Ganesha’s vessel, Jinako Carigiri, remains conscious to complete the proof.

## 3. Even Shut-Ins Are Allowed to See the Sky

The crucial point in this plan is not simply that god sees and punishes whatever he calls defective, nor even Jinako’s immense sacrifice. During thousands of years of solitude, she exhausts every game that the god of wisdom’s power lets her access, then finally breaks down and drifts toward delirium. The plot wrings emotion from that ordeal. What matters for my argument, however, is the reunion password agreed upon by Jinako and the protagonist.

Chaldea races toward God’s Skyboulder, the shell into which Lakshmi Bai transformed, to rescue the two allies who have endured an unknowably long interval. The protagonist presses the hand bearing the Command Spell against the boulder and says: “Even shut-ins…are allowed to see the sky.” The official English script says “shut-ins”; my argument concerns Jinako’s own identification as a NEET.

If we used the Indian Lostbelt only to illustrate Agamben’s anthropological machine, we would risk the problem that Zhong Zimo identified in a Douban post whose title is not preserved here. TYPE-MOON works already “turn abstract concepts into representations.” Giving them the theoretical interpretation they seem to solicit can strip the concepts of their seriousness and reduce criticism to another conceptual game. The point I have been working toward in this essay—the point that might carry us beyond that game—is the password.

Jinako’s status as “defective and unnecessary” is not a verdict imposed only by the distorted Indian Lostbelt. She has also learned to judge herself by the assumptions of Proper Human History. Under its prevailing social norms, a NEET appears as someone nobody needs, a surplus person for whom society has no use. Jinako has internalized that judgment. The password answers a structure that relentlessly purges such people.

What does the line actually say? At the literal level, it says that shut-ins are allowed to see the sky. That can be heard as an affirmation of a right that should never have been denied. The word “allowed” alone does not prove that the speaker considers Jinako inferior. The critique that follows therefore rests on one possible, paternalistic reading rather than on an uncontestable meaning of the scene: the line may also sound as if an established social “we” has graciously decided that even someone already labeled useless may enjoy a basic good. On that reading, tolerance leaves the hierarchy intact. It admits the NEET only as an exception pardoned by those who still set the standard.

This is the third kind of exclusion defined above: exclusion through inclusion. Wu Guanjun applies the idea to artificial intelligence. When people anthropomorphize a fundamentally nonhuman intelligence, they may seem to welcome it into the human community while recognizing only the humanlike features they can assimilate. Whatever does not fit that image disappears from view. The same operation can be performed on a NEET. First, the social order casts the NEET as a defective kind of person, outside productive humanity. Then it announces that “NEETs are human too,” but only as a fixed, inferior type who must be tolerated. The person has been readmitted; the assumptions that made them disposable remain untouched.

The other two forms of exclusion clarify the difference. Jinako concealed within God’s Skyboulder is an unseen blind spot: because Arjuna cannot register her existence, she exposes a crack in his authority. A named outcast, by contrast, is highly visible. The order marks that person as the exception against which normal membership is defined. Exclusion through inclusion does something subtler. It makes the excluded person visible again, but only through a social image that deprives their difference of any power to challenge the norm.

This is also the limited point behind my intervention in a Chinese-language discussion of Black Lives Matter. In Chinese discussions I encountered, the movement was sometimes glossed as “Black lives are lives too.” That is not the literal expansion of BLM, which is “Black Lives Matter,” and it can reduce a political demand to bare admission into the category of lives that count. My own interpretive paraphrase was “Black people should live better”: a phrase meant to point beyond minimal recognition toward the material conditions under which Black people live.

I am not equating Black political struggle with the stigma attached to NEETs, nor offering a complete account of BLM. The narrower analogy concerns one form of recognition politics: an established order congratulates itself for admitting a marginalized group while leaving intact the standards and material relations that produced the group’s subordination. Black people do not need to qualify as human by becoming identical to an implicitly white norm, or to be told that although they fall short of that norm, they are “at least human.” The political demand concerns power and conditions of life, not a charitable extension of minimum status.

Karna’s attitude toward Jinako suggests a different relation, although the contrast is not absolute. From the outset, he never treats her as unreliable or unnecessary. The task he entrusts to her brings Jinako into the fight and helps her stand her ground under tremendous pressure and fear. The Hero of Charity does not first classify people as fit or unfit to count as human and then make a generous exception. He begins by assuming that Jinako can bear a responsibility of her own.

The politics I am criticizing is therefore not every demand for minority recognition, nor the affirmation of marginal cultures as such. It is the kind of recognition that loves a marginalized identity only as a safe, decorative niche; asks the existing order for tolerance without challenging how that identity was made inferior; and treats admission to the mainstream as the final horizon of justice. Such recognition can preserve the hierarchy it appears to overcome. It welcomes a manageable image of the excluded while disarming the ways their lives, demands, and actions might transform those who already count as normal.

The Indian Lostbelt is therefore not simply the opposite of Proper Human History. Both can operate through the same machinery: identify an outcast, define normality against that figure, then offer readmission on terms set by the people who were never expelled. Dystopia magnifies a structure already active in reality. A radical reading of the Lostbelt begins when we recognize that shared structure rather than using the Lostbelt to congratulate our own world.

## 4. Nous ne sommes rien, soyons tout!

In contrasting Karna’s attitude with the paternalistic reading of the protagonist’s password, I do not mean to condemn the protagonist on moral grounds. The line can be heard more generously, as I have said. It may also pander to the game’s otaku audience by reassuring players that even a shut-in belongs. Genuine acceptance of excluded people such as NEETs, however, cannot stop at cheap tolerance. It has to be forged in a common struggle.

A commercial game may be unable to express a fundamental revolt against reality. Its conflicts tend to become contests over which is worse: our reality, or an erroneous one. Yet it can still contain material for a radical reading. Here, I choose Karna. His trust in Jinako and the task he entrusts to her resemble the role that Žižek attributes to Lenin as a master in revolution. Again, I am paraphrasing a Žižekian argument rather than pointing to a specific text. Karna authorizes himself through action, then uses that authority to demand that those who follow him take responsibility for their own actions. The command makes action possible.

Karna’s confidence does not condescend to grant Jinako permission to exist. It recognizes that her existence is already hers, and his command helps make possible the radical action through which she can assert and secure it. We should still ask whether another model could do without a “master of the revolution.” If we cannot move beyond the idea that action requires a master’s coercive command, perhaps we will never see the slave’s final victory—the point in Hegel’s master–slave dialectic at which truth belongs to the slave.

Faced with the anthropological machine’s production of excluded people, our most basic position must be to refuse its division rather than accept the categories and petition for kinder readmission. The division itself must be rendered inoperative. It is not that the excluded “also” deserve a minimum of recognition. The excluded themselves “are” everything. As Badiou likes to quote from “The Internationale”: “We are nothing; let us be all! (*Nous ne sommes rien, soyons tout!*)”
