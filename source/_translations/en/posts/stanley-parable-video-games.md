## Introduction

Games are still widely dismissed as a useless waste of time[^ref-12], and in mainland China they were notoriously branded “electronic heroin” during the turn-of-the-millennium moral panic over young people and computer games. But why? Because they contain violence and sex? So do literature and film. Because they seem made for naive children or idle teenagers? Children’s literature is nevertheless taken seriously. Because playing is merely a way to pass the time? For most people, so is watching film or television. Players often answer that games are the **ninth art**—a ranking inherited from European enumerations of the arts and common in Chinese game discourse—though some use the slogan mainly **to excuse their own pastime**. Video games do have a shorter history than literature or painting, and the theories used to discuss them are still developing[^ref-22]. Cinema underwent a similar process in the twentieth century, but theory does not mature through the passage of time alone; it takes sustained exploration and thought. ***The Stanley Parable* is a game that undertakes precisely that work by examining the medium it belongs to.**

## I. Metagame: A Game About Games

Where most games emphasize **the imitation of reality**, metagames emphasize what we might call “**the imitation of process**.” Here *metagame* is meant in the metafictional sense, not the competitive “metagame” of strategies formed around play. Rather than simply depict a fictional world, such a work foregrounds **how a fictional world is depicted**. Its form becomes its content. By exposing the conventions normally hidden in production, it shows how such works are made; it also reveals the player’s relationship to the work and makes familiar forms newly visible[^ref-35].

The main menu is already full of metagame devices. Above the title *The Stanley Parable* appears the small phrase “You are playing,” while the computer beside it displays the same menu, nested screen within screen. The trick announces a **game about games**[^ref-10]. Better still, **the player’s real monitor can be counted as another layer in the recursion, blurring the boundary between game world and world outside the game.**

[fig] The game’s menu screen.

![The main menu of *The Stanley Parable* with its recursively nested computer screens](attachments/roof-archive/cv8322667/03-main-menu.png)

After most endings, the game restarts automatically, and the loading screen reads “The end is never the end,” making every apparent conclusion provisional.[^ref-9]

[fig] The loading screen after an ending is completed.

![A loading screen bearing the words “The end is never the end”](attachments/roof-archive/cv8322667/04-loading-screen.png)

The opening narration makes the self-reference still clearer. Stanley is Employee 427, and his job is to press whatever keys his monitor tells him to press—tedious work that nevertheless makes him perfectly happy. That is also a bare description of playing a video game: follow its prompts, press the right buttons at the right time, and satisfy its demands. Other games dress the procedure in different mechanics and stories, but its core resembles Stanley’s work[^ref-9]. During the apartment sequence, the screen even instructs the player to press particular buttons so that Stanley will perform domestic actions and advance the scene. **The obedient player has become the Stanley introduced at the beginning.**

[fig] The apartment sequence turns domestic routine into the explicit prompt “PLEASE PRESS ‘Z’ TO BE AT WORK IN THE MORNING.”

![An English on-screen prompt reading “PLEASE PRESS ‘Z’ TO BE AT WORK IN THE MORNING”](attachments/roof-archive/cv8322667/05-button-prompt.png)

In the Countdown Ending, a timer ticks toward detonation while the Narrator comments on the game’s design, points out that no bomb-defusing mechanic exists, and mocks the player’s futile attempts. In the Museum Ending—called the “Meat Patty Ending” in the Chinese essay, after the crushing machine on the route—the player enters an exhibition about the game’s own production, further blurring the line between game and world. In the Games Ending, the player strays into an unfinished area while the Narrator asks what “would have made this game better” and drops the player into *Minecraft* and *Portal*. (That route also offers a red door and a blue one, in an obvious echo of *The Matrix*.) These moments **reinforce** the **metagame** character of *The Stanley Parable*. Instead of offering seamless “immersion,” it makes **the player notice not only what the game represents but the machinery of representation itself—and, through play, think about games as a medium.**

The principal—perhaps the only—mechanic of *The Stanley Parable* is **choice**. Like *Firewatch* or *The Painscreek Killings*, it can be described as a walking simulator: you cannot even jump, and can do little beyond walking and using a handful of objects. Yet choice goes to the heart of games. **Whether we are playing a first-person shooter or a fighting game, every input can be understood as a choice made at a particular moment.** The sparse mechanics keep our attention on the game’s questions; because it offers no conventional challenge, **understanding the game becomes the challenge**[^ref-5][^ref-9]. A game whose only real activity is matching wits with the Narrator also unsettles **established definitions of games and habits of analysis**[^ref-36]. Games that layer metagame devices over richer systems may offer more varied play, but that very variety can **dilute their self-reflection.**

The chart also distinguishes answering the telephone (Apartment Ending) from unplugging it (Not Stanley, Incorrect, or Real Person Ending).

There is another productive contradiction. The Narrator may sound “surprised” by a player’s decision, but his surprise was necessarily **designed in advance**. As a metagame, *The Stanley Parable* pretends that the player has exceeded its designers’ script—as if it could execute unwritten code or tell an unwritten story. Literally that is impossible; aesthetically, the fiction becomes a metaphor for the player’s effort to outrun what the designers anticipated[^ref-12].

## II. Stanley and the Narrator: Who Is Driving the Story?

When we leave the office, the Narrator follows, speaking from an apparently **omniscient, godlike perspective**. Yet a subtle tension immediately appears between his story and the protagonist we control: **the Narrator speaks in the past tense, while we can change Stanley’s actions in the present.**

The tension first peaks at the first **explicit** choice: a hallway with two open doors. The Narrator says that Stanley took the left door. Obey him all the way and we reach the Freedom Ending—which this essay pointedly calls the “Deception Ending.” Along this route he mostly preserves the distance expected of an omniscient storyteller, but the player’s agency keeps disturbing it. In the boss’s office he casually reveals the keypad code; once the player enters it, he hastily repairs the story: “Yet incredibly, by simply pushing random buttons on the keypad, Stanley happened to input the correct code by sheer luck. Amazing.”

[fig] The first two-door choice. The English subtitle records the Narrator’s instruction to take the left door.

![Two open office doors beneath the subtitle “When Stanley came to a set of two open doors, he entered the door on his left”](attachments/roof-archive/cv8322667/08-story-screenshot.png)

Defy that first instruction and take the right door, and the Narrator again scrambles to save his account: perhaps Stanley “wanted to stop by the employee lounge first.” Once there, he extravagantly praises a room supposedly worth the detour, his voice edged with mockery. Linger too long and the joke turns reflexive: “Stanley sat around waiting for more dialogue, but when a long time had passed and there was no more, he decided that the game was trying to send him a message.”

The more the player resists, the further the Narrator falls from his original position—**omniscient, all-powerful, and outside the story**—and the more directly he interferes. His addressee also becomes uncertain: is he speaking to Stanley or to the person at the keyboard, about the game world or the world beyond it? Continue past the lounge instead of returning to the meeting room, and he begins speaking to Stanley directly. Take the lift down on the return route, initiating the Confusion Ending, and he hesitates, loses his place, and audibly leafs through papers as if checking the script. He knows that he is inside a game and can manipulate it—opening and closing doors, even restarting it—yet the later loops reveal that his knowledge and power have limits. When he finally discovers the schedule governing the Confusion Ending, he is astonished and **vows to defy it by refusing another restart**. The game restarts anyway.

The Museum Ending introduces a second, female Narrator. She mocks her male counterpart, then leads not Stanley but **the player as player** through a museum devoted to the game’s production. As the player leaves, she asks them to quit: “Press ‘escape,’ and press ‘quit.’ There’s no other way to beat this game.” The Escape Pod route goes further still: turn back as soon as Stanley enters the boss’s office and the closing doors leave the Narrator behind.

This may recall **polyphonic narration** or the **unreliable narrator**, but neither label quite captures the Narrator of *The Stanley Parable*. He certainly contradicts the world before us—the door Stanley enters, for example, or the Confusion Ending’s potted fern that will supposedly be “very important later in the story”—and he can intervene in that world. What distinguishes him is that the player’s route continually changes his position: outside the story and inside it, powerful and powerless, reliable and unreliable. In the Zending, for instance, he begs Stanley not to climb the staircase and leap to his death, yet the player remains able to do so. Since the Narrator has already demonstrated both self-awareness and the power to alter the game, perhaps his plea is another manipulation; perhaps that power has failed; perhaps something else is at work. The point is not merely that his account is uncertain, but that **the Narrator’s status is itself unstable**[^ref-9].

Like Kafka’s parables, *The Stanley Parable* **cannot be exhausted by any single interpretation; its possible readings may even contradict one another**[^ref-6]. That ambiguity is much of its appeal. (The simplest answer is the one suggested by the Insanity Ending: Stanley is mentally ill and the entire game is his delusion[^ref-9].) What follows is therefore one possible reading, and an admittedly reductive analogy: the tension between player and Narrator reflects the tension between player and game designer[^ref-32].

The essay adopts a deliberately loose shorthand: the maker is a “first author,” while a reader becomes a “second author” by interpreting the completed text. This is not a doctrine Foucault proposes. His account of the “author-function” instead helps unsettle the idea that a work’s meaning is simply owned by its biographical writer[^ref-31]; the essay uses “second author” for the reader’s active production of meaning. Games make that second role especially conspicuous[^ref-32]. Their narratives are **procedural** in the broad sense used here: they require the player’s activity to unfold, and even within a single playthrough the sequence of events may change with the player’s actions[^ref-22]. The medium ranges from *Pong* and *Pac-Man*, with their **minimal narrative**, to games with **rich, complex stories** such as *Mass Effect* and *The Last of Us*. *The Painscreek Killings* uses **interactivity and environment to deliver a nonlinear story**; *Dear Esther* uses environment chiefly to build atmosphere[^ref-7]. *White Album 2* and *Detroit: Become Human* make branching choice a selling point, while the *Grand Theft Auto* series promises open-world freedom. Yet environmental storytelling never makes every object interactive; *The Painscreek Killings* controls exploration by locking doors and distributing keys; visual novels restrict players to written options; and open worlds still contain inaccessible places and inert objects. Designers may promise greater freedom, but narrative and artistic expression require limits. To imagine a game with no constraints at all—quite apart from whether it could be programmed—is to misunderstand not only games[^ref-12], the essay argues, but artistic form itself. Its final extension of the point to reality is a polemical analogy, not a literal equivalence[^ref-24].

The Narrator is what gives *The Stanley Parable* much of its meaning and pleasure; without his tension with the protagonist, the game would be dull[^ref-2]. He tries to steer the player toward the Freedom Ending—the essay’s “Deception Ending”—and becomes more agitated and interventionist with every departure, moving from sarcasm to changing the available paths. During the Confusion Ending’s first restart, the familiar two-door hallway instead contains six doors. Wander through one and the Narrator eventually declares this “the worst adventure I’ve ever been on”: there is no story. After the next restart there are no doors, so he simply ushers the player to a destination and declares them the “winner.” The first version offers freedom for which no story has been designed; the second removes so much agency that play can barely continue[^ref-34]. Neither satisfies. A victory obtained by following a single path feels empty because it presents no real challenge, while the Games Ending’s leaderboard mocks systems that rank players and induce repetition[^ref-8].

The Narrator can alter the game, but he is neither omniscient nor omnipotent. His mistake in the Confusion Ending lets the player reach the boss’s surveillance room too early, much as designers leave gaps in their own work. In the Not Stanley Ending—also called the Incorrect or Real Person Ending—unplugging the telephone astonishes him, just as players routinely discover actions designers seem not to anticipate. (Answering it instead begins the Apartment Ending.) The world then degenerates into broken textures while he complains that the player has ruined the game[^ref-32]. He wants the player back on his route and can change some of the rules, yet his control is never absolute: Stanley can still climb the Zending staircase while the Narrator begs him to stop, and the Confusion Ending restarts despite the Narrator’s plea that it should not. The same contradiction structures actual game design: a designer determines the field of play but must still leave something for the player to do.

On the essay’s Lacanian reading, the Female Narrator occupies a position above both the male Narrator (as designer) and Stanley (as player), while remaining bound to the game as an object: she figures the **big Other**. For Lacan, communication is never simply a private exchange between two self-contained individuals; it takes place through a symbolic order that precedes them[^ref-23]. Here that means that neither designer nor player controls meaning alone: their encounter is mediated by the game’s rules and language, and by wider structures of **ideology** and **social discipline** through which both make sense of it. Those structures can redirect what a designer intends. As suggestive anecdotes from Chinese player reception, the essay points to *Command & Conquer: Red Alert 2*, whose anti-Soviet caricature could strengthen some players’ affection for the Soviet Union, and *Frostpunk*, whose emphasis on individual value some players instead read as a demonstration of discipline, faith, and collective power. These examples illustrate divergent readings, not proof of a single audience response or authorized meaning.

The Museum Ending brings that mediation into the production process itself. The Female Narrator observes that however relentlessly the male Narrator mocks Stanley and labors to complete his story, one click on “Begin the game” restores everything. The scene sounds at once like the developers mocking their own labor and complaining about players who approach games without thought or respect. As the museum ends, she implores us to quit because we “can still save these two.” The game asks to be stopped in order to make its point. That is only superficially paradoxical: refusing to continue can itself function as an ending. The 2013 game’s Steam achievement “Go outside”—earned by not playing for five years—extends the same joke beyond the running program.

A game becomes expressive through play, and that process is shot through with **tension between designer and player**. Players seek freedom and influence; designers impose obstacles and limits to shape an experience[^ref-30]. Remove either side and there is no complete play experience, while the larger symbolic order shapes their encounter. **That tension is not an accident in the player’s experience; it produces the experience.**

## III. False Freedom: Power Relations in Games

The first crucial choice is also the simplest: left door or right? More choices follow, but as we have seen, the key to *The Stanley Parable*—and, the essay proposes, to games in general—is choice[^ref-33].

The player can climb through an office window and fall into an empty white space. The Narrator remarks that Stanley first assumed he had broken the map, until the narration itself proved the excursion had been designed all along. The joke exposes the limits of choice in games: every available action belongs to a possibility space made in advance. *Grand Theft Auto* lets us address only certain NPCs and enter certain buildings; we cannot ask any passerby for directions unless that exchange was written. A visual novel offers only its scripted responses, however badly we might want to answer in our own words. *Street Fighter* lets us choose only from moves the designers provided. Game freedom is therefore bounded freedom: the ability to select among authored possibilities, not to do absolutely anything.

The Countdown Ending makes the limit explicit. The player searches for a way to stop the bomb, but no such mechanic exists, and the Narrator savors that helplessness. The window scene likewise mocks the belief that finding an apparent bug has escaped the game’s control. Most endings then restart the game beneath the line “THE END IS NEVER THE END,” returning us to Office 427. A route may take only ten or fifteen minutes, so most players try another, then another—much as visual-novel players commonly explore several routes. *The Stanley Parable* actively invites that repetition[^ref-9]. In practice, however we choose, we are likely to see every outcome, which further **drains any single choice of finality**. The structure resembles Italo Calvino’s *If on a winter’s night a traveler*: a novel about novels whose stories repeatedly break off and begin again, at once alike and different[^ref-8]. Choice feels spontaneous, yet both its means (authored options) and its usual end (exhausting the branches) have already been anticipated.

The game’s endless restarts and freely reset time raise another set of questions that space prevents us from pursuing here[^ref-39][^ref-40]. But the first point leads back outside the game: are our real choices unconstrained? Conversation with a stranger follows tacit limits; a fight follows rules; Chinese students choosing universities after the *gaokao* must navigate scores, quotas, family circumstances, and a finite list of programs. From this angle, the essay reads a game as a power structure, with game and player joined in a power relation[^ref-21]. In its Foucauldian frame, that term is not automatically praise or condemnation: subjects are formed within relations that shape what actions are possible[^ref-20]. (The protagonist will also be treated below as a subject[^ref-3].) Starting a game means accepting its rules, learning what it permits and rewards, and acting within them, much as we do in social life. Through play, the person at the keyboard enters a relation with the game and is constituted as a player-subject. Thinking about choice in games can therefore become **a way to think about choice in reality**.

Much of what we call freedom, in either sphere, is merely **formal freedom of choice**. Substantive freedom would include not only choosing among options but changing the conditions that define them[^ref-11]. In games, limiting power is necessary to artistic form[^ref-12]. What about outside them? The circular surveillance room behind the boss’s office visually evokes the panopticon discussed by Foucault[^ref-19]. The analogy is partial: panoptic discipline depends on subjects internalizing the possibility of unseen observation, whereas Stanley is addressed by a conspicuously present voice and the player knows that the game has authored the field of action. What the comparison clarifies is the asymmetry—the Narrator can monitor and classify Stanley while Stanley cannot see him—and the way the player can begin to anticipate the Narrator’s demands. The game can therefore feel dystopian[^ref-12][^ref-17] if we treat the Narrator and its systems as powers that shape available action, and Stanley’s wandering as an attempt to recover his position as a subject. On that reading, the Narrator’s moments of apparent impotence become especially suggestive[^ref-18].

## IV. Meaningless Exploration: Absurdism

Setting *The Stanley Parable* in an office is an inspired choice. The first time I played it, on a summer afternoon, I still felt a chill. There is little conventional horror, yet the game sustains unease and tension. The feeling differs from the exposed wilderness of *Firewatch*, the abandoned town of *The Painscreek Killings*, or the overt horror of *Five Nights at Freddy’s*. Why should an ordinary scene from daily life feel so disturbing?

The unease begins **immediately**. Every colleague has vanished, placing Stanley in an unfamiliar situation inside a painfully familiar environment. He searches the enormous office for a reason, but the game never supplies one. Like Kafka’s protagonists—arrested without knowing their crime or awakening as an insect—Stanley cannot explain the world around him, a classic **absurdist** predicament. The office is also a workplace shaped by modern bureaucratic life, well suited to representing alienation[^ref-17], much like the court in *The Trial* or the bureaucracy surrounding the castle in *The Castle*. Graffiti in the executive washroom, the meeting-room whiteboard, and corporate presentation slides all invite a critique of capitalist exploitation. We know Stanley only by his job, never by family, interests, or personality, which sharpens his alienation.

His story recalls Camus’s account of the moment when routine collapses into the question “why?”[^ref-28]: “It happens that the stage sets collapse. Rising, streetcar, four hours in the office or the factory, meal, streetcar, four hours of work, meal, sleep, and Monday Tuesday Wednesday Thursday Friday and Saturday according to the same rhythm—this path is easily followed most of the time. But one day the ‘why’ arises and everything begins in that weariness tinged with amazement.” Stanley begins by blindly pressing the keys his monitor requests, the daily routine of a modern office worker distilled to its absurd core. Then he is abruptly, in the essay’s phrase, “thrown into a desert, searching for meaning in a place without meaning.” The office unsettles us because it reflects our own fear of **absurdity and emptiness in ordinary life**.

Absurdist works often invite us to ask “what is happening?” and then confront the meaning of existence. *The Stanley Parable* asks “what should I do?” and turns that question toward the meaning of choice in games. Its branches embody the problem. The causal link between a decision and its ending is often obscure; why should this turn lead to that outcome? Real life is hardly more transparent. The Broom Closet Ending makes the joke wonderfully small. Most office doors are sealed, but an unmarked broom closet happens to open, and the Narrator builds a “story” around the player’s decision to remain there. Choices with no obvious meaning, and outcomes with no obvious relation to them, intensify both the game’s **absurdity** and the Narrator’s **black humor**.

Most endings return automatically to the beginning beneath the words “THE END IS NEVER THE END,” recalling the repetitive, inconclusive action of Beckett’s *Endgame*[^ref-13]. Stanley and the Narrator likewise **make one another’s roles possible**, like the mutually dependent figures of absurdist drama[^ref-13].

## V. Who Is Stanley? How the Player “Enters” the Game

We have discussed the tension between designer and player, but players can “enter” a game in many ways. *Street Fighter* and *The Last of Us* give us characters to control; *Civilization* and *Cities: Skylines* do not require a single embodied avatar. In either case, the player must be “present” to the game world by perceiving **its environments or events**[^ref-4]. Does that mean complete immersion, as though its events happened to us, or detached spectatorship without identification[^ref-4]? If immersion were ever complete, anyone who reached the white-phosphorus attack in *Spec Ops: The Line* would need prolonged counseling. We need not imagine ourselves as soldiers to play *Rainbow Six*, and someone may play a visual novel simply to see its CGs or exhaust its routes. Yet Joel’s final decision in *The Last of Us* asks for substantial **identification and empathy** if it is to feel intelligible, while *Five Nights at Freddy’s* depends on our projecting ourselves into its situation strongly enough to be frightened. Different players occupy different points between distance and identification at different moments, just as readers and filmgoers do. What difference, then, does interactivity make?

At the end of the Not Stanley Ending—not the Apartment Ending—the Narrator realizes that someone beyond the screen has been controlling Stanley. This is the branch in which the player unplugs the telephone rather than answering it. The camera shifts from first person to third and control is withdrawn; Stanley stands motionless before the original two doors while the Narrator urges him to choose for himself. He cannot. The credits roll, which has led some players to call this the 2013 game’s “true ending”—though the familiar loading screen and another restart make the scare quotes necessary. The scene distinguishes player and protagonist as two subjects whose link can be broken. Yet each also makes the other possible: without a player, Stanley can do nothing; **without the protagonist who carries us into the game, we cease to occupy the role of player.**

With a first-person game such as *Counter-Strike: Global Offensive*, or even Chinese online text role-play in which participants write in character (often called *yǔ C*, short for “language cosplay”), identification may seem straightforward: we look through a character’s eyes and respond. But how do we relate to a third-person or separately characterized avatar, such as a character in *Ace Attorney* or *Grand Theft Auto*? We control them, yet cannot choose their histories or personalities. The essay turns to Lacan’s **mirror stage** to describe that relation[^ref-16].

In Lacan’s account, an infant identifies with the coherent image in the mirror even though its own bodily experience is still fragmented and uncoordinated. The image is recognized as “me” through a formative misrecognition: an ideal unity that the subject can approach but never simply become. This identification helps organize the subject’s relation to the world[^ref-15]. Film theory has often treated the cinema screen as a kind of mirror; why not the computer screen as well? We control game characters but never literally become them. They give us capacities within a world we could not otherwise inhabit. Their fixed histories and personalities do not prevent identification: during play they can operate as second bodies through which we enter the game, while outside play we can still treat them as independent fictional people in fan works and other adaptations.

**Many games seek such identification**, for commercial or artistic reasons. Others deliberately produce **estrangement**. In *Spec Ops: The Line*, the protagonist’s perception is repeatedly distorted by hallucination, forcing us to notice both the gap between player and protagonist and the protagonist’s own estrangement from his world[^ref-3]. Strictly speaking, the player interacts not directly with the protagonist but with the game as an object, and the game mediates their connection; from another angle, it can even be treated as a subject[^ref-3]. Some games make this object function like a mirror, while others disrupt the reflection, producing a hybrid identification that belongs wholly to neither player nor character[^ref-5]. The white-phosphorus sequence makes the relation literal: the targeting display shows the protagonist’s reflected face, while the player may see their own reflection in the physical monitor. Two screens bind the two figures together[^ref-3].

In *The Pervert’s Guide to Cinema*, Žižek argues that players may take fictional personae too lightly rather than too seriously[^ref-26]. Someone who adopts a violent, domineering avatar may explain it as compensation for weakness in everyday life. Žižek reverses the explanation: perhaps the virtual persona gives freer expression to desires that social constraints suppress, so that the supposedly “false” self is psychically revealing. I do not want to decide that claim here. My point is that it assumes a degree of identification between player and avatar that is not self-evident and demands closer analysis[^ref-16][^ref-21][^ref-38]. At first glance, we control characters and use them to enact desires. Yet when we spend time, effort, and money building and leveling them, it can feel as though the characters control us, recruiting our labor to fulfill their in-game desires[^ref-42].

A brief digression: as games become more social, social life itself becomes more game-like. Leaving sponsored and influencer accounts aside, consider how people fashion images of themselves on WeChat Moments, a contact-based social feed, or on the public microblogging platform Weibo: not simple copies of offline selves, but versions of the people they would like to be. Follows, likes, and reposts operate as **feedback systems**, continually driving further engagement. Philosopher Lan Jiang’s writing on digital capitalism uses the idea of a **“virtual body”** for this second body in cyberspace[^ref-41][^ref-27]. Calls to “separate the virtual from the real” miss the point. Virtual relations are already part of reality and help constitute it; remove them and reality itself changes[^ref-26].

## VI. A Real Person: How to Become a Good Player

[fig] The 2013 game’s Steam achievements, including “Click on door 430 five times,” “Go outside,” and “Commitment.” Percentages shown are the historical unlock rates captured with the original article.

![The English Steam achievement list for *The Stanley Parable*, including “Go outside: Don’t play The Stanley Parable for five years”](attachments/roof-archive/cv8322667/13-apartment.png)

Follow every instruction and Stanley shuts down the Mind Control Facility, leaves the building, and becomes “free.” Yet that freedom is **the product of unfree choices**. We have obeyed the Narrator from beginning to end, behaving exactly like the office-bound Stanley who follows instructions from a screen and feels inexplicably content. The Freedom Ending therefore cannot straightforwardly count as the true ending or as victory. Some fan discussions instead single out the disobedient Not Stanley Ending as the “true ending,” while the Confusion Ending goes on to mock the very idea of victory. These are interpretations, not official designations.

The Steam achievements sharpen the joke. They reward not playing for five years (“Go outside”), playing for all of a Tuesday (“Commitment”), clicking door 430 five times, and restarting the game (“Welcome back!”). Players perform these arbitrary tasks because the achievement system tells them to, exposing the **absurdity and emptiness** of achievements as ends in themselves[^ref-37]. The Museum Ending even makes quitting part of the work. If a game is a means of artistic expression, then to “win” is not simply to trigger a success state but to understand what the work is doing. One short review on the game’s Douban page—Douban is a Chinese social platform for books, films, games, and reviews—joked that when the writer’s computer ran out of power during play, they counted the shutdown as another ending. Winning and losing are expressive devices, not final purposes. In *September 12th: A Toy World*, missiles aimed at terrorists inevitably kill civilians, whose survivors become new terrorists. The unwinnable system ends in ruins and uses defeat to make a political argument[^ref-22]. In competitive games, meanwhile, players may keep choosing a beloved but comparatively weak character—Ryu in the version of *Street Fighter V* current when this essay was written, for example. Winning is plainly not their only value.

*Spec Ops: The Line* can be read as a game-world cousin of *Apocalypse Now*. Near the end, after Captain Walker has lost his squad and reached Dubai’s tower, his imagined confrontation with Konrad turns into an indictment of Walker himself: the soldiers and civilians dead along the way have served his need to remain the hero, and stopping earlier would have prevented much of the carnage. The accusation reaches through Walker toward the player, just as *Apocalypse Now* addresses its audience as well as its characters. Like *The Stanley Parable*, *Spec Ops* makes **the option not to continue playing** part of its challenge to the player.

A similar tension appears in *Grand Theft Auto IV*[^ref-21]. Niko Bellic repeatedly speaks of coming to America for a new life, yet advancing the plot requires the player to keep him in crime. We can obey, leave the main missions untouched and roam Liberty City as a law-abiding citizen, or close the game and suspend our relation to Niko altogether. **Recognizing the tension does not require choosing either form of refusal.** Continuing the story can instead deepen it: Niko wants to begin again but is driven by a force outside his control—the player beyond the screen. By the same logic, *The Stanley Parable*’s Mind Control Facility can stand for the game as a whole: the machine that controls Stanley’s mind is itself part of the machine controlling him. All three responses are allowed by *GTA IV* and can produce meaning; none is automatically superior. The harm remains fictional, but the response we choose changes our ethical stance toward Niko’s story. The essay’s trolley-problem analogy is loose: its point is not that the scenarios are identical, but that **we should not mistake our choice for an absolute good or forget what it sacrifices; different choices disclose different aspects of the game.** I cannot help wondering how much more intricate—and disorienting—the relations in *The Stanley Parable* would become if Stanley had dialogue and an explicitly represented subjectivity of his own.

If quitting can carry meaning, does a game stop working on us when the program closes? “Go outside,” which requires five years away from the 2013 release, suggests otherwise. Player communities make the point more plainly[^ref-21]. They act back on games through mods and debates about competitive balance, while also producing memes and vocabularies that circulate beyond the software. A brief encounter may persist in memory or through a community long after the program closes. Video-only playthroughs further complicate who counts as a player, as does *yún wánjiā*: a usually pejorative Chinese label for people who speak as players despite knowing a game mainly through streams or recorded playthroughs.

What, then, makes a good player? *The Stanley Parable*’s answer is: **do not stop thinking**[^ref-33]. Do not simply let design lead you by the nose, as in the essay’s “Deception” Ending; do not reduce play to victory, as the Confusion Ending warns; and do not seek only a temporary escape in the Apartment Ending’s scripted domestic fantasy[^ref-37][^ref-38]. Try to **understand what the designer is attempting**, while remaining able to read beyond both programmed limits and authorial intent[^ref-29]. Even when a designer intends no grand statement, a work still bears the marks of its culture—the domain the essay has associated with the big Other. Bogost can find a tension between nostalgia and modernity in a toaster[^ref-1]; Žižek can extract everyday philosophy from a disposable Hollywood film. An attentive player should likewise find meanings that exceed a game’s explicit content. We play not merely for numbers and victories, but for an encounter among the designer’s intentions, the game’s affordances, and the player’s interpretation.

The Art Ending asks the player to perform a transparently boring task: keep pressing buttons so that a baby does not crawl into a fire and, later, a puppy is not eaten by piranhas, for four hours. Few people will persist. Those who do are rewarded with a white screen, a monolith resembling the one in *2001: A Space Odyssey*, and the Narrator’s lavish praise. It looks like recognition of endurance, but it also satirizes the **mutual self-congratulation of “art game” designers and players**: the designer reduces the player to blind obedience, adds conspicuous “art,” and congratulates everyone for profundity.

Yet a player might genuinely find a reading in those four hours: the repetitive emptiness of work, the blurred boundary between labor and games[^ref-12] (the computer is both an office tool and a game machine, and the apartment sequence turns domestic life into button prompts), or questions about play itself. What does unthinking repetition mean? How is another game different? Would an automated four-hour input change the work[^ref-5][^ref-12]? The player may reconsider the time spent pursuing victory, or mock both game and audience: perhaps the makers and admirers of *The Stanley Parable* are flattering themselves just as we flatter ourselves by extracting so much from this tedious button game[^ref-14]. But once the game has provoked those thoughts, can it still be dismissed as meaningless? The essay ends with a polemical question: if players want games recognized as art while rejecting the criticism routinely applied to novels and films as mere overinterpretation, is that not contradictory[^ref-1]? Like *Desert Bus* or Andy Warhol’s film *Empire*, this little game makes **the act of playing matter more than the finished object, and the act of interpretation matter more than either. It asks, finally, what kind of player we choose to be.**
