[author-bio] Motto: “High banks become valleys; deep valleys become hills.”

A boy is linked to the fate of the world through a girl: behold, Kojima’s *sekaikei*! (No, not quite.)

*Sekaikei* is the loose name given to stories that tie a private relationship—usually between a boy and a girl—directly to a crisis on the scale of the whole world. Politics, institutions, and everyday society, the middle ground between those two levels, fade from view. *Death Stranding* does not fit that formula neatly. But it keeps approaching the same structure from a different direction: through family.

The visual echoes of *Evangelion* are easy to spot: the Beach and the sea of LCL, giant Amelie and giant white Rei, the sixth mass extinction and Third Impact. Once you start looking, the parallels are everywhere. When Sam wakes on the Beach with five—or however many—figures floating on the horizon, my first thought was of the Mass Production Evangelions mauling Unit-02 in *The End of Evangelion*.

But the resemblance that matters is structural. Both works advertise a vast “mystery of the world,” then refuse to make it their emotional destination. Preventing the sixth mass extinction is not the climax of *Death Stranding*. The real mystery is painfully domestic: the knotted love and resentment within Sam, Amelie, and Cliff’s ersatz Holy Family, a broken parent-and-child triangle staged in overtly Christian terms. The end of the world leaves both stories stranded at the threshold of a family drama. Meanwhile, Kojima has Higgs and Die-Hardman deliver speech after speech about ropes and sticks, knots and guns, even as the game’s systems let strangers build asynchronous connections without ever meeting.

![Key visual for *Death Stranding*](attachments/roof-archive/cv4275616/02-death-stranding-key-visual.png)

That split matters because “connection” is not one thing in this game. *Death Stranding* offers three competing answers to the question of what can hold a society together.

The first belongs to the player. People freely leave ladders, roads, signs, and other useful traces for strangers they may never encounter. Taken as a fantasy of social order, this has something in common with Robert Nozick’s “minimal state”: formal authority recedes while voluntary exchange appears to organize the world by itself. The analogy only goes so far—leaving a ladder is an act of mutual aid, not a political program—but the game gives us the cheerful side of a dream often associated with Austrian economics and neoliberalism. The second answer comes from the UCA itself. Die-Hardman’s and Higgs’s ropes and sticks, knots and guns, remind us that a state also connects by applying power. Politically, this is where the rebuilding project ends up: scattered settlements are brought back under a national authority.

Then the cutscenes introduce a third answer, which we might call the “sacred state” or “Kingdom of Heaven.” This model turns political membership into a sacred family: people are bound not by reciprocal help or administrative force, but vertically, through blood, divine parentage, and love. Hence the story’s recurring images of the Son of Man, the Son of God, and the sacrificial parent. The gameplay imagines a community of strangers; the plot keeps searching for a family.

What finally holds the story together, then, is the old *Evangelion* pun, “The Beast That Shouted ‘I’ at the Heart of the World”: the Japanese *ai* can sound like the English “I” or the word for love. Sam, Amelie, and Cliff cannot recover the families they have lost, yet each still longs for family, so they assemble substitutes from the wreckage. By sending Sam back from the Beach, Amelie becomes his surrogate mother. By reviving Lou, Sam becomes Lou’s surrogate father.

This is also where the psychoanalytic “Name-of-the-Father” enters the picture. It does not simply mean one’s biological dad. It names the symbolic authority that gives kinship, law, and identity their place in an ordered world. *Death Stranding* turns the search for that authority into a succession of literal and surrogate parents. Its final question is not merely whether Sam can find a father, but whether he can occupy the position of one. In that sense, it is an *Evangelion*-like story—and perhaps a *sekaikei* story after all.

![Poster for *Evangelion: 3.0+1.0 Thrice Upon a Time*](attachments/roof-archive/cv4275616/03-evangelion-3-0-plus-1-0-poster.png)

That leaves *Death Stranding* with a serious narrative problem: its gameplay and its cutscenes pull in opposite directions. The player rebuilds the UCA—good old ’Murica—through ordinary acts of mutual aid. I leave you a bridge; someone else finishes my road. It is “one for all, all for one,” powered by fellow feeling and that little oxytocin hit when the likes arrive. The cutscenes, however, reserve their deepest bonds for blood and quasi-blood relations. Kojima makes the metaphor flesh: every strand is also an umbilical cord. The longer the story dwells on that cord, the further it moves from solidarity among strangers and toward the family.

Azuma Hiroki offers a useful way to describe this movement in his essay on *AIR*, “Before Moe: Stopping at Impotence—On *AIR*” (「萌えの手前、不能性に留まること――『AIR』について」). *AIR*, too, is a story about failing to become a father. The male player begins from the familiar romance-game fantasy of meeting a heroine and becoming her partner. But as the story stretches across time and generations, romance gives way to the harder demand of care: can he accept adult responsibility for another person, as a husband or eventually as a father? He cannot.

The “impotence” here is not simply sexual. It is the inability to assume that adult, parental position. *Sekaikei* may operate on a cosmic scale, but its emotional world rarely grows beyond one would-be family. I call the position “fatherhood,” rather than adulthood in general, because the genre—especially *sekaikei* eroge—organizes responsibility through a male player’s gaze and casts maturity in explicitly masculine, parental terms.

The emotional peaks of *Death Stranding* keep returning to that problem. Mama’s bond with her Bridge Baby turns motherhood into both attachment and loss. Heartman repeatedly dies in the hope of finding his wife and daughter. Cliff’s pursuit of Sam resolves into a father recognizing his son. Amelie returns Sam to life as a surrogate mother; Sam, in turn, brings Lou back as a surrogate father. The relationships are not all the same, but each asks who can become family after the original family has failed. Textbook *sekaikei*—or close enough that the label, so often used as a charge against a work’s narrow emotional world, begins to stick.

![Kamio Misuzu in *AIR*](attachments/roof-archive/cv4275616/04-air-misuzu.png)

This is why I have never expected very much from *Evangelion: 3.0+1.0*. The grand “mystery of the world” matters less than people imagine. Anno could fill in every last corner of the setting and still fail to give the story a climax. What matters in *sekaikei* is the personal deadlock: can Shinji, Asuka, Rei Ayanami, and Misato Katsuragi take another step toward one another, or has the story left them nowhere to go?

The first three Rebuild films—*1.0*, *2.0*, and *3.0*—gave me little reason for confidence. Did they loosen any of the original’s emotional knots? Did they even touch them? Not as far as I could see. By then, the Rebuilds had begun to feel like one giant *Evangelion* fan project. Is the atmospheric-entry battle at the beginning of *3.0* brilliantly animated? Certainly. But another virtuoso set piece from Anno the animator is not what I need. I want to know whether Anno the storyteller can get his characters out of the trap he built for them.

![Hodaka and Hina in *Weathering with You*](attachments/roof-archive/cv4275616/05-weathering-with-you-rooftop.png)

I would even call Kojima’s *Death Stranding* more thoroughly *sekaikei* than Makoto Shinkai’s work—though I am comparing modes of experience here, not claiming that the two plots are identical. *Weathering with You* formally removes the middle ground between a couple and the climate of the entire world. Yet its release was accompanied by advertising on seemingly every available surface in Japan. That campaign did not change the film’s narrative structure, but it did erode the distance it might otherwise have created from ordinary social life. A form once prized for carrying viewers away from the everyday was folded straight back into its commercial texture.

Videogames work differently. Aside from augmented-reality games, playing one requires us to enter a constructed cybernetic space. The medium begins by placing a virtual layer between the player and ordinary life. That distance is never complete, but it is built into the experience rather than undone by the mere fact that a game has been advertised. Perhaps that is one reason why cinema simply “can’t win” against games on this score.

There is a small joke hidden in the directors’ names. *Kojima* contains the character for “island” (島), while *Shinkai* contains the character for “sea” (海)—two familiar *sekaikei* motifs. Their work reverses the names. Kojima the “island” builds a sea of connections; Shinkai the “sea” remains marooned on his island.

There are other paths through the game. *Death Stranding*, for example, gives unusually concrete form to what we might call *sekaikei*’s feminized nation-state: political authority is imagined through a girl or woman whose body and fate stand in for the world. The traditional nation-state presents itself as “Big Brother,” a masculine signifier of surveillance, command, and paternal authority. *Sekaikei* replaces him with the girl at the center of the private bond. The UCA makes that substitution literal: it is a country founded by women and a political order embodied in feminine symbols. Usually, “the fate of the world” is too vague an image to take us very far. Here it has a face, a family, and a national bureaucracy. For the present argument, what matters is that even the state has been pulled into the story’s family romance.

![Sam holding Lou in *Death Stranding*](attachments/roof-archive/cv4275616/06-death-stranding-sam-and-lou.png)

One question remains: why should a quasi-family offer any resistance to neoliberalism? Neoliberal culture promises that we may become anyone, continually remaking ourselves through choices, tastes, and affiliations. The underside of that freedom is interchangeability: if every identity can be selected and revised, it becomes harder to say who any one of us is.

You can see the anxiety in online subculture groups whose names follow the template “An Encyclopedia of [Whatever] Kinks,” with the blank filled by an ever narrower object of desire. People keep adding labels to themselves, then finer labels, then labels for ever smaller niches. Even sexuality—the part of the self that ought to feel most intimately ours—starts to seem indefinite. A tag tells a platform what category we occupy; it does not tell another person who we are.

Kinship appears to answer that problem because it binds identity to a history and to particular others. Who is Sam Bridges? A son, a brother, a surrogate child, Lou’s father: the answer arrives as a list of relations he cannot simply swap out for new ones. In this sense, family becomes the last anchor of identity. But an anchor is not an escape. Replacing the market’s endless labels with blood or simulated blood merely trades one binding order for another; it does nothing to remove the conditions that made everyone feel interchangeable in the first place. Does that substitution solve the problem? Of course not.

## Tea-Drinking Comments

**Red Tea:** By Azuma’s definition, Mi Ze Wei Hua is describing the intellectual climate behind *sekaikei* more than the distinctive otaku imagination that emerged from it. The postmodern decline of grand narratives can produce many different imaginative forms. *Sekaikei* is one particular response: it refracts that historical condition through the emotional bond between “you,” a girl, and “me.”

So when Mi Ze Wei Hua reads *Death Stranding* through the problem of the family and calls it more *sekaikei* than *Weathering with You*, what he really means is that *Death Stranding* has been more thoroughly stripped of grand narrative—not that it is more *sekaikei*. To carry the argument further, he would need to explain how cooperation among interchangeable strangers—any one of whom might happen to leave the ladder—gives way to *sekaikei*’s bond with one irreplaceable person: “you,” the girl, in relation to “me.”

That is one problem of contingency; Azuma’s account of family poses another. A family is not guaranteed in advance by nature or grand narrative. It may be assembled through accidents, choices, and substitute relations, even though the people inside it then become irreplaceable to one another. The essay exposes the ideological work performed by *sekaikei* and asks whether quasi-family and nation-state can still sustain a shared narrative. It has not yet explained how a family can be contingent in its formation while feeling necessary from within.
