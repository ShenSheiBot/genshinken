Tomohiro Furukawa gave anime fans a striking visual experience with *Revue Starlight*. In this first interview tracing the sources of his creativity, he talks about the director’s “formal beauty” that he found in the theatrical installment of the hit anime based on Masami Kurumada’s manga.

## Yamauchi’s *Saint Seiya* Is Packed with Formal Beauty

**—The first title you chose is *Saint Seiya: Legend of Crimson Youth* (hereafter *Legend of Crimson Youth*). What does this film mean to you as a director?**

**Furukawa:** It was the first anime I ever became obsessed with. The *Saint Seiya* TV anime started when I was in kindergarten or first grade, and I encountered the anime before I read the original manga. I got completely hooked, and somewhere in the middle of that I discovered *Legend of Crimson Youth*. I probably didn’t see it in a theater; I think I watched it on a rental tape my mother brought home. She probably rented all of the theatrical films for me. But *Saint Seiya: The Heated Battle of the Gods* and *Legend of Crimson Youth* left such an overwhelmingly strong impression that, until I was an adult, I thought there had only ever been two *Saint Seiya* movies and that *Legend of Crimson Youth* had been made as the grand culmination of the animated *Saint Seiya*. (laughs)

**—Before the internet, it wasn’t exactly easy to look these things up.**

**Furukawa:** Right. Around the time I entered university I finally got internet access, so I looked it up and was shocked: “Oh! The only *Saint Seiya* movies that stuck in my memory are the ones directed by this guy named Shigeyasu Yamauchi!” Later I got to work with Yamauchi on *Dream Eater Merry*, and I made sure we had a proper conversation about the background art in the *Saint Seiya* movies.

**—I’m jealous. The art direction has such a wonderful atmosphere.**

**Furukawa:** That was when Tadao Kubota of Mukuo Studio was really in his prime. (He was the art director, credited jointly with Fumihiro Uchikawa.) And it isn’t only the art. Yamauchi’s *Saint Seiya* is just packed with formal beauty in every respect. That’s what makes it so good. With *Legend of Crimson Youth*, everything is already there in the way the film opens. It starts with a silent scene of destruction … a premonition of the future, cutting back and forth with Athena (Saori), surrounded by beautiful flowers, playing with white butterflies. Then a black butterfly flutters through this space of images. That beautiful black butterfly is superimposed on the image of destruction. Beauty and death—those two spectacles alternate, and yet there are no sound effects. It’s fantastic. I was electrified even as a child, and watching it again as an adult, it’s simply too perfect as a film opening!!!! Sorry, sorry—I got a little carried away. (laughs)

> Going to save Saori is, at the same time,
> going to recover the grounds
> for who they themselves are—
> that is what the story becomes.
>
> 沙織を救いに行くことが同時に
> 自分たちが何者であるかの
> 根拠を取り戻しに行く
> 話になっている

**—No, no, that’s exactly the kind of thing we want to hear in this series! And that scene is quite long. Seiya and the other main characters barely appear for a while.**

**Furukawa:** Exactly. And in the scenes before Seiya and the others appear, Abel—the guest antagonist in *Legend of Crimson Youth*—and Saori are supposed to be brother and sister, but the film very clearly depicts the two of them as “a man and a woman.” So when Saori later says goodbye to Seiya and the others, I remember thinking, “Whoa, they took his girl!” And I was still a kid who didn’t understand anything about relationships between men and women.

**—It really does feel like someone’s girlfriend has been stolen away.**

**Furukawa:** And then later, after Athena dies, Abel kisses her goodbye even though they’re siblings. I remember getting confused and thinking, “They’re breaking the taboo—incest!” A lot of adults may think that kind of thing is “something children won’t understand,” but even if kids don’t understand it, they can still feel that it’s unpleasant. Yamauchi seemed very clearly to understand that “even children are going to hate this,” and he deliberately pushed right at it. And Greek mythology itself is full of outrageous taboos, incest included. I suspect he used that as an excuse to control the work. Even if someone objected to the direction of the story, he could say, “This work uses Greek mythology as its motif, and in that world this sort of thing is normal, so let’s do it.” The more I’ve worked as a director myself, the more I’ve come to love that feeling of making a work by exploiting the setting to the limit. The highly accomplished episode directors who have worked at Toei Animation each have their own techniques for getting around all kinds of restrictions, and I have this image of those techniques becoming part of each director’s auteurial signature. In this film, I feel Yamauchi’s techniques are on full display. And there’s another key point: the film puts great importance on the act of “taking away.”

**—“Taking away”?**

**Furukawa:** What does it mean for Seiya and the other Saints when Abel takes Saori away? The film really depicts that carefully. The instant the person they are supposed to protect is taken from them, they go from being “Saints who have fought since the age of myth” back to being “ordinary boys.” Looking back now, I wonder whether the women who were older than me at the time were watching Seiya and the others in that way: beautiful, fragile boys who could collapse the moment something set them off … And in the second half of the movie they have to fight the usurper, but going to save Saori is at the same time a story about recovering the grounds for who Seiya and the others themselves are. In other words, it becomes a film about boys fighting to recover themselves, and in that sense I think it’s actually very wholesome as a work for boys. Abel and Saori’s incestuous eroticism and the boys’ wholesomeness coexist in the same film, which is probably why the whole thing has this strangely humid quality.

**—I see …!**

**Furukawa:** And then you get one wonderful scene after another of these wholesome, beautiful boys being beaten to a pulp, which gives the whole thing a very fetish-y quality. Shingo Araki’s way of drawing bodies is already fetishistic to begin with, and then they get smashed face-first and the blood just goes gushing everywhere! That direction is great too. And alongside the lavish animation you only get in a theatrical film, it also properly uses the sliding still-frame expressions that the TV series invented as something distinctively *Saint Seiya*. That’s what fans want to see, after all—as formal beauty. Those refined expressions that arise from working within restrictions are much harder to produce in anime production today. The fact that you can see them so clearly here is another thing that makes this film so good.
