Monster movies are one of cinema’s most enduring genres. This is not a history of the genre so much as a personal route through it, beginning with Merian C. Cooper’s 1933 *King Kong* and then jumping to two landmarks from the early 1950s: *The Beast from 20,000 Fathoms* and the original *Godzilla*.[^1] The boom that followed *Godzilla* produced a flood of new films, some excellent and many not. By the time we got the American *Godzilla* in 1998 (honestly, I would sooner watch *DARLING in the FRANXX* or *The Lost Village* ten times than sit through that embarrassing thing crawling around New York) and Japan’s *Godzilla: Final Wars* in 2004, good monster movies seemed increasingly hard to find. Then came *Pacific Rim*: “a Mexican director using Hollywood money to make a Japanese monster movie.” Its greatest achievement, by far, is its first-rate visual effects, which still hold up today.

![Poster for Pacific Rim](attachments/roof-archive/cv1641117/pacific-rim-poster.jpg)

Take *Pacific Rim* and the 2014 American *Godzilla*. Once someone has bought a ticket and settled into a theater, what will keep their butt in the seat for the next two hours? Both films answer with superb visual effects: buildings collapse with convincing weight, the Golden Gate Bridge gets destroyed yet again (Q: What is the Golden Gate Bridge for in movies? A: Getting destroyed), and the audience thinks, damn, that giant monster is ridiculously cool. No wonder monster movies can look, at first glance, like special-effects showcases little different from Marvel’s superhero blockbusters.

![Poster for Shin Godzilla](attachments/roof-archive/cv1641117/shin-godzilla-poster.jpg)

But are monster movies really nothing more than effects blockbusters? Convincing effects certainly matter. A 100-meter-tall creature merely strolling around will never make the same impression as one firing a beam from its mouth and slicing a skyscraper as tall as itself—or taller—cleanly in half. Yet the monster need not be the story’s protagonist. It is hard to call Godzilla the protagonist of the original 1954 film, and in Hideaki Anno’s 2016 *Shin Godzilla*, the central character is not even a person: it is the Japanese government. The nominal male lead heads the task force responding to Godzilla, but he matters chiefly as the face of that institution. Both films are excellent, and neither depends on treating Godzilla as a conventional hero. The original needs no defense; *Shin Godzilla* finished second among domestic Japanese films at the 2016 box office, behind only *Your Name.*

Together, the two films show that spectacle alone does not have to carry a monster movie. Story matters just as much, and a good monster-movie script is rare. Takeo Murata and director Ishirō Honda wrote the 1954 *Godzilla*, with Eiji Tsuburaya directing the special effects. That division of labor matters: the film’s human drama and its creature effects were built to support each other. Hideaki Anno, who wrote *Shin Godzilla*, brought to it both a screenwriter’s sense of institutions under pressure and a lifelong love of tokusatsu. (Anno, asked about the ending of *Rebuild of Evangelion*: I’m working on it, I’m working on it.jpg.) Among anime fans, his name recognition now arguably trails only Hayao Miyazaki’s, though his relationship with tokusatsu deserves an essay of its own.

For me, a great monster movie is not ultimately a movie about monsters. It is a movie about the people—and sometimes the institutions—forced to respond. What, then, does the gigantic creature represent? Disaster—but not merely a disaster visited upon humanity. The monster may be a victim of that disaster too. In the 1954 *Godzilla*, nuclear testing awakens an ancient creature from its sleep. In *Shin Godzilla*, an unknown marine organism evolves after years of feeding on radioactive waste dumped by human beings. An ordinary disaster film asks how people confront an impersonal catastrophe. A monster movie can give that catastrophe a body, a history, and perhaps a consciousness. Humanity must decide how to respond, while the monster has been wrenched into an existence it never chose. To wake or mutate because of human interference, then find no place for oneself on the planet, is its own tragedy. A line often attributed to Ishirō Honda captures the idea: “Monsters are tragic beings. They are born too tall, too strong, too heavy—that is their tragedy.”

![Darth Vader’s “I am your father” reveal, used here as an example of story outlasting spectacle](attachments/roof-archive/cv1641117/translations/en/darth-vader-i-am-your-father.png)

So, back to the audience: when we watch monster movies, what are we really watching? Even the finest crew in the world cannot make a great film without a great script. What many people remember from *Star Wars* is not one more exchange of lightsaber blows, but Darth Vader’s revelation: “No, I am your father.” That image is an analogy, not evidence about monster movies; its point is simply that spectacle becomes memorable when a story gives it weight. Effects can bring in enormous box-office returns, but revenue alone does not decide which films later generations remember.

The films that endure have something of their own to say. The 1954 *Godzilla*, for example, reflects not only on nuclear weapons but on the logic of weapons themselves. Dr. Serizawa creates the Oxygen Destroyer, a weapon even more powerful than nuclear arms, then chooses to die with Godzilla so that humanity can never use his invention in war. The effects give physical form to that argument: they let us experience a creature as a living natural disaster rather than merely hear one described. The audience may come to see Godzilla destroy a city, but the film asks what created this catastrophe, how people answer it, and what their answer might unleash next. Those are the questions worth carrying out of the theater.

[^1]: Of course, I have no objection if some people also count *Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde* or *Frankenstein* as monster movies. But if Mr. Hyde and Frankenstein’s creature are monsters, should we count the Hulk as one too? After all, these stories all stage human animality, or place the beast inside our idea of the human.

## Fenglinhuoshan’s Note

This essay gets at something central to tokusatsu—and to science fiction, anime, and related forms more broadly: however strange the premise, these genres finally concern human conduct.

Whether the subject is a monster or a robot, Ultraman or Kamen Rider, the drama returns to what people do when confronted with something beyond their control. I have not seen many monster movies, but I still vividly remember the moment in *King Kong* when airplanes shoot Kong from the Empire State Building. His enormous body falls in a struggle driven by human self-interest, greed, hunger for fame, and cowardice. Against that, the film sets a heroine who is independent, strong, and brave, and a Kong willing to sacrifice himself for love. *King Kong* became a classic, I think, because its spectacle lays bare human character, exposes the society that produced it, and speaks critically to its time. Likewise, *Godzilla* and its sequels ask what industrial pollution and nuclear weapons do to the world, how governments behave in a crisis, and what people will sacrifice in the name of national interest. Those questions are what give the monsters their force.

This does not make effects dispensable. Effects stage those conflicts in bodies, movement, and ruined cityscapes; they make an argument felt. Once we approach tokusatsu as a way of thinking about people and society, we have an answer to the reflexive dismissal that “Ultraman is childish,” “Kamen Rider is childish,” and so on.

## Li Paopao’s Note

This review argues that “a great monster movie is not a movie about monsters, but a movie about people.” It is an intriguing claim, but I do not think it goes far enough. The monster may be a tragic character, as the essay says, while the human institutions inside the story still treat it as nothing but a threat. The film itself can frame either side sympathetically—or keep wavering between them. What, then, do monster movies ask us to think about?

In one respect, monster movies can loosen our anthropocentrism. A monster’s gigantic body and destructive power force human beings to acknowledge a nonhuman presence that cannot be relegated to the scenery. Fear alone does not make that shift; the film must also let us see the monster as a creature with a history and a claim on the world. When it does, human intelligence no longer guarantees mastery, and human life is no longer the only life that matters. **Before the monster, humanity is made to feel its own smallness.** Films such as *Godzilla* go further by making the monster a product of the ecological damage wrought by industrial civilization. The creature then becomes both a threat and evidence of what human beings have done to their environment.

Yet the same films can also reinforce anthropocentrism. Their human institutions label the monster a “living natural disaster,” organize society around its destruction, and invite the audience to cheer for humanity as a single community under threat. **Within that institutional view, the monster becomes a mindless object that knows only destruction**, not another living creature with which coexistence might be possible. A film may pity the monster as a character and still resolve its plot by making humanity’s survival the only moral horizon. Subduing the monster then becomes a fantasy of subduing nature. *King Kong* and *Godzilla* expose this tension, but neither pursues the ecological critique very far or resolves it.

*Ultraman Gaia* offers a different possibility through Hiroya Fujimiya. The Radical Destruction Bringer feeds him a distorted account of Earth’s crisis: humanity appears to be the true disaster, while the planet’s monsters appear to be its natural defenders. Fujimiya accepts that account and turns against humanity. His conclusion is manipulated and extreme, but the question it raises is not therefore worthless. By treating monsters as part of the Earth rather than as invaders without minds of their own, **his position challenges the older Ultraman habit of presenting them as targets to be destroyed.**

The series eventually moves beyond both Fujimiya’s anti-human conclusion and humanity’s claim to absolute priority. In the final battle, Earth’s monsters fight alongside human beings against the Radical Destruction Bringer. They are no longer mere hazards or tools; **they act as beings capable of making a moral choice.** The scene imagines coexistence not as humanity graciously sparing nature, but as different forms of life defending a shared world. That, I think, is what a monster movie should aspire to.
