> A damsel with a dulcimer,<br>
> In a vision once I saw:<br>
> It was an Abyssinian maid,<br>
> And on her dulcimer she played,<br>
> Singing of Mount Abora.

> —Samuel Taylor Coleridge, “Kubla Khan”

No myth reaches us with its first set of eyes. However ancient the setting, every retelling draws on the habits and instruments of its own present. Those instruments change what bodies, distances, and spans of time a storyteller can imagine.[^animation-machine-1] Coleridge’s Xanadu offers a modest example. Ted Nelson borrowed the name for Project Xanadu, his long-running design for a network of linked documents.[^animation-machine-2] The computer project did not remake “Kubla Khan,” and hypertext does not spring to mind for most readers of the poem. But an old literary place came to name a technical future, and readers who know both may now hear a faint echo of one in the other.

Isao Takahata’s *The Tale of the Princess Kaguya* (2013) makes the journey in the opposite direction: it carries a modern way of seeing into an ancient story. In both cases, inherited imagination and technical modernity each change how the other can be seen. This two-way traffic also complicates a contrast Thomas Lamarre draws between two currents in Japanese animation. On-screen, Studio Ghibli tends to hold technology at a distance, while GAINAX displays machinery and makes a spectacle of its operation.[^animation-machine-18] The contrast is real, but a machine shown in a film is only one form technology takes. There is also the equipment that produces the image, the workflow that allocates shots, and the schedule that organizes the working day.

By **technology**, I mean a material system that joins tools, procedures, and organized labor to make an operation repeatable. Such a system converts some part of experience into a stable, reproducible form that makes coordinated action possible. Spacecraft and cameras turn an otherwise inaccessible planetary vantage into images; clocks turn passing duration into standard hours that synchronize separate bodies; an animation pipeline turns imagined motion into drawings, assigned shots, revisions, and deadlines. These systems have different purposes and political stakes. What they share is this practical conversion, not a moral identity. Classification is one such operation: it enters the mechanism when measurements, records, schedules, or chains of command make a category actionable. Ghibli can therefore banish machines from its landscapes while relying on technical arrangements that shape both what we see and the work that lets us see it.

## Poor Life, Not Pastoral Innocence

Nature often seems to be Ghibli’s answer to the damage its characters suffer. In *Princess Kaguya*, that answer first appears in the countryside where Kaguya grows up. Yet Takahata never offers the place as an innocent pastoral. When Kaguya flees the capital and returns home, she finds a bare winter forest instead of the green refuge she remembers. The people who live there must find food, cut fuel, build shelter, and move on when the land can no longer sustain their work. Here, **materiality** means the pressure that physical circumstances exert on what people can do and imagine, not a picturesque glow of authenticity.[^animation-machine-3] Sutemaru steals in the capital, but his deprivation did not begin at the city gate.

The fact that he is poor does not make his theft harmless. A material account neither excuses him nor reduces the act to bad character. Maria Chehonadskih’s idea of **poor life** takes poverty seriously without allowing deprivation to exhaust the meaning of a poor person’s existence. People living amid damage still invent ways of living, make things, form attachments, and flourish in ways that cannot be measured by usefulness alone. Chehonadskih’s term differs from “bare life,” which names a political condition discussed below.[^animation-machine-5]

Andrei Platonov’s *Chevengur* imagines poor life through objects as well as people. Fredric Jameson pauses over a discarded shoe: its leather decays as a red-willow shoot grows through it, and the roots preserve the shoe’s outline even as the object sinks back into the soil.[^animation-machine-4] Takahata gives us a gentler echo when a charcoal burner shows Kaguya a tender new shoot. Kaguya herself first appears inside bamboo. In every case, growth comes from a world already marked by use and damage. It does not redeem deprivation; it keeps deprivation from having the last word. The countryside can nourish Kaguya and brutalize Sutemaru at the same time.

*Chevengur* is just as divided about machinery. One peasant believes industrial labor consumes the natural world it claims to master. Another greets the wheels and breathing of a locomotive with almost Futurist delight.[^animation-machine-4] Fear of industrial destruction and desire for industrial power coexist in the same poor countryside. Nature has no single, pure voice.

*Princess Kaguya*, by contrast, leaves most visible machinery outside the story. Its loose lines and pale washes recall old picture scrolls, making a vast contemporary production feel as immediate as a brush touching paper.[^animation-machine-6] In his later account of Takahata, Lamarre notes that the director wanted to limit digital equipment while also loosening the specialized division of tasks and fixed working relationships of industrial animation.[^animation-machine-7] That account does attend to labor. The tension appears when we place Lamarre’s production account beside the simpler Ghibli–GAINAX contrast at the level of the finished image. Once production enters the frame, “handmade” no longer means “outside technology.” It names an effect that a large, carefully organized production has labored to create.

## The View from Outside

Near the end of the film, Kaguya looks back at Earth. Her pale profile fills the left half of the frame. A small blue planet hangs in the black distance, while the white curve of the Moon enters at lower right.

[图题] Kaguya faces a stylized full Earth whose scale and coloring recall the planetary photographs of the space age.

![Princess Kaguya looking back at the blue Earth from space](/attachments/roof-archive/cv11249482/7170235113b6afec7d1d3b896403d56d52a5abb0.jpg)

The vantage makes sense within the plot: Kaguya is traveling from Earth to the Moon. The picture she shares with the audience, however, belongs to a particular historical moment. Apollo 17 produced the photograph known as the Blue Marble in 1972. Takahata’s image is not a copy of it, but it uses the same visual convention: Earth appears all at once, bright and bounded against empty space. Spaceflight and photography made this view familiar enough to enter a Heian-period tale without explanation. Modern technology supplies no prop to the scene. It supplies its visual grammar.

Peter Sloterdijk helps name the change in scale. We inhabit a **world** among paths, seasons, and other people, within a horizon that moves as we do. A **sphere** is something we picture from outside. The globe compresses innumerable local worlds into a single object of sight.[^animation-machine-8] Seen this way, our common home has a sharp edge. Its finitude becomes vivid, as does its vulnerability.

The full-Earth image compresses many lives into a single bounded object, but that visual unity tells us nothing about how vulnerability is distributed. Giorgio Agamben’s **bare life** names the political reduction of a person to biological existence exposed to sovereign violence. Judith Butler’s **precarious life** begins from bodily dependence and asks why social arrangements expose some lives far more than others. Both concepts keep the planetary image from smoothing over what institutions do to actual bodies; neither follows from the globe’s shape alone. Takahata uses the image to move between two more immediate scales of finitude: the life of a forest and Kaguya’s impending separation from Earth.

At ground level, finitude appears as a cycle of use and recovery. The charcoal burners leave the mountain after felling its trees, and one of them directs Kaguya’s attention to new growth on the stripped slope. Their departure neither erases the damage nor turns extraction into conservation. The shoot tells us only that the forest’s time exceeds the present harvest.

Kafka’s Odradek sharpens that thought from another angle. The spool-like creature in “The Cares of a Family Man” has no discoverable function, yet seems complete in itself. The family man is troubled by the thought that this useless household remainder may outlive him.[^animation-machine-9] Odradek is not nature, and Kafka’s house is not Takahata’s forest. The exact point of contact lies in the discarded shoe and willow shoot from *Chevengur*: used matter persists and takes forms that the household neither intended nor knows how to value. Utility does not decide when a thing must disappear.

The distant Earth turns planetary finitude into personal loss. Kaguya is being carried away from every place and person she knows. Heidegger’s **being-toward-death** does not mean calmly accepting death. It names a limit that no one else can undergo for me, and that gives my choices their urgency. Kaguya’s departure is not death, but the scene draws its force from a similarly untransferable ending.

Her agency is divided across several moments. When the Emperor corners and embraces her, Kaguya panics and calls on the Moon to rescue her. That plea summons the lunar envoys. Once she grasps the consequence, she wants to stay on Earth, but the call cannot be withdrawn. Her cry rejected the court’s claim on her body; it was never a considered preference for lunar immortality. The Moon responds to the plea she made under coercion by stripping away everything she had hoped to save: memory, attachment, grief, and joy. The celestial robe will make her forget.

Her departure therefore cannot be reduced to country against city or nature against civilization. She has lost the countryside of her childhood, cannot build a life with Sutemaru, and refuses the noblewoman’s role her father has chosen for her. In her last exchange with her parents, she defends sorrow because it belongs to the same earthly life as happiness. Yet her appeal to the Moon turns that refusal against her. The act becomes self-destructive because a desperate bid for escape hardens into an irreversible sentence of oblivion, not because she calmly wishes for death. Her resistance recalls Antigone’s refusal of an acceptable compromise, but the analogy must not beautify coercion and loss as willing sacrifice.[^animation-machine-10]

## Life Against the Machine

The film’s contrast between Moon and Earth makes **life** mean more than biological survival. It includes touch, memory, attachment, loss, and the certainty of death. That cluster of values has a troubling history. The German **philosophy of life**—*Lebensphilosophie*—also opposed lived experience, bodily time, intuition, and organic growth to rational abstraction and the machine.[^animation-machine-11]

The comparison between Takahata’s film and reactionary *Lebensphilosophie* turns on a particular sequence. Modern regulation first appears lifeless and mechanical. Immediate sensation then promises to restore an older organic wholeness. Finally—and here aesthetics becomes politics—struggle, suffering, or death is taken as proof that a person belongs to that whole. Takahata’s film stages the first conflict and draws emotional force from the second promise. It does not make the third move, much less inherit a German political program. The history matters because it identifies the hinge at which a longing for wholeness can become a politics willing to spend individual lives for an imagined collective.

Nitzan Lebovic traces that turn within the reactionary branch of the philosophy of life. In the youth-education programs he studies, peril, struggle, and readiness for death became ideals for training German students. During a mid-1920s debate over J. J. Bachofen’s work on “mother right,” some German interpreters transformed a theory of ancient social origins into a myth of motherhood and fate: the mother gives both life and death, and nature embraces the two together.[^animation-machine-11] In one setting, vitalist ideas became an organized education in struggle; in the other, they made mortality serve a story of return to origins.

The proto-fascist potential of reactionary vitalism lies here. It emerges when the organic whole outranks the people who compose it, pain becomes proof of vitality, and unity demands that alien, sick, or rootless bodies be identified and expelled. Institutions turn that demand into practice through teaching, medical categories, administration, and political force. These mechanisms made the rhetoric of life available to fascism.

The philosophy of life did not begin with the Nazi state. It arose in a fin-de-siècle culture exhilarated by speed and terrified of exhaustion. Its revolt against abstract reason sought bodily immediacy and a unity older than conscious thought. At the same time, the language of fatigue and degeneration was becoming racialized.

A separate archive makes the exclusionary logic brutally concrete. Daniel Paul Schreber’s memoir of mental illness records an antisemitic fantasy in which the Wandering Jew carries syphilis and corrupts an imagined Aryan body over centuries.[^animation-machine-12] The memoir is not a link in some chain of influence from vitalism to Takahata. It shows how a story of social decline can be recast as disease, then assigned to the body of an outsider.

Kafka’s Odradek offers a literary counterimage. “The Cares of a Family Man” need not be an answer to the philosophy of life for the contrast to matter. Some interpreters hear Jewish associations in Odradek’s unplaceable name and in the patriarch’s fear of a creature he can neither assimilate nor discipline nor outlive. That coding remains an interpretation. The reversal is plain on the page: the apparently useless remnant survives the household that cannot classify it. The dream of a pure, seamless community breaks against this obstinate little thing.

Odradek exposes organic unity by embodying what it cannot absorb. Takahata’s films pose a related but different test: does natural beauty promise restoration, or reveal what beauty cannot repair? In *Princess Kaguya*, the green countryside and changing seasons answer the capital’s rigid names, lessons, clothes, and marriage market. In *Pom Poko* (1994), the tanuki’s great spectral parade briefly overlays the suburbs consuming the Tama Hills with vanished animals and landscapes. The vision ends; development continues; the survivors must disguise themselves or retreat. *Grave of the Fireflies* (1988) is more bitter. Fireflies turn Seita and Setsuko’s shelter into a private constellation, then lie dead the next morning as the children’s own resources run out. Nature offers beauty and likeness, not rescue. These scenes promise no reunion between the individual and an organic whole. They ask instead: what does such beauty help us overlook—history, labor, or abandoned lives?

Reactionary vitalists looked to the image as a route back to primordial life. For Ludwig Klages, an image was more than a copy of an object; it could put the viewer in direct contact with primordial life. Reading Nietzsche, he pictured Greek prehistory as an ecstatic flood of color, sound, scent, and erotic feeling through which the individual met a world-soul. His account of Chronos set bodily duration against dates, clocks, and other measured units. Sensory images were meant to carry the modern subject back beyond the clock’s rule.[^animation-machine-11]

That theory helps explain the appeal of Takahata’s animation without requiring a debt to Klages. An earlier essay on Takahata describes Kaguya’s frantic run after the naming banquet. Her formal robes fly apart, faces and buildings dissolve, and the brush line seems to tremble with rage, sorrow, and speed. The essay invokes Henri Bergson’s lived duration, in which feelings and movements flow into one another instead of lining up like seconds on a dial.[^animation-machine-13] Takahata does more than depict Kaguya’s emotion. The line’s shifting density and violence make the spectator feel its motion.

Mamoru Oshii’s *Ghost in the Shell* (1995) creates a comparable break by nearly opposite means. Its long city interlude leaves the investigation behind to watch rain, traffic, canals, shop windows, mannequins, and anonymous faces. Reflections double bodies; a woman who resembles the Major passes without explanation. Music holds the fragments together while the plot waits. Kaguya’s run accelerates until the world comes loose. Oshii’s montage slows until the city ceases to look like scenery for a mission. In both sequences, animation stops carrying events transparently and becomes the event to which we attend.

The rail journey into the Zone in Andrei Tarkovsky’s *Stalker* (1979) produces a third kind of suspension.[^animation-machine-14] The camera holds on the travelers’ faces while the trolley clatters and the industrial wasteland slides behind them. The journey has a destination, but its length and rhythm make the passage itself palpable. Plot gives us **dramatic time**; the duration we must sit through becomes **experienced time**. Sound and texture loosen the destination’s hold on the scene.

Žižek’s account of Tarkovsky’s dream imagery clarifies the appeal: dreams do not carry us away from sensuous reality but strip away our intellectual distance until matter presses against us.[^animation-machine-14] Kaguya’s breaking line, Oshii’s rain-dark city, and Tarkovsky’s metallic rhythm all weaken the plot’s command and intensify sensation. The achievement resembles the vitalist hope that images can reach beneath measured, rational time. It turns political only when sensuous immediacy is offered as evidence of an organic whole to which actual lives must submit.

## Dream Time, Factory Time

How could a philosophy that denounced mechanical modernity flourish in an industrial society, then coexist with the technical apparatus of Nazism? The paradox becomes easier to understand once we look at the daily experience from which it emerged. The dream of unmeasured life arose after clock time had already entered the body.

*Chevengur* makes that entry almost comic. A watchman senses each hour approaching even in his sleep. He wakes, rings the bell, feels the pressure lift, and starts waiting for the next hour. Jameson calls this an inaugural experience of secular, organic time.[^animation-machine-4] It is secular because a civic schedule, not a sacred calendar, sets the rhythm. It feels organic because the watchman’s nerves now keep time without conscious calculation. Yet the unit governing his body—the equal, repeatable hour—is artificial. He wakes for sixty minutes, not for sunrise, hunger, or a change in weather.

The clock supplies the unit, and the bell coordinates everyone subject to it. This is the practical mechanism of **clock discipline**. As factories and other large organizations moved workers away from variable, task-based rhythms, a common timetable coordinated when many people worked, rested, and left.[^animation-machine-15] From the 1880s onward, urban electric-light networks extended the usable evening, so darkness mattered less in the timing of work and leisure.[^animation-machine-16] Sleep and bodily rhythms remained, but dawn and dusk lost some of their authority as institutions gained more power to schedule the day.

Dreams took on new intellectual weight in this reorganized night. Psychoanalysis and the philosophy of life both treated dreaming as continuous with waking existence rather than as mental noise, though they wanted different things from it. Psychoanalysis read dreams for conflict, desire, and repression. Klages saw relief from rational distance and a return to images older than the calculating self. The two projects could oppose one another while responding to the same disruption of waking and sleep.

Nineteenth-century science offered another route by which the machine entered the body. Helmholtz’s 1847 formulation of the conservation of energy gave physics a general account of energy that could neither arise from nothing nor vanish. Physiologists, engineers, and researchers of industrial work adapted its vocabulary to measure exertion, fatigue, food, heat, and useful output. In Anson Rabinbach’s history, the worker becomes a **human motor**, with labor power that can be depleted and restored.[^animation-machine-17] The metaphor turns a working body into an energy converter and recasts exhaustion as a problem of input, loss, and efficiency.

Descartes had distinguished ensouled human beings from animal-machines in the *Discourse on Method* (1637). The new calculations did not have to refute him. A physiologist could leave the soul untouched while measuring the body’s heat and work. Thermodynamics did not explain the whole person; it made particular bodily processes available to management.

The passage from energy science to racist politics was institutional, not logical. Racial medicine helped build it by treating a population as one vulnerable organism and social difference as infection or degeneration. Schreber’s antisemitic fantasy condenses the result: the Wandering Jew gathers syphilis, sexual excess, rootlessness, and racial mixture into one body marked for exclusion. The supposedly healthy national body is promised more than efficiency. Discipline and purification will restore its energy, ecstasy, and unity with life.

Reactionary vitalism could thus attack the machine while borrowing the machine age’s habits of thought. It rejected clockwork reason but imagined the population as a system whose energy had to be guarded. It celebrated bodily immediacy while relying on schools, medicine, and administration to produce the right kind of body. The revolt was “mechanized from within” in a precise sense: its escape from technical order depended on technical measurements and institutions to decide who belonged to living wholeness.

## The Machinery Behind the Image

We can now return to Lamarre’s distinction and state the common mechanism without flattening its results. Kaguya’s view of Earth and the Zero fighter in *The Wind Rises* (2013) are neither morally nor technically equivalent. A spacecraft-and-camera system fixes a full-Earth vantage in an image that can circulate far beyond the body and location of the person who took it. Aerodynamic calculations, technical drawings, standardized parts, factories, and the Japanese military state turn a design for flight into reproducible weapons. In each case, instruments, procedures, and organized labor stabilize a possibility in a form that a coordinated project can repeat. One result is a visual convention; the other is a military aircraft. The shared operation does not erase that difference—it makes the difference legible.

*Grave of the Fireflies* turns the issue into one of historical vision. The film opens with Seita dying in a railway station in 1945. At the end, the spirits of Seita and Setsuko sit above the brilliantly lit skyline of modern Kobe. By holding the dead children and postwar prosperity in the same frame, the film places lives omitted from a triumphant story of national recovery back inside it.

Ghibli need not endorse every machine it draws for these examples to matter. They show why an on-screen choice between nature and technology cannot settle the argument. A pastoral image may criticize military industry, urban development, or courtly discipline while still relying on modern ways of seeing and an industrial division of labor.

Heidegger and Foucault clarify two levels of the problem. Heidegger asks how modern technology reveals a river, forest, or person as a reserve of usable capacity. Foucault’s **apparatus** is a concrete network of institutions, routines, expert knowledge, laws, buildings, and power relations that makes people knowable and governable. **Biopolitics** names practices directed at life on the scale of bodies and populations. A visual technology changes what can be seen; measurements, classifications, schedules, policies, and chains of command connect that vision to organized power.

Animation labor offers the nearest example. A director’s demands take on a repeatable technical form when the production pipeline divides a film into assigned shots and specialized tasks, sets deadlines, and sends the work through cycles of review and revision. The machinery can produce a handmade line while remaining invisible within it.[^animation-machine-19]

Toshio Suzuki recalls the animator and director Yoshifumi Kondō breaking down in tears under Takahata’s demands and protesting that Takahata was going to kill him.[^animation-machine-20] One harrowing episode cannot stand for the entire studio, but it does show what the image alone conceals. The loose, living line on-screen did not arise beyond hierarchy and pressure. Those forces could help produce it.

This is where Lamarre’s later account of production unsettles his screen-level contrast. The point is not that he ignored animation labor. It is that “nature-loving Ghibli” and “machinery-loving GAINAX” become harder to keep apart once the organization of production enters our reading of the finished image. Close reading tells us what a film makes visible and how. It cannot tell us, on its own, how the image was scheduled, divided, revised, or paid for. The machine behind Ghibli’s nature lies in that gap: between what the image invites us to feel and the organized work it allows to remain invisible.

## Editorial Postscript: No Nature Outside Technology

*This unsigned editorial supplement applies the essay’s question to two further works. The judgments in it belong to the editors, not to Author-kun.*

In *The Wind Rises*, Jirō’s dreams and design work make flight intensely sensuous. Aircraft glide through luminous skies, and their curves invite admiration before military use intrudes. The film registers the cost of that use, but for the editors, warning does not neutralize fascination. Its lingering pleasure in the Zero as a designed object remains repellent precisely because the design cannot be separated from the national war project that brings it into being. The judgment concerns the film’s arrangement of beauty and consequence, not the mere appearance of an airplane.

The manga ending of *Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind* presents a different problem. Custodians of the old humanity have engineered the people capable of surviving the polluted world as instruments of a future restoration. Nausicaä’s destruction of the custodians can look like liberation from technical control. Yet the confrontation is also a fight over who may classify populations, design their futures, and decide which forms of life continue. The people defending their freedom are themselves products of the system they resist. There is no original, untouched humanity waiting for them to return. There are only technologically altered lives contesting the purpose assigned to them.
