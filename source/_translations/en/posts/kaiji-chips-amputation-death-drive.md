## Prologue: No Legends for the Living Dead

Film and television love an underworld ruled by men in black suits, where gambling and illegal deals take place beyond public view. This mysterious heterotopia satisfies our fantasy that reality has a backstage. On screen it becomes a theater of excitement: banknotes thrown into the air, gunfights in the street, deals made in back alleys after midnight, and betrayals plotted by people who trust no one. These secret operations, unthinkable in ordinary life, are perfect material for daydreams.

Their protagonists are gambling gods with dazzling technique or action heroes who can take on a hundred men and walk through a hail of bullets. Like tightrope artists, they perform at the edge of death and let us savor every apparent brush with disaster. We also know they cannot truly fail. A street performer may slip, but the hero on screen will not. However likely defeat appears, the performance depends on preventing it. The deadly wager and the flying bullets only simulate death; they exist to magnify the hero’s exploits. That is what makes the formula a cliché. Give it enough chips and gunfire, however, and even a bad film will find an audience.

These stories may take the underworld as their subject, but they can do so only by neutralizing the thing that makes it run: death. Once death is stripped of its finality, chips no longer circulate unpredictably and stray bullets never strike the hero. Simulated danger replaces death itself. The protagonist is exempted from the law that governs everyone else and acquires a sacred glow. Whether he happens to be shot in a particular scene is beside the point; from the outset, he is imagined as someone who transcends death. No one is truly wagering life against life, because the hero’s death has already been packaged as part of the spectacle and cannot circulate like an ordinary stake. Adventure gives danger a purpose and a mission.

Kaiji’s story works differently. I use the Freudian term **death drive** here in a limited sense: not a conscious wish to die, but a compulsion to repeat actions that undo self-preservation. Kaiji fights desperately to live inside each game, yet survival repeatedly leads him back to another mortal wager. Nobuyuki Fukumoto’s Kaiji Itou is an idle, hopeless gambler. He steals and borrows to scrape together a little cash, then immediately wastes it on dice or pachinko. After unwittingly cosigning another deadbeat’s loan, he is left with the debt when the borrower disappears. A collector tells Kaiji that he can never repay it and steers him into a high-stakes contest among people dismissed as social refuse, where freedom and even life are at stake. Kaiji’s story begins with that gamble and never escapes its orbit. Whenever he barely survives one deadly game, he returns to being a listless bum, gets conned, and falls into another. Most of the series is therefore about how Kaiji gets out alive. He gives up his life at the start of every game; everything that follows is an effort to win back what he discarded so casually. Calling this pattern a “suicide game” is the essay’s interpretation of his repetition, not a claim that Kaiji simply wants to die.

Kaiji surrenders his death in exchange for the stake with which he hopes to buy it back. That surrender is already a kind of suicide. Through symbolic death he becomes a “gambler” and earns admission to the suicide game. Yet the gambler’s identity is an identity defined by dispossession: only someone with no foothold in respectable society sinks to this position. Such people are the living dead. Adventure and legend are no longer available to them; they can seek only resurrection or final death.

## I. “Gambling Economics” as a Model

> Only gambling crosses so easily over nationality, age, wealth, sex … over every barrier, letting people communicate … You might call it a common language …![^11]

### 1.1 The Chip Is Code

Consider the casino as a formal model, with its buildings, workers, regulation, and operating costs deliberately bracketed. Within that model, value appears through rules: the denomination assigned to chips and the calculation of odds. The chip is the exemplary object of this casino economics. It enters play with a fixed face value and circulates smoothly inside the game; wagers increase or reduce a player’s holdings, not the denomination of each chip. The model contains no production, only redistribution and renewed wagering—none of the “perilous leap” involved in selling a commodity. Capital alone changes hands, cycling through purposeless circulation and satisfying the demand for reinvestment. “Free trade” means exactly what it says here: trade alone is free. This is a deliberately narrow model of the exchange system staged by *Kaiji*, not a factual account of every real casino.

![Kaiji and his companions watch pachinko balls pour from the Bog; the visible English text says that each ball is worth 4,000 yen](attachments/roof-archive/cv14084596/translations/en/figure-3-r2.jpg)

The chip is money’s money, its terminal form. It completes the universal equivalent’s mission by escaping every purpose and social referent. It becomes “substance” in the strict sense: reproduction makes it its own cause and, because everything else depends on it, the cause of everything around it. Common sense says that chips merely simulate money. The casino shows the reverse: simulation replaces the real, or the real itself becomes simulation. “Reality” no longer has value because it cannot circulate or be transformed through exchange. Contemporary capitalism wants whatever is easiest to exchange. Hence the proliferation of simulacra.

> The monetary sign is severed from every social production: it then enters into speculation and limitless inflation … The disconnection of wages from the “rational” value of labor power, the disconnection of money from real production: in each case, reference is lost.
>
> Emptied of the finality and the affect of production, money becomes speculative. From the gold standard to floating capital and generalized floating, it passes from a referential sign to a structural form. This is the logic proper to the “floating” signifier … the signifier freed from every signified that might check its proliferation and its unlimited play. Money can thus reproduce itself by a simple game of transfers and bookkeeping entries, by an incessant splitting and repetition of its own abstract substance.[^1]

Baudrillard’s account of money in contemporary capitalism describes exactly what we see in the casino. “Dead labor” has overwhelmed living labor, pushing human effort toward the margins of production, so general labor can no longer function as the standard of value. Baudrillard goes further: it never truly served as that standard, but belonged to the fantasy of productivism. Capital has never submitted to productive labor. From the beginning, it has made a god of itself, absorbing workers, factories, technology, consumption, and everything else into an infinitely self-reflexive system. They become signs—floating signifiers governed only by capital’s grammar.

### 1.2 The Production of the Living Dead

Capital draws its lifeblood from the process that absorbs and homogenizes everything around it. “Surplus” does not first appear when part of labor’s price is withheld. It appears at the more basic moment when labor receives a price and labor power becomes tradable. Once concrete elements of production are converted into code and numbers, surplus has already been produced; from then on, whatever enters circulation is already subordinated. Baudrillard describes this original sale as surrendering one’s own death. Social subjects are hostages because their deaths have been given over to symbolic exchange; in return, they receive a quantified death as the standard of social life. In the published English translation, “death is general equivalence.”[^2] Value and death come to look interchangeable, giving rise to the fantasy that accumulating value can postpone death toward an infinitely distant endpoint. Capital’s infinity merges with the infinity of time and makes wealth accumulation appear progressive. But the promise is fraudulent. Exchangeable death is not real death. Real life is slowly depleted while capital steals from that depletion the energy that keeps it moving.

Ordinary work depletes life slowly, and life usually ends before accumulation does; that timing protects the fantasy. A gambler, by contrast, squanders the stake borrowed against his life long before his body dies. Symbolic death arrives first. He has been expelled from symbolic exchange, yet his flesh remains—an impossible condition from capital’s point of view. The first three *Kaiji* series are full of such people. In the shipboard Restricted Rock–Paper–Scissors game, each debtor starts with three star badges and must dispose of every card while retaining enough stars to qualify; losing them means confinement in a glass room. Elsewhere, men are buried in a lightless underground labor camp, and a casino boss is hauled away as soon as Kaiji bankrupts him. Teiai’s ubiquitous black suits act as sanitation workers, removing defeated players from sight as soon as they lose their symbolic identity. Fukumoto makes the symbolic death of bankruptcy resemble physical death through the terror of being taken away. Kaiji himself shows us what life in that paradox looks like. Outside a gamble, he lies all day in a filthy room or, when his fingers start itching, loses his last coins at pachinko or a minor gambling stall. He is one of the living dead: symbolically dead but physically alive.

Yet “the stake is gone; only flesh remains” is not quite symbolic death, because flesh can itself become a stake. Treating the body directly as gambling capital reveals that the line between symbolic and physical death was only provisional. Symbols have lethal force: power is, after all, the management of death, though that fact is usually concealed. The underworld is the place where the cruelty of symbolic exchange becomes explicit. Fukumoto depicts just such a world, where the body is one chip among others. His body-devouring gambling economy becomes a model of contemporary capitalism.

## II. Limbs with Price Tags

### 2.1 Cruel Chips

The chip circulates freely; its owner is the prisoner. His fate hangs from a string of numbers. The moment he enters one of Teiai’s predatory games, his life, too, becomes numerical and enters the calculation. Here we find a distorted form of surplus value. By advertising gambling as free and fair, *Kaiji*’s house attracts debtors carrying money, then extends credit against their freedom or bodies when the money is gone. The chip is only the visible object of exchange; in this fictional economy, what the house ultimately covets is the customer’s life. Real insurance and medicine also assign compensation or actuarial values to injury and death, but that is not the same as owning a life. *Kaiji* turns those ordinary calculations into a cruel and literal spectacle.

> Money today has become “cool”—“cool” designating a close but affectless correlation of terms, a play that draws its nourishment solely from the rules of the game, from the substitution of terms and the exhaustion of these substitutions … We are in the cool phase of the sign. The present system of labor is cool, money is cool, the structural configuration in general is cool.
>
> Cool is the pure play of the values of discourse and of the permutations of writing; it is the ease and distance of those who now toy only with figures, signs, and words; it is the unlimited power of operational simulation … When the medium becomes the message, we enter the cool era. This is exactly what has happened to money. Once it reaches a certain stage of disconnection, money is no longer a medium, no longer a means for the circulation of commodities; it becomes circulation itself—the form the system takes as it realizes itself in its spinning abstraction.[^3]

Money has been reduced to its barest meaning: a string of numbers. It can no longer be hard-earned money or dirty money. All that remains is the tautology that money is money. It obeys only the rules of exchange, and every operation performed on it becomes a move in a game. Baudrillard calls money “cool” because it replaces one term—or sign—with another in infinite, purposeless play governed only by rules. But “cool” does not yet describe money’s cruelty.

First, anyone who uses money is compelled to participate in its game of exchange. This is money’s universal coercive force. Second, codification expands money far beyond cash, producing a kind of total monetization—or total informatization. Money tries to draw everything into exchange and the endless circuit of circulation. Everything receives a valuation, and “value” no longer means only price; it means the capacity to be exchanged and circulated. Anything can be traded once it becomes a sign: user data passed between websites, for example, or the compulsive display and exchange of personal information on WeChat Moments. Circulation becomes the sole principle and absolute command. Whether money, information, signs, or something else circulates no longer matters. Circulation makes all of them alike. That process is cruel in itself:

> The very possibility of quantitative equivalence presupposes death. The equivalence of wages and labor power presupposes the death of the worker; the equivalence of commodities presupposes the symbolic extermination of objects. Death makes possible both the calculation of equivalence and regulation by indifference. This death is not violent or physical; it is the mutual neutralization of life and death in survival—death deferred.[^4]

Circulation begins with symbolic violence. Under its force, every element becomes the same kind of dead thing. Once that violence reaches an organic body, its destructive power becomes immediately visible. The following passages from *A Thousand Plateaus* help us describe the process.

### 2.2 Bodies for Sale

The plane on which the code runs is precisely the “plane of consistency” in the strict sense:

> The plane of consistency does away with all metaphor: all that exists is the real. They are electrons themselves, veritable black holes, real cellular units, genuine sequences of signs. Only, they have already left their own strata—destratified, decoded, deterritorialized—and it is exactly this that makes it possible for them to draw near and interpenetrate one another on the plane of consistency. A silent dance. The plane of consistency knows nothing of differences of level, orders of magnitude, distances. It knows nothing of the difference between the artificial and the natural. It knows nothing of the distinction between contents and expressions—all of these exist only through the strata and in relation to the strata.[^5]

In simple terms, Deleuze and Guattari’s “strata” are mechanisms that classify and divide things. The family defines father, mother, and son; political economy defines capital, proletariat, and labor; the social division of labor separates occupations. Strata give things form and organize them into systems. Deterritorialization cuts across those strata, loosens their partitions, and breaks their boundaries, allowing unlike things to move in undifferentiated form on the plane of consistency. Deleuze and Guattari call this motion a “continuum of intensities”: circulating particle-signs differ only in intensity or magnitude and therefore form an uninterrupted flow. From the plane of consistency they develop the idea of the **body without organs** (BwO):

> The BwO is what remains when you take everything away. What is removed is the phantasy: signifiance and subjectification as a whole.[^6]
>
> Is not Spinoza’s *Ethics*, after all, the great book of the BwO? Attributes are types or genera of BwO; substances, powers, and zero intensities are matrices of production. Modes are everything that happens: waves and vibrations, migrations, thresholds and gradients, intensities produced in a given kind of substance from a given matrix.[^7]
>
> The BwO is the field of immanence of desire, the plane of consistency specific to desire.[^8]

A body without organs—the drug user’s body, the masochist’s body—is not a body literally missing organs. It is a body released from the functional organization that turns organs into an **organism**. An organism is hierarchical, functionalized, and centrally administered; for Deleuze and Guattari, this stratum constrains the body. Once the usual organization falls away, the drug user can experience an intensity of cold and the masochist an intensity of pain. The body becomes a plane across which desire flows. The BwO is therefore the aim of a practice, but a dangerous one: destratification must loosen the body without destroying it.

From here I am making an analogy, not restating Deleuze and Guattari’s doctrine. Their BwO is specific to desire; replacing desire with money changes the concept. I borrow only the formal image of established boundaries being loosened so that intensities can pass across a plane. The question is whether monetary circulation can be read as exerting a comparable pressure on the organized body.

This analogy names money’s cruelty: monetary valuation can disregard the body’s organic integrity and divide flesh into exchangeable units. Smugglers conceal drugs in the rectum or stomach; traffickers remove organs; insurers assign compensation values to lives and limbs. None of these damaged or commodified bodies is literally a BwO. The comparison instead draws attention to capital’s pressure on the partitions that ordinarily keep person, body part, commodity, and collateral distinct. Unlike the dangerous practice Deleuze and Guattari describe, that pressure comes from outside and wounds the body it reorganizes. What remains is not liberated desire but a deformed assemblage of human flesh and merchandise offered for sale.

The body for sale recurs throughout Kaiji. In the first shipboard gamble, losers are stripped naked and locked in a glass room. The middle-aged man who mocked Kaiji is redeemed by his companions not out of solidarity but because a medicated patch on his back hides valuable jewels. He has become a body for sale. Stripping and segregating an eliminated gambler produces naked flesh as the game’s remainder, its waste. The only way to keep that waste from being discarded is to attach a compensating commodity—the “patch”—and prevent its symbolic value from reaching zero. Jewels worn as decoration and jewels smuggled inside the body are not so different. Both conceal the body’s status as refuse.

These examples quantify death through the price of valuables embedded in a body, the sums assigned to limbs, and the payout of a life-insurance policy. Quantification itself threatens life: when code deterritorializes the body, it passes a death sentence. Its subject is merely granted an interval before the sentence is carried out. For a gambler, that interval is the time spent at the table. Seen this way, all Kaiji’s efforts win him only a stay of execution.

## III. Kaiji Itou’s Survival Game

### 3.1 A Minimum of Fairness

The moment someone enters a gamble, their life is already on loan. The threat of death does not appear immediately, because the game’s surface conceals it. Symbolic exchange operates within a system of rules and assigned roles. Its relation to death is visible from the metalevel analyzed above, but a player sees only what the system reveals from within: how the exchange works, what effects it produces, and whom the rules favor. Players have no time to examine the game itself, especially when their immediate interests are at stake. The canny gambler therefore chooses rules that are easy to manipulate and roles that offer the greatest advantage, then waits for a sucker.

Power over the rules is the asset on which Fukumoto’s minor villains pride themselves. Some are petty bosses who force subordinates to gamble, cheat, and skim the proceeds. Others run gambling dens and bait victims with jackpots that appear to require nothing but luck. The first type is represented by the foreman in the underground labor camp in *Tobaku Hakairoku Kaiji*, the manga’s second series. The second includes the casino boss behind the Bog—a casino-scale, elaborately rigged pachinko machine—in *Hakairoku*, and the boss who stages “17 Steps,” a two-player, seventeen-tile variant also called minefield mahjong, in the third series, *Tobaku Datenroku Kaiji*. These greedy local tyrants control the visible rules, the concealed method of cheating, and the violence that protects both. Their business seems almost risk-free. Undetected cheating carries no cost; even discovery means only one fewer victim. They assume the unequal exchange can continue forever: as long as they hold the advantage, they cannot lose.

The unequal rules place Kaiji at an extreme disadvantage. Even when he discovers the fraud, denouncing it cannot recover his losses; at the gaming table, blame falls not on the cheat but on the fool who failed to detect the trick. Yet once play begins, even rigged rules cannot be changed. However unfair they are, they must remain in force until the final settlement. This immutability allows a game to proceed and symbolic violence to operate. If the person in power simply altered the rules whenever necessary, symbolic violence would become direct violence. The breach would create a scandal, discrediting the authority that either has to step down or conceal what happened. Fixed rules therefore provide gambling’s bare minimum of “fairness,” because they are also the minimum condition for symbolic exchange. Kaiji can exploit only this constraint:

> Legitimate places are useless! It has to be underground … a place with a guaranteed way to win, where the other side has some secret that makes victory certain …! In other words, what I want most is a place where they can cheat! Precisely because they cheat, if I turn things around and break through the single trick behind that guaranteed victory, victory is guaranteed for me![^12]

Kaiji recognizes a metatheorem of gambling: whatever the specific rules, they must contain some reversibility. If a rule guarantees victory for the house, turning it back against the house can guarantee victory for him. At this level—and only this level—Kaiji, who owns nothing, becomes his opponents’ equal. They regard him as worthless scum who can be toyed with at will. When that minimal equality suddenly appears, they are terrified: the rules on which they relied seem to change sides and bind them instead. In truth, rules obey no one. Gamblers who depend on manipulating them are small fry. In the Kaiji series, Chairman Hyōdō may be the only exception.

[fig] Ōtsuki’s internal monologue explicitly distinguishes the normal dice from his secret dice (*Tobaku Hakairoku Kaiji*, chapter 37, “Reassessment”; adapted in *Kaiji: Against All Rules*, episode 7, “Magic Dice”). Later in the arc he uses dice bearing only fours, fives, and sixes, and Kaiji answers with all-ones dice.

![Foreman Ōtsuki wonders whether he accidentally used the normal dice and realizes that he forgot to pretend to reach for his secret dice](attachments/roof-archive/cv14084596/translations/en/figure-5-r2.jpg)

The underground dice game offers the clearest example of this minimum fairness. Kaiji learns that in a decisive round the foreman will use dice bearing only fours, fives, and sixes. He waits until the foreman cheats, then seizes the dice. The foreman had grown suspicious, but with 500,000 perica—the labor camp’s scrip, exchanged at ten perica to one yen—at stake, he could not resist using them. Kaiji does not accuse him of breaking a rule; he accuses him of concealing one. To keep the game going, Kaiji declares that special dice were a hidden part of the rules and can remain legal now that the rule has been disclosed:

> Of course … just as I said, we’ll continue with the special dice. Don’t worry about being exposed—go ahead and use them! Because we’ll be using something … similar …![^13]

Kaiji then produces loaded dice of his own, made from the foreman’s discarded steak bones and Kaiji’s blood. Every face shows one, and three ones earn the highest payout. The foreman is forced to accept that “companions may use special dice.” He had also changed the usual rule that a winning dealer keeps the bank, hoping to disguise his own cheating: instead, the dealer must remain in place for two rounds, win or lose. Together, those two rules turn a wager of 500,000 perica into losses of more than ten million, emptying the foreman’s accumulated treasury.

From this angle, gambling might look like a game anyone can master by learning the trick, with victory going to the cleverer player. But Kaiji never enters merely for amusement or profit. He knows he is staking his whole life and more. His “entry fee” against the foreman is three months’ wages saved by Kaiji and five companions. Defeat would condemn them to at least fifteen more years underground, where the conditions make surviving another fifteen years nearly impossible. Kaiji gambles by repeatedly committing symbolic suicide. His only remaining capital is his body, and once he has lost his symbolic life—his ability to repay and his personal freedom—physical death is only a matter of time. He must stake his life and more, because otherwise this living corpse cannot be resurrected. Fukumoto later has Kazuya Hyōdō call this the “qualities of a gambler.” It distinguishes Kaiji from petty cheats. He commits his whole body and soul; each gamble is not just a game but a suicide game that directly concerns death.

### 3.2 The Only Possible Way to Challenge

To redeem his life, Kaiji must let the stakes expand without limit. Gambling for money becomes gambling for life—or rather, reveals that it was gambling for life all along. This is the Kaiji series’ central theme. Kaiji owns nothing and has no credit, so his only collateral is his body: his freedom and organs. The moment he enters a game, death hangs over him.

Within *Kaiji*’s fictional economy, gambling within one’s means is almost impossible: Teiai and its associates keep extending the stakes beyond finite assets. This is not a description of ordinary gambling but the model the series makes visible. The body is not one asset among others but the entirety of what Kaiji owns. At Teiai’s tables the boundary between flesh and banknotes collapses, so death becomes the ultimate chip and treats rich and poor alike. The series makes capital’s bottomless demand spectacular by having the wager exceed what its participants can pay and finally claim their freedom, limbs, or lives.

Putting death at stake changes the nature of the game, just as Russian roulette differs fundamentally from a toy shark that snaps harmlessly at a finger. Death’s price is always excessive because the symbolic order is founded on excluding death. Death ends meaning and breaks exchange and circulation. Society must therefore domesticate it. “Natural death,” the modern ideal, is clean, authorized, and “good”: it marks the proper exhaustion of a person’s biological capital, while accidental death is treated as a failure to fulfill social obligations.[^9] In place of real death, society circulates symbolic or simulated deaths that can be quantified and calculated. This harmless substitute locks unexchangeable death inside the body and keeps it from view. Gambling calls it back into exchange. As “Bodies for Sale” argued, the gambler’s body is especially open to deterritorializing flows; at the table, concealed death returns in its irrational and intolerable form.

At the gaming table, the body becomes collateral with a quoted price: meet that price and its ownership can change hands. In his characteristically perverse humor, Chairman Hyōdō therefore proposes that Kaiji stake his hearing in a showdown with Tonegawa. The game is E-Card: Emperor beats Citizen, Citizen beats Slave, but the Slave alone can defeat the Emperor. Kaiji plays the disadvantaged Slave side for a fivefold payout, while every loss drives a needle farther toward his ear:

> Simply risk losing something of your own, and you have a chance at one hundred million yen … For you, Kaiji, who just wagered your life, it is only a small risk … an eye, perhaps, or an ear …![^14]
>
> As far as we are concerned, that ear is collateral. Half the rights to it already belong to us—we cannot let you do whatever you please with it! For now it is merely on loan. Once the result is known, your hearing will be … repossessed by me!

The ear held as collateral becomes Kaiji’s way out. He realizes that the device is not only a penalty mechanism: it also measures his pulse and other physical reactions, feeding Tonegawa information about his hand. On the pretext of going to the restroom, Kaiji smashes a mirror, cuts off the ear with a shard of glass, and hides the wound beneath a towel. A companion then remains in the restroom holding the detached device so that it continues to transmit a steady pulse and mislead Tonegawa’s monitors. Once again, Kaiji wins by turning the rules back on their author. He has wagered more than his hearing: at its maximum setting, the device’s needle would pass through his eardrum and damage the inner ear, very likely killing him. E-Card’s symbolism could hardly be clearer. The Slave challenges the Emperor by staking the one thing he possesses—his own death:

> You remember what you said, don’t you? You said the Slave is the one who has nothing … the one who is trampled underfoot …. But that man with nothing … a Slave who stakes everything can defeat the Emperor …! Take this! It is the final cry my companions and I send from the edge of death … our last stand …![^15]

In the latter half of *Gambling Apocalypse: Kaiji*, the series’ first part, Kaiji and other debtors are forced through lethal spectacles staged for the pleasure of the rich. By the time E-Card begins, he has endured humiliation and fraud and watched companions succumb to fear, despair, and death. His comeback victory forms the story’s emotional climax. Kaiji wins enough to clear his debt, while Tonegawa must perform a dogeza—a kneeling prostration of apology—on a red-hot plate. The story could end there. Instead Kaiji extends his “righteous” revenge and challenges Chairman Hyōdō himself. He has discovered that a Slave with nothing can challenge an Emperor only by putting death on the table.

Some readers may regard Kaiji’s challenge to Hyōdō as a sequel hook, but it is also necessary to the series’ logic. Hyōdō embodies its highest authority, and Kaiji’s rebellion demands a reckoning with that authority. Gambling’s minimum equality lets Kaiji turn rules against those who made them, but only when an authority outside the game enforces the result. In the match with foreman Ōtsuki, Teiai executive Yoshihiro Kurosaki presides; without him, the foreman would surely wriggle out of paying. Higher authority thus guarantees both the game and the oppressive order that humiliates people like Kaiji. If Kaiji wants to negate that order, he must challenge its ultimate representative, Chairman Kazutaka Hyōdō. Yet the confrontation also overturns the image established at the beginning of *Gambling Apocalypse: Kaiji*: Kaiji as the indomitable, righteous gambler-hero.

### 3.3 The End of the Hero’s Image: The “Suicide Game”

By manga convention, Hyōdō should be the final boss. His defeat should redeem Kaiji’s ordeal and turn it into heroic legend. Yet, as the prologue argued, Kaiji’s story is neither adventure nor legend. It is the story of a gambler struggling to survive at the edge of death, and the match with Hyōdō is what makes that fact unmistakable.

Kaiji gives his ordeal a purpose: win back his life, then defeat Hyōdō. His “survival” means recovering a place in the symbolic order by clearing his debts and returning to society; his challenge is meant to humiliate that order’s authority on its own terms. The logic continues a theme introduced in Tonegawa’s speech aboard the ship:

> At best, all you think is, “Wouldn’t it be nice if I won?” That is why you are the trash standing here. You must not think it would be nice to win … you must think that you have to win! No one cares about you now because you have been losing all your lives! Poor and indecisive, at the bottom … crawling, crawling, crawling … crawling on and on …. And why? Because you have kept losing, from then until this very day! There is only one answer: win …. If you do not win, you are trash … You must win, you must win … you must win![^16]

Tonegawa is the model striver. Exams, social maneuvering, and relentless competition have carried him into Teiai’s upper ranks, and his creed is simple: only victory can improve one’s symbolic standing. Kaiji begins at the bottom but follows much the same logic. What he rejects is its inhuman face—the cruelty that casts losers into hell. He therefore challenges the symbolic order by staking death, the one object too great for that order to absorb. For precisely that reason, the death gamble also exposes symbolic exchange as a machinery for producing pleasure.

Hyōdō is ultimately less the personification of order than the gambler in his purest form: a man who delights in gambling itself. He is no majestic Big Brother but an inverted Name-of-the-Father, an obscene father sunk in pleasure. Reading Kaiji’s gamble as a challenge staked on death lends it a revolutionary charge; Hyōdō brings desire back into the picture. He recasts Kaiji’s righteous cause as the pleasure of playing:

> Surely you have tasted it by now, Kaiji …. Crossing the steel bridge alive, and then your victory at E-Card … the elation, relief, and joy of that instant—how intense they must have been! Struggling at the brink of death is bitter, yes, but deeply fulfilling …. Escaping death by a hair is the sweetest thing in the world—the supreme blessing …! That pleasure must already be branded into your brain! In all likelihood, Kaiji, the rest of your life … will be one endless journey in pursuit of it …![^17]

Kaiji’s self-appointed mission indefinitely postpones any final end and lets him reenact “survival” again and again. Hyōdō punctures that self-deception. Kaiji must confront the desire beneath his mission—the thrill of walking beside death. His frantic drive to live springs from an equally frantic drive back toward mortal risk. That drive repeatedly leads him, by choice, into lethal wagers; this compulsion to repeat is the sense in which gambling becomes his “suicide game.” His best play emerges only when death bears down on him. When nothing but money and signs are at stake, he is as mediocre as anyone else. Both the match with Hyōdō and the “17 Steps” minefield-mahjong game in *Tobaku Datenroku Kaiji* bear this out.

After cracking the “Bog” pachinko machine and escaping the underground camp, Kaiji is approached by his former companions Miyoshi and Maeda. They claim that their gambling-den boss uses employees to peek at customers’ cards, and that they can reverse the trick by spying for Kaiji. In fact, they have already joined the boss in a routine con: tell a customer that the boss cheats, then lure that customer with the promise of cheating him in return. Kaiji accepts because the wager appears both profitable and safe, and walks straight into their trap.

His match with Hyōdō follows the same pattern. Kaiji tries to construct a game he cannot lose. In advance, he hides marked winning slips inside the wall of a tissue box in the restroom. Then he pretends to improvise a fair game and guides Hyōdō toward drawing lots from that box. Hyōdō sees through the entire scheme. Kaiji loses—and loses four fingers with it.

Kaiji’s counterattacks have such force precisely because he is despised as one of the “living dead,” with no secure place in society. Each escape from death can flood the spectator with pleasure. Justice and revenge matter, but this essay also reads that pleasure as the rebirth of someone who had symbolically discarded his life. The death invited by staking his life becomes the condition of his return. On the level of symbols, this is pure repetition: no one actually dies, and the value before and after the exchange remains unchanged. On the level of desire, however, the encounter with death has been performed in full. Staged self-destruction acquires aesthetic value because it exceeds the cold exchange of signs and delivers an experience that resists symbolization.[^10]

## Conclusion: The Suicide Game as a Genre of Performance

In one sense, *Gambling Apocalypse: Kaiji* is another “death performance” of the kind described in the prologue. Formula films stage adventure and legend around a hero. The first three *Kaiji* series belong to another genre. Their protagonist is one of the “living dead,” stripped of social standing, who survives only by repeatedly entering death traps. More precisely, the series stages the “suicide game”: a cycle that reproduces pleasure through mortal risk and survival.

We have watched money become cruel. Yet in *Kaiji*, images of dismemberment and death—and the struggle to escape them—also generate intense dramatic pleasure. The series turns voluntary exposure to mortal danger into a fantasy of subversion: Kaiji seems to take back from authority the power to dispose of his own life. But that fantasy never fully becomes politics. In the spectacle of self-sacrifice, viewers may be moved not only by the justice of the act but also by its sacrificial form. Once a fictional subject gives himself wholly to an action that hastens death, every movement acquires a fascination that is difficult to put into words. This is a claim about *Kaiji*’s aesthetic treatment of lethal risk, not a general claim that real suicide is redemptive or that Kaiji wants to die.

Completed suicide would end the cycle. Kaiji, however, works endlessly to keep death from arriving while retaining the mortal wager’s allure. The “suicide game” can therefore continue as game and performance, reproducing pleasure without end. Death has been coded into a repeatable sign. Formulaic heroic films build performance by excluding death; *Kaiji* builds it by bringing death to the brink and withdrawing it for another round. The relation between those forms may resemble the relation between political economy and the economy of symbolic exchange.

At the time of writing, *Kaiji* had been serialized for twenty-five years and had reached *Tobaku Datenroku Kaiji: 24-Oku Dasshutsu-hen*. It had also produced spin-offs such as *Mr. Tonegawa: Middle Management Blues*. Fukumoto’s prolific output is one reason for this longevity, but the story itself also has the reproductive structure described above. Kaiji’s suicide game, like the everyday comic episodes of *Mr. Tonegawa*, can keep generating new performances for an audience.
