## My First Encounter with Character Theory and the “Workplace Story” Anime

**—The second title you chose is *Patlabor: The Movie*, but this basically means “all of Mamoru Oshii’s work,” right?**

**Furukawa:** People online write that I’m a direct disciple of Kunihiko Ikuhara, and I acknowledge that myself. I’ve also said in previous interviews that I like Hideaki Anno. But before either of those, I had already been thoroughly baptized in Oshii’s work, and I wanted to take this opportunity to say so. The next anime I got hooked on after *Saint Seiya* was *Patlabor*. I followed the TV series, the OVAs, and the films, and among them there were works that were obviously much denser than the others. Thinking about why, there was this moment when I realized, “Oh—this guy Mamoru Oshii is special.”

**—What first made you take notice of his name?**

**Furukawa:** At first it was the screenplay credit. Then *GHOST IN THE SHELL* came out and the name “Mamoru Oshii” suddenly got pushed right into the public spotlight, didn’t it? When tons of interviews with him and pieces he had written himself started appearing, I devoured them with this strange rush of pride, like, “Our Mamoru Oshii is a worldwide hit now …” Even today, I don’t just keep print interviews—I save copies of pages I find online too. That’s the level I’m at.

**—That’s impressive.**

**Furukawa:** I’d been exposed to Oshii’s ideas about anime direction that whole time, but it wasn’t until I was in a position to weigh in on direction myself—around when I became assistant director on *Yurikuma Arashi*—that I could finally understand them for myself with much greater clarity. Before that, I treated them as special words spoken by a special person. Take the famous idea of “controlling the amount of information” in anime. Until then, it was basically just a piece of otaku trivia I happened to know.

**—Anno talks about that too.**

**Furukawa:** Actually, Ikuhara used to say almost exactly the same thing on the production floor. In other words, all top-tier anime directors do it. My interpretation is that it comes down to which material you choose for the expression you want to achieve. When you want to express some phenomenon in images, do you do it through dialogue? Music? 3DCG? Background art? Animation?

Both Oshii and Ikuhara carry that process of choosing the optimal material through their own work from beginning to end. And their directing styles are completely different. That’s why I think it’s a problem when so many people immediately steer discussion of Kunihiko Ikuhara toward things like “themes.” Or “aestheticism.” “Aestheticism,” in the end, is just the impression people have of *Revolutionary Girl Utena*, isn’t it?

**—That hits close to home.**

**Furukawa:** When Ikuhara was drawing storyboards, he was always thinking about the material. He would constantly say things like, “The script describes this as a character action, but I think it would be better to express the same thing through the background art.” Every one of those remarks was a culture shock to me. I came to see Kunihiko Ikuhara as a director who, on production floors where there is never much breathing room, is always selecting among materials and continuing to make the best film possible under the circumstances. He has even changed themes he had in mind to fit the materials that were actually available. Yet even a theme changed that way reaches the audience with a distinctive propulsive force—that is Ikuhara’s strength as an auteur. And that is what great predecessors in the anime industry, Oshii among them, have always done. You could say that the way a director chooses among materials determines their auteurial identity. It sounds obvious when you stop and think about it, but actually carrying it through is incredibly difficult. …Anyway, going back to the first *Patlabor* theatrical film: the opening begins with a cel-slide shot loaded with dense photographic effects, and it is simply perfect as the way into the film. *Saint Seiya: Legend of Crimson Youth* does this too—I have absolutely no resistance to movies like that. (laughs) And I feel like this film was the first time I was shown “character theory” in visual form.

**—What do you mean by that?**

**Furukawa:** That “you don’t necessarily need to draw a character.” You can have somebody else talk about that character. In other words, I learned that the style of *Waiting for Godot* can be used to portray a character. Then the film is decorated with the sort of biblical elements middle schoolers love, and at the end you get this exhilarating destruction sequence and robot-on-robot action. That’s great too. And I can’t get enough of the highly detailed mechanical animation.

**—It really is well balanced as entertainment.**

**Furukawa:** Looking at it from another angle, *Patlabor* was also the first “workplace story” I encountered. It was the first time I discovered there were works about how hard it is to be an adult. Basically, people are shaped by the place they occupy and the role they are given, but every now and then you also get someone like Captain Gotoh. Adults are constantly wavering between their own feeling that they want to be “on the side of justice” and the norms of society. I think *Patlabor* is a series that fully depicts that emotional range within the special setting of a police force, while also combining it with robots. And it isn’t just this title. Oshii actually spends a lot of his work depicting people in unusual professions. Part of that may simply be that he “has a thing for occupations,” but ever since *Urusei Yatsura*, he has constructed drama through the roles characters occupy. Because *Urusei Yatsura* centers on an alien character, Lum, its world can tolerate almost any kind of outrageousness. In other words, Lum functions as a dramatic device. After that, Oshii kept putting characters into specialized roles and situations—not only “police officers,” but also “detectives” and “pilots.” Then, within that situation, he pairs someone whose basic mode is “I have to act according to my role” with someone whose thinking falls outside that pattern, and the buddy pairing drives the story. That kind of setup lets you build the characters’ emotional range out of a pair: one part within what we can imagine, and the other beyond it. Precisely because there is a part everyone can recognize, the part that falls outside it stands out all the more. Oshii is always consciously building devices like that, and that is extremely important for a director making an original work. So, based on everything I’ve just said, there’s something I always bring up in planning meetings for original projects.

**—What is it?**

**Furukawa:** “When you develop an original anime project, the first thing you should think about is how to design the way the audience will enjoy it.” That’s what I understand Oshii and Ikuhara to have been doing all along. The director has to start designing ways for the audience to enjoy the work at the producing level. To use my own work as an example: “You say you’re doing theater, but how are you going to connect those elements to animation?” Or, “How can people enjoy Twitter’s real-time commentary culture and its sense of simultaneity?” A director has to think like a producer about those things. Why? Because it isn’t the screenwriter or the producer who turns it into images; it’s the director who actually draws the storyboards. How do you build in, from the script and planning stages, elements that can be expanded through the storyboard? I think it only becomes “making original animation” when the director thinks and acts that far upstream. You can draw a mind-blowing image board nobody has ever seen, come up with a brilliant twist, and then pour effort into the animation, and yes, you can “deliver” an amazing original anime. But I learned that whether people can actually enjoy watching it is a completely different question.
