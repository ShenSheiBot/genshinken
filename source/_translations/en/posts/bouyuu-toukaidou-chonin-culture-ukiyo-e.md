At the time of writing, ZUN had made nine albums centered on the Secret Sealing Club. Apart from the charity album *Unknown Flower, Mesmerizing Journey*, each sends its two young women on a new adventure. In 2006, two years after the second album, *Changeability of Strange Dream*, [*Bōyū Tōkaidō ~ Retrospective 53 Minutes*](https://shop.hakurei-sukeikai.com/detail/id/00000120001) was unveiled at the Third Hakurei Shrine Reitaisai. Where its predecessor had supplied personal details about Merry and Renko, this album’s booklet chiefly enlarges the world they inhabit. The story remains understated, but it lets us glimpse their not-so-distant future. Its central concern continues the previous booklet’s inquiry into reality and illusion. Unlike the stories before and after it, however, Merry and Renko never use their abilities, while narratorial commentary takes up far more of the booklet. The inquiry also broadens from personal worldviews to culture. The ideas it raises may have shaped ZUN’s later creative outlook.

## I. An outline of the story and some thoughts on its setting

The booklet follows Merry and Renko on a fifty-three-minute underground train journey from Kyoto to Tokyo. Its story is really a string of conversations only loosely connected to one another. In his afterword, ZUN writes that it should feel as though someone sitting nearby has happened to overhear the pair talking, giving it the romance of travel. That atmosphere proves exactly right for the questions the text explores.

Tokyo is described as a city that is “spiritually immature.” No one minds the gaps in its barriers, and it retains many “amusements for ordinary people,” including indoor theme parks and food courts. A shrinking population and the abandonment of cars have left its roads overgrown with red spider lilies during Higan, the Buddhist observances around the equinoxes. Even Renko calls Tokyo “the countryside.” Kyoto, by contrast, is never described directly, yet it projects the authority of a metropolis, one whose sole remaining diversion is the “New Tea Ceremony.” The story in *Changeability of Strange Dream* says that Kyoto has studied spiritual science for a thousand years. Merry and Renko’s club is only one commonplace psychic society among many, and even Merry’s university subject is “relative psychology.” Kyoto is therefore a city with a profound body of research into the spiritual and the fantastic, perhaps the mark of “maturity” in Renko’s eyes. Yet the two repeatedly call their club a “delinquent club,” because excursions to the barriers between different worlds are forbidden. Although they suffer no punishment, the prohibition stands in sharp contrast to Tokyo’s neglect.

Introducing Tokyo to Merry, Renko mentions Shinjuku and Shibuya, calling them “places with a sense of history.” Today they are two of Japan’s busiest districts, at the leading edge of modernization even by global standards. In a country rich in historic sites and comparatively successful in preserving tradition, neither seems readily associated with historical atmosphere. The phrase suggests that the Kyoto of the story is no longer the ancient capital we know, but an entirely new metropolis built upon the most advanced civilization. In such a setting, not only monuments with centuries of continuity but even buildings we now regard as modern may have disappeared. This Kyoto still claims to be a thousand-year capital, but it has erased every material memory that could bear that history. Add the fact that the “New Tea Ceremony” is its only remaining amusement, and the city’s oppressiveness is hard to miss.

A comparison reveals many affinities between this future Kyoto and the Lunar Capital. Both possess an extremely advanced civilization and are spiritually “mature.” Yet one great difference remains. The Lunar Capital is very old, and even its most advanced technology always appears in traditional forms. Reimu, whose own life is steeped in traditional things, finds it strikingly “primitive.” Kaguya, once a Lunarian, believes that humanity will return to such a traditional way of life after technology reaches its peak, but Reimu disagrees. ZUN’s idea of the future is already visible in this album: people forever pursue the latest advances while unconsciously treating traditional culture as a fetter and casting it away. Traditional culture represents inheritance and continuity. To the Lunarians, who seek eternity, those qualities are so important that they may be called the core of Lunar culture. For Reimu, however, such “eternity” is almost unbearable for human beings. No matter how life is prolonged, death remains a part of it: “so that no one would go on living once the heart had rotted.”[^13] Yet discarding everything is no answer. ZUN rejects both the Lunar Capital, which tries to make tradition eternal by purging every impurity, and Kyoto, which has erased tradition until only empty form remains. Touhou repeatedly returns to the Buddhist truth that all living things must die.[^16] Rather than treating preservation or abandonment as an irreversible choice, people must decide what in a disappearing past remains worth carrying forward.

The descriptions of Mount Fuji and the Kaleido-screen make this clearer. In ZUN’s Music Room comment for *Imperishable Night*,[^1] he writes: “The real reason Mount Fuji was not selected as a World Heritage site is that it is so sacred that even the world could not choose it.” The album appeared seven years before UNESCO inscribed Fuji as a cultural site in 2013. Mount Fuji’s position as a symbol of Japanese culture and national spirit needs no rehearsing. Even in the near future of *Retrospective 53 Minutes*, the Japanese government regards boring a tunnel beneath it as an act of “great irreverence,” so the mountain remains an object of veneration. Renko, however, admits that she has barely noticed the real mountain and shows no special reaction to passing beneath it by train. Together with the observation that few people climb it anymore, this reveals how far Mount Fuji has receded from ordinary life. Within the story, its recognition as a World Heritage site therefore becomes an important symbol: the mountain, and the culture it represents, have died in practical terms. The essay’s analogy now turns from sacred landscape to monument. The Colosseum belongs to Rome’s World Heritage listing. Its cries and bloodshed vanished more than a thousand years ago, leaving a monument and limitless fantasies of past glory, while Roman law, which helped lay the foundations of human civilization and still exerts a deep influence, could never receive that kind of material commemoration. Mount Fuji’s change from a “sacred peak beyond human judgment” into an “ordinary” World Heritage site likewise marks, in the essay’s reading, the loss of its spirituality, or its cultural meaning. The scenery on the Kaleido-screen, repeatedly praised as “quintessentially Japanese,” is merely false scenery. To Merry it is even “boring visual stimulation.” The so-called Japanese spirit has vanished from life and survived only as fantasy.

Mount Fuji’s “death” is not in itself frightening. The danger is that Kyoto—the new spiritual emblem adopted by people whose lives no longer include the mountain—offers no new way of seeing or living. In Renko and Merry’s world, natural food is hard to find, and even liquor made with real alcohol has become a luxury. Technology dictates the categories through which people perceive the world. In the essay’s terms, they have traded their memory and everyday experience of Fuji for a “synthetic civilization.” Its systems can reproduce Japanese imagery, but cannot teach anyone to recognize the world before them. Humans survive by making new cultures, and no tradition can remain intact forever. Yet a new culture has no life if it preserves old signs while losing the practices and perceptions that once gave them meaning.

## II. Chōnin culture

ZUN offers no explicit answer to a question as vast as what should remain and what should pass away in traditional culture. The story nevertheless provides a number of clues.

In Renko’s account to Merry, Tokyo teems with young people in strange clothes. The city may count as “the countryside” beside elegant, severe Kyoto, but it seems as rough and alluring as the American West, the setting of countless adventures. Gensokyo still retains the appearance of Japan before the Meiji Restoration. It is hard not to think of the vigorous, sometimes bizarre, yet hopeful urban culture made by Edo chōnin.

[fig] KS, [*Touhou Hyakki Yagyō*](https://www.pixiv.net/artworks/15322975), Pixiv artwork 15322975.

![A colorful crowd of Touhou characters filling a lantern-lit night market in KS’s *Touhou Hyakki Yagyō*](attachments/roof-archive/cv6229226/02-touhou-hyakki-yagyo.png)

In Japan, *chōnin* is a specific historical designation for urban commoners, especially merchants and artisans; wealthy merchants were its most conspicuous members under the Edo shogunate. The word itself appeared as early as the Heian period. At that time, *chō* could refer to a fixed urban space associated with shops or markets rather than the later administrative unit.[^2] *Chōnin* then meant simply “people of the chō,” but the word’s association with commercial districts gradually linked it to merchants.

[fig] “Shop: commonly called chō.”

![A manuscript page from *Ruiju Myōgishō*, with the entry for “chō” outlined in red](attachments/roof-archive/cv6229226/03-ruiju-myougishou.png)

After the Heian period, the decline of state-sponsored trade opened more space for private commerce. Merchants settled around temples and harbors, forming districts that included the temple towns known as *jinaimachi*. It was during this period that chōnin began to enter history as a distinct group.[^3] Large quantities of metal currency from the Asian continent stimulated the commodity economy and encouraged towns to form around markets, ports, and other busy sites of exchange.[^4] To strengthen the military and economic power of their territories, daimyō of the Warring States period developed castle towns, or *jōkamachi*, beneath their fortifications as political and commercial bases. Artisans and merchants increasingly gathered there. Handicraft production, distribution, and trade followed them, gradually turning the castle towns into military, economic, and transport centers and giving rise to the *jōkamachi chōnin*.[^5]

Nurtured by these new conditions, chōnin finally took shape as a distinct group in the Edo period. The essay also considers Christianity and its suppression as part of this changing institutional background. Missionaries began spreading Christianity in Japan late in the Muromachi period. The Tokugawa shogunate later suppressed the faith and required households to register with Buddhist temples. More broadly, it promoted Neo-Confucian learning among the warrior class, restricted foreign contact, and censored publications in its effort to secure social and political control. Those policies did not form a single program, nor did they have a single outcome. Commoner education spread through *terakoya* schools, while limited foreign trade continued to admit Western books and nourish Dutch learning. Together, literacy and this narrow channel of outside knowledge helped create conditions in which chōnin could develop a culture of their own.

This history calls to mind Touhou’s “outsiders.” From the Moriya family in *Mountain of Faith* to Sumireko in *Urban Legend in Limbo*, and even the Secret Sealing Club itself, people from the Outside World bring new customs and perspectives into Gensokyo’s traditional world. With the addition of characters from other worlds, including the Lunar Capital and Hell, ZUN even treated the “immigration problem” with mock seriousness in *Alternative Facts in Eastern Utopia*. Yet the arrival of these characters and the cultures they represent did not deprive Touhou of its “fantastically retro” aesthetic core. Instead, they renewed it in more approachable forms. As Zhu Xi’s verse asks, “How can the channel run so clear? Fresh water comes from the source.” Perhaps the phrase “Gensokyo accepts everything”[^14] is ZUN’s first answer to the question of how a tradition may be reborn.

As noted above, Edo-period Japan was strictly stratified. Each estate had its assigned function, and without an examination system comparable to China’s imperial civil service examinations, social mobility was limited. The warrior estate lived largely on stipends—often paid in rice—from its lords. Yet Japanese society was already heavily commercialized, so warriors had to sell stipend rice through merchants and convert it into money to spend. The Tokugawa shogunate also imposed the alternate-attendance system, *sankin kōtai*, upon the daimyō. It required them to maintain residences in Edo and divide their time, on schedules set by the shogunate, between the capital and their own domains; their wives and heirs generally remained in Edo. This secured the shogunate’s control while burdening the daimyō with travel and the maintenance of multiple residences. Merchants supplied goods and services to those processions and households, further stimulating commerce.

Some merchants amassed enormous fortunes, but status rules and restrictions on landholding made that wealth difficult to convert into formal political power or landed rank. Consumption became one of its principal outlets. Craftspeople, artists, and performers in the cities made a livelihood from that demand. Kabuki, *jōruri*, haiku, ukiyo-e, and many other forms flourished through this process, eventually constituting what we now call chōnin culture. Unlike the aristocratic culture of the Heian period or the warrior culture that prevailed from the Kamakura shogunate onward, this was largely an urban commoners’ culture. Its makers and consumers were drawn chiefly from townspeople, and popular forms such as *rakugo* and kabuki prospered. These performances and artifacts were thoroughly commercial, and their development drove further urban growth. Woodblock carving and related printing technologies supplied the necessary technical support. Artists had long depended on aristocratic, religious, or warrior patrons, a dependence that shaped what art could represent. Once works could be reproduced in quantity and sold among commoners, the themes and images of urban culture flooded into them. Their makers gained more room to work beyond elite patronage, even while remaining subject to shogunal censorship.[^6]

The rise of chōnin culture challenged traditional ideas on many fronts. A social ethos built on money and consumption made personal satisfaction part of cultural life. The refined might have dismissed such conduct as unbearably vulgar, but they could not arrest the appeal of this way of living. The *ukiyozōshi* and *kōshoku* literature exemplified by *The Life of an Amorous Man*, together with the many erotic *shunga* produced within ukiyo-e, make the point plain. The “amorousness” of the floating world did not simply mean surrender to carnal desire. It elevated love as a pursuit and released feelings suppressed within the heart.[^7] The appearance and wide circulation of these works registered a popular affirmation, even a celebration, of human desire, feeling, and vitality. They stood in sharp contrast to the Cheng-Zhu Neo-Confucianism honored by warrior culture, with its maxim to “preserve heavenly principle and eliminate human desire.” The pleasure quarters centered on courtesans, the performing arts exemplified by kabuki, and the visual arts exemplified by ukiyo-e all used abundant urban subject matter to unsettle inherited judgments of refinement and vulgarity, beauty and ugliness. The essay reads this as a new morality not founded on political rank: in a society that represented the order of warriors, farmers, artisans, and merchants as immovable, chōnin culture could carry a radical promise of equality and openness.

Read Touhou’s stories from this perspective and the desire and everyday warmth of street life quietly recur throughout the works. ZUN has never supplied a detailed monetary or socioeconomic system for Touhou, but money’s importance in its stories is undeniable. The poverty of the Hakurei Shrine is not only a consensus among fan creators; it appears repeatedly in official works. Kappa are perennial business operators. Even in Hell, the shops on the Road of Liminality form an important source of revenue. Money and commerce may matter in Touhou not only at face value but as an acknowledgement of actual life. The girls may not be merchants by occupation, yet from the festivals held at every opportunity to the fierce struggles over faith, they already possess much of the merchant’s temperament.

Money is bound to consumption, and consumption is driven by desire. Yet people ultimately consume in pursuit of bodily pleasure, emotional fulfillment, and richer lives. In a poll message for Mokou, ZUN once wrote: “The foremost basis for being human is the conviction that one is human. DNA comes second.”[^15] Mokou is no longer an ordinary human being, but her deep fixation on death allows her to retain human desire. This sharply contrasts with ZUN’s rejection of the “purity” represented by Kyoto and the “eternity” represented by the Lunar Capital. Kyoto has discarded everything. However keenly it searches for shadows of the past, it only adds more “boring” vexations. If one clings to tradition and lives where every “impurity” has been excluded, meanwhile, endless time wears every desire away. Only a desireless being can attain true eternity. In *Silent Sinner in Blue*, even the Lunarians must treat spiritual fulfillment as an important problem. Reimu may be called a “yōkai shrine maiden,” but the powerful air of the marketplace about her makes her thoroughly human. *Ten Desires* even introduces her as the “Shrine Maiden of Five Desires.”[^17] The girls care about money without being bewitched by it, and lacking money never plunges them into real deprivation. They are therefore spared the corrosive side of a money society. The essay treats that freedom as a blessing ZUN bestows on these chōnin girls.

The Tokyo Renko describes in *Retrospective 53 Minutes* does not materially surpass Kyoto. Rather, it holds fast to the “Japanese spirit.” Its vast shopping centers, freely accessible amusements, and fashionably dressed crowds may lie far from traditional forms, but they carry more of Edo’s blood than the New Tea Ceremony confined to a “sealed room.” In our own world, Kyoto is conventionally treated as the representative of Japan’s traditional spirit, while Tokyo symbolizes the troubles of modern Japanese society. If Kyoto is read not as a city but as a symbol of that traditional spirit, the track “Retrospective Kyoto” becomes easier to understand. What the two women remember is not the historical city of Kyoto, but the stubbornly vital Tokyo toward which they are traveling.

At a more fundamental level, the desire to enjoy life, the ties people form through “consumption,” and the question of how to regard tradition in a changing world all express a powerful and distinct will to live. This does not merely mean surviving bad conditions without abandoning hope, nor does it mean withdrawing from the world and scraping by within one’s own enclosure. To live this way is to make things, take pleasure in them, and turn dissatisfaction into the possibility of something new. The incidents stirred up by yōkai, the fairies’ endless daily pranks, and the struggles around the Human Village or Gensokyo itself all share this quality. Participants and troublemakers alike are enjoying themselves. The revelry and drinking after an incident is resolved make that clear.

One further element is indispensable: openness, though not the indiscriminate acceptance of everything. The culture created by chōnin contains brilliant treasures and coarse, vulgar pleasures of the flesh. These are two faces of the same coin, naturally fused and indivisible. Its works accept reality and depict it without evasion. Their creators have values and positions of their own, but do not partition the world into “them” and “us.” That is why so many colors and styles can express reality in all its breadth. “Gensokyo accepts everything” means more than accepting the “outsiders” discussed above. Every sort of character can find a place there. From ZUN’s standpoint as creator, both the arrival of new characters and their ceaseless recreation in fan works give them new personalities and continually reshape Gensokyo. This openness may be a major reason Touhou has flourished. The world of the Secret Sealing Club presents the reverse: expelling the “heretical” also means closing oneself off. As *Magical Astronomy* says, no unsolved problems remain in that world. Like Mount Fuji after it becomes extinct, it can endure as cultural heritage forever, but it can create no new spirit.

## III. Ukiyo-e, Utagawa Hiroshige, and Katsushika Hokusai

Another object discussed by Merry and Renko in *Retrospective 53 Minutes* is the scenery projected by the Kaleido-screen. Its designers presumably used ukiyo-e as a model to accentuate the system’s “Japanese spirit.” In their age, however, repackaged ukiyo-e no longer means what it did when first made. From one angle it is, just as Merry says, no more than “boring visual stimulation.”

As the visual expression of chōnin culture, ukiyo-e makes that colorful era more immediately palpable than words can. The term itself—“pictures of the floating world”—points to contemporary life and the fashions of the day. Street life and the emotions and desires displayed in everyday existence remain its abiding center.

[fig] Katsushika Hokusai, [*Mitsui Shop at Surugachō in Edo*](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/55287), from *Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji*, ca. 1830–32.

![Workers on the roofs of the Mitsui shop fly a kite as Mount Fuji rises beyond Surugachō in Hokusai’s print](attachments/roof-archive/cv6229226/04-hokusai-mitsui-surugacho.png)

Early ukiyo-e drew on native pictorial traditions, including *yamato-e*, while redirecting older painting and printing techniques toward the life of the contemporary city. Woodblock printing had been used in Japan for centuries, especially for Buddhist texts; the innovation of ukiyo-e lay in how artists and publishers put it to new secular and commercial uses. Hishikawa Moronobu was so important to this development that later accounts often called him the “father of ukiyo-e.”[^8] An accomplished illustrator, he gave images increasing independence from text and helped popularize single-sheet genre prints. His paintings and prints of courtesans also helped establish *bijin-ga*, images of beautiful women, as a major subject for later artists.

[fig] Hishikawa Moronobu, [*Beauty Looking Back*](https://www.tnm.jp/modules/r_collection/index.php?colid=A60&controller=dtl&id=11&lang=en&t=type), late seventeenth century.

![A woman in a patterned red kimono glances back over her shoulder in Moronobu’s hanging-scroll painting *Beauty Looking Back*](attachments/roof-archive/cv6229226/05-morunobu-beauty-looking-back.png)

In ukiyo-e’s middle period, color, paper, and production methods advanced greatly. Okumura Masanobu and Utagawa Toyoharu incorporated Western perspective into ukiyo-e, giving scenes a new sense of depth. Suzuki Harunobu and others pioneered *nishiki-e*, or “brocade pictures,” whose rich arrays of printed color evoked the brilliance of woven brocade. Poetry circles’ enthusiasm for exchanging privately commissioned picture calendars, together with the flourishing of kabuki, supplied a broad market. Economic growth and a professional division of labor among the artist (*eshi*), block carver (*horishi*), printer (*surishi*), and publisher (*hanmoto*) made higher output possible. Together, these technical and commercial changes accelerated ukiyo-e’s development.

[fig] Utagawa Toyoharu, [*Perspective Picture of a Grand Party in the New Yoshiwara*](https://collections.mfa.org/objects/206648), ca. 1775–80.

![Courtesans, attendants, and servants form a line through the deep interior of a New Yoshiwara house in Toyoharu’s perspective print](attachments/roof-archive/cv6229226/06-toyoharu-shin-yoshiwara.png)

Traditional *bijin-ga* changed substantially in this period. As artists became more attentive to setting and everyday gesture, their “beauties” acquired a greater sense of individual presence. Torii Kiyonaga, Kitao Shigemasa, and others often placed graceful but relatively natural-looking women in scenes of ordinary life, rather than presenting beauty only as an isolated ideal. The portraits of courtesans and kabuki actors made by Kitagawa Utamaro and Tōshūsai Sharaku exerted an especially deep influence on later ukiyo-e artists.

[fig] Torii Kiyonaga’s *Cooling on the Riverbank* and Kitagawa Utamaro’s *Three Beauties of the Kansei Era*.

![Side-by-side prints: Kiyonaga’s group of women and a child cooling by a river, and Utamaro’s close portrait of three women](attachments/roof-archive/cv6229226/07-kiyonaga-utamaro.png "=100%")

Later, figure-centered ukiyo-e gradually ceased to lead the art’s stylistic development. *Meisho-e*, pictures of famous places devoted to landscape and urban life, came to prominence. Hokusai’s *Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji* and Hiroshige’s *Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō* became cultural emblems of Japan. These works are not confined to topographical realism, nor do their makers simply present beautiful scenery. They lodge feeling within the landscape and use scenery to express emotion—an “impressionistic” mode in the essay’s broad sense of the word. In composition and color, both artists pursue expressive force rather than merely foregrounding the pleasures of everyday custom. Japanese prints would later become an important influence on European artists, including the Impressionists, through the movement known as Japonisme.

With the arrival of the “Black Ships” and the Meiji Restoration, the program of “civilization and enlightenment,” new forms of mass media, and Western-style photography gradually displaced Edo-period ukiyo-e from its old place in the popular market. Its subjects also shifted toward current affairs in “news pictures” and “enlightenment pictures.” Output remained substantial, but the older production system and its audience changed rapidly. Ukiyo-e eventually ceased to be a dominant mode of commercial visual culture, even as its creative elements and its record of the “Japanese spirit” continued to circulate.

[fig] Utagawa Yoshitora, [*Vehicles on the Streets of Tokyo*](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/55545), 1870.

![Horse-drawn carriages, handcarts, riders, and pedestrians crowd a Tokyo street in Yoshitora’s three-sheet print](attachments/roof-archive/cv6229226/10-yoshitora-tokyo-vehicles.png)

Although a product of a particular historical period, ukiyo-e became one of visual art’s finest records of Japan’s traditional spirit. Two of its most conspicuous artistic traits are line and color. Its compositions generally avoid symmetry. The resulting imbalance gives the image the immediacy of a captured moment and, with it, a sense of motion. People and things are shaped with fluent lines rather than masses of color. This both reveals their graceful postures and intensifies the picture’s movement. In some sense, it also suggests that the artist does not stand outside the work but merges with the scene.[^9]

In color, many ukiyo-e prints rely on flat planes and sharp contrasts rather than modeled light and shadow. This can lend the picture clarity while intensifying the “impression” that its places and figures make upon artist and viewer. The nineteenth-century adoption of synthetic Prussian blue greatly expanded the range of deep, stable blues available to artists such as Hokusai and Hiroshige.[^10] The essay connects this emotional expression to the spirit of *mono no aware*, the aesthetic idea systematized by Motoori Norinaga, and to the encouragement of feeling in *kōshoku* literature. *Mono no aware* chiefly means an emotional responsiveness to things and to the changes of human life. It is not confined to lamenting impermanence or grieving that beauty fades. It can also include delight at new life or the sense of force a viewer feels before a crashing wave.[^11] Ukiyo-e thus does more than record its subjects: it draws viewers into a felt world of traditional Japanese life.

Seen in this light, the works of Hokusai and Hiroshige approach the matter differently, yet both possess a powerful Japanese character. Even the booklet of *Retrospective 53 Minutes* has Merry and Renko briefly debate which artist deserves to represent “Japanese scenery” on the Kaleido-screen. People of the future choose Hiroshige because they can no longer bear Hokusai’s “madness.” Hokusai did frequently call himself a “madman,” but his paintings are hardly as inaccessible as contemporary black metal. The meaning of the choice cannot be so simple.

Born in Edo and adopted into the household of a mirror polisher, Hokusai led a life almost as restless as his pictures. He apprenticed as a block cutter before entering Katsukawa Shunshō’s studio, changed artistic names and residences repeatedly, and endured serious personal and financial difficulties. In the late 1820s, deaths in the family, a wayward grandson’s gambling debts, and a possible stroke coincided with a decline in commissions. In 1839, a fire destroyed his home and much of his working material. None of this extinguished his devotion to art. In the 1834 postscript to *One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji*, he summed up his ambitions:

> “Until the age of seventy, nothing I drew was worthy of notice. ... At one hundred years I will have achieved a divine state.”[^12]

Despite a life filled with hardship, Hokusai’s pictures remain full of energy and force. In *Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji*, he grants ample character and living presence both to Fuji and to the people who live around it. The images move. Their greatness lies not only in the marvel of natural power but, in this essay’s reading, in Hokusai’s spiritual transcendence of suffering. His best-known landscape series followed the difficult years of the late 1820s, and at life’s end he still wished for a little more time to become a “true artist.” In the essay’s reading, the hope that carried Hokusai through pain also deepened his art.

[fig] Katsushika Hokusai, [*Under the Wave off Kanagawa*](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/45434) (“The Great Wave”), ca. 1830–32.

![Three boats pitch beneath a towering claw-like wave while Mount Fuji appears small on the horizon in Hokusai’s *Under the Wave off Kanagawa*](attachments/roof-archive/cv6229226/11-hokusai-great-wave.png)

Hiroshige’s life, by contrast, unfolded amid contradiction and constraint. Born into a low-ranking samurai family, he inherited a minor hereditary post in Edo’s fire service. That position placed him within the ruling estate without making him wealthy, and designing prints became another source of income. The essay reads his muted style through this divided status: drawn to the chōnin’s freer urban culture, yet restrained by his place within the warrior order. His travel imagery, above all *Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō*, can awaken a powerful longing for places seen or imagined. An attachment to nature, moonlight, and wind appears throughout his pictures. Unlike the force felt in Hokusai, Hiroshige’s work is quiet. Yet the people and things within it often seem spiritually connected—an affinity the essay links to Japanese animism. The many boldly foregrounded motifs in *One Hundred Famous Views of Edo* place the spectator inside the view and heighten its emotional immediacy.

[fig] Utagawa Hiroshige, [*Yoroi Ferry, Koami-chō*](https://www.brooklynmuseum.org/opencollection/objects/121659), from *One Hundred Famous Views of Edo*, 1857.

![Boats cross the Nihonbashi River beside a long row of riverside warehouses in Hiroshige’s *Yoroi Ferry, Koami-chō*](attachments/roof-archive/cv6229226/12-hiroshige-yoro-no-watashi.png)

In Renko and Merry’s age, people ultimately choose Hiroshige, just as Mount Fuji finally becomes a World Heritage site. The essay suggests that they do so because humanity has become spiritually “mature” and will advance no further. Immortal reinforced concrete replaces mutable nature and becomes the new “nature” in collective memory. The ultimate problems of physics have been solved, and no one need explore how to face a question whose answer is unknown. Even metaphysical things such as culture and traditional spirit retain no value beyond commemoration and ornament. The vitality in Hokusai’s work has become too heavy for such people. Only Hiroshige’s nostalgia fits their commitment to a permanent, technologically managed world. This may be the second meaning of the track “Retrospective Kyoto.”

Whatever the case, the idea is steeped in self-deception. Mount Fuji does not truly die when it becomes an extinct volcano, but when it is designated a World Heritage site. Only a spirit that no longer lives in everyday experience needs commemoration. To Merry, an outsider, any scenery however “Japanese” is only “boring visual stimulation.” The feeling in Hiroshige’s work depends on both artist and viewer responding to a landscape they know as part of life—the kind of responsiveness called *mono no aware*. To advertise a true “Japanese spirit” through false scenery is an exquisite irony.

In his afterword, ZUN says that the story continues *Changeability of Strange Dream*’s inquiry into “dreams and reality, virtual and real.” When the album appeared, much of this remained fantasy. Now that VR and AR have become practical technologies, its scenes present a credible possibility. Technology has made a future in which virtual images displace direct experience seem foreseeable. In literature and the visual arts, meanwhile, cyberpunk worlds defined by digital technology have become immensely popular. Tall towers and glaring neon utterly dominate the visual field. “Reality and illusion” here chiefly concerns the loss of our ability to perceive the old natural world after living in such an environment. *Retrospective 53 Minutes* does not explicitly identify the virtual with a world constructed from information, but a later album, *Dr. Latency’s Freak Report*, directly raises the problem of information worship. In the essay’s reading, dependence on information and environments built from it will increasingly mediate what people perceive until their experience itself feels virtual. Consider one core of the Japanese spirit discussed above, *mono no aware*. On this essay’s account, genuine feeling arises when the mind responds to an actual thing. If an image alone is the object, the feelings we experience may already have been designed and directed. This is especially true in a society saturated by marketing, where gifted artists and propagandists exploit everything from popular emotion to popular thought. Is humanity really virtual in essence? Perhaps the claim is only a satire upon a society overly dependent on information. ZUN is not advocating a return to the past. Civilization has reached the present by continually creating information and using it to build systems that resist risk. Yet instrumental reason becomes dangerous when the tool itself becomes the substance of thought. Reality and illusion then collapse into one because people no longer care to distinguish them.

How can this problem be solved? ZUN’s story gives no clear answer. For Merry and Renko this is simply their reality. They may feel the need to seek another way, but their habits of thought and their vantage point make doing so difficult. As readers living outside the story, we can recognize that danger and resist a future whose beginnings are already visible. No projected vista, however beautiful, can replace the color of the actual sky. Even the finest fantasy needs real sunlight to enter it. Human beings do not encounter the world through belief or data alone. We need to look at things directly and remain able to respond to them. The essay finds in ZUN a hope that people can preserve this appetite for living even in a highly mediated society. Desire makes them dissatisfied with stasis; hope moves them to make something new. If people surrender their perception to a world assembled entirely from information, they lose that capacity to change, and managed eternity becomes a dead end.

In the end, Touhou itself is one virtual world among countless others, and the reality readers feel in its stories may partly be their own projection. Its beauty and longing matter only when readers carry them beyond the fiction: taking the hope and strength the stories provide, looking closely at the world they inhabit, and using that attention to live and create more fully. Perhaps that is the deepest reassurance an audience can offer ZUN—that his imagined world sends them back to reality with a stronger desire to live.
