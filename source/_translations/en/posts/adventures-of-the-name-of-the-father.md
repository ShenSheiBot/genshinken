> Pilot session in a discussion series on animation, comics, and games (ACG), organized by Fudan University’s Feidian (Boiling Point) Manga Club<br />
> Speaker: **Fishboard**<br />
> Commentator: **Zhang**

**Fishboard:**

Let’s begin with Freud’s famous Oedipus complex and the bourgeois nuclear family that supplies its model.

![Simple family triangle, with father and mother above the subject or child](attachments/roof-archive/cv1708099/translations/en/01-family-triangle.png?v=71bbb843)

The diagram is a simple triangle: father and mother above, subject or child below. In Freud’s account, the child first identifies with the mother. The father intervenes with the threat of castration, breaking that bond and redirecting the child’s identification toward himself. Anna Freud and several British and American schools of psychoanalysis later placed more emphasis on identification with the mother. Jacques Lacan, by contrast, claimed fidelity to Freud partly by keeping the father at the center, though he radically changed what “father” meant.

Lacan’s main tool was structuralism. Put very broadly, structuralism takes insights from twentieth-century linguistics and asks how systems of difference organize other forms of human life. Lacan keeps the Oedipal triangle but turns the father from a biological parent into a position within a symbolic structure. That position gives us the first thread of today’s talk: the **Name-of-the-Father**, or *Nom-du-Père*.

A joke about Slavoj Žižek, which I first heard from Zhang, brings us close to the idea. When journalists ask Žižek the usual tedious questions about his private life—whether he gets along with his children, for instance—he replies, “I’m not a good father.” In ordinary speech, that might mean he is too absorbed in his work to take proper care of them. In Lacanian terms, however, no flesh-and-blood man can ever be the “good father.” The father properly assumes his role only when the actual man gives way to a name. As a signifier, the word “father” replaces—or, in Lacan’s idiom, “kills”—the concrete person as a thing and installs a symbolic function in his place. The proper father is this function carried by a name, not the person behind it. James Joyce draws the distinction beautifully in *Ulysses*:

> Fatherhood, in the sense of conscious begetting, is unknown to man. It is a mystical estate, an apostolic succession, from only begetter to only begotten.
>
> On that mystery and not on the madonna which the cunning Italian intellect flung to the mob of Europe the church is founded and founded irremovably because founded, like the world, macro and microcosm, upon the void. Upon incertitude, upon unlikelihood. AMOR MATRIS, subjective and objective genitive, may be the only true thing in life. Paternity may be a legal fiction. Who is the father of any son that any son should love him or he any son?

Joyce is playing with the Christian doctrine of the Virgin Mary, but he has also put his finger on an old asymmetry. For most of human history, maternity could be known with a certainty unavailable to biological paternity. Yet uncertainty about begetting did not leave everyone fatherless. Kinship, names, and law supplied the father as a social and symbolic fact. Paternity may be a fiction, but it is a consequential one: it gives the child an external vantage point from which to imagine what kind of person he ought to become. The ego ideal arises not from the actual father’s perfection but from the empty office occupied by his name.

That brings us to our first anime and its most famous father: Gendo Ikari. In this narrow Lacanian sense, Gendo is an outstandingly “good” father. Consider his first encounter with Shinji in episode one of the *Neon Genesis Evangelion* television series.

[fig] In the name of the father.

![Gendo Ikari issuing an order to Shinji Ikari](attachments/roof-archive/cv1708099/02-gendo-orders-shinji.jpg)

This is Gendo’s first appearance in the role of the Name-of-the-Father. Why call him such an exemplary father? Before this scene, he has done almost nothing to care for Shinji as a parent. Yet the moment he appears, he stands high above Shinji, all distance and absolute authority, and gives him an order. That order is also a prohibition. Lacan’s pun makes the point compactly: *Nom-du-Père*, the Name-of-the-Father, shades into *Non-du-Père*, the father’s “No.”

In the classical Oedipal story, the child desires the mother until the father’s threat of castration breaks that bond and leads the child to identify with him instead. Lacan reads this intervention not simply as an interruption but as an exchange. In return for renouncing the mother, the subject receives something from the paternal order: **the symbolic phallus, $\Phi$, in exchange for the imaginary phallus, $\varphi$.**

The Greek word *phallus* originally referred to the male organ. Since Freud introduced the term into psychoanalysis, however—and especially in the work of Lacan and his followers—it has come to mean something different from the penis as a bodily organ. The symbolic phallus is a signifier, a word that represents the subject’s desire. The father offers this word in exchange for the subject’s renunciation of desire for the mother.

The *Evangelion* scene stages this exchange with unusual clarity. It is not only Gendo’s first appearance but Rei Ayanami’s as well. When Gendo orders Shinji to pilot the Eva, the object Shinji gives up is Rei: the imaginary phallus, whose body is in fact a clone of Shinji’s mother, Yui Ikari. The Oedipal reference could hardly be clearer. In exchange, “Eva” becomes the signifier that represents Shinji’s desire and gives him a place within the paternal order.

[fig] The graph of desire (left).

![Blurry archival chalkboard sketch of the graph of desire, with arrows joining circled symbols and most annotations too indistinct to read](attachments/roof-archive/cv1708099/03-desire-graph.jpg)

The chalkboard photograph is rough, and most of its annotations are illegible, so let me spell out the part we need without asking the image to do that work. The barred S, $\bar S$, stands for a subject divided against himself: Shinji at the start of the scene, a bewildered weakling who cannot say what he wants. The capital A stands for the big Other, the symbolic authority whose demand addresses him; Gendo occupies that position here. Once Shinji identifies with that authority, “Eva” gives his desire a name. The exchange costs him the imaginary phallus, the fantasy bound up with the mother, but it also produces an ego ideal. Shinji can now see himself from the vantage point of the role he has accepted: the boy who pilots the Eva. The graph turns an empty “What am I supposed to be?” into a position, however punishing that position may prove.

Claude Lévi-Strauss used structural anthropology to study societies he regarded as less developed and “primitive.” In those studies, the paternal signifier acts in two directions at once. The incest taboo does not merely forbid marriage within a kin group; it also directs marriage outward, organizing exchange between groups. The law says both “not this person” and “someone elsewhere.” *Evangelion* gives this double movement dramatic form. Because Rei is cloned from Yui, she occupies the forbidden maternal position; Asuka occupies the permitted position beyond it. If Gendo is the stern father who forbids Shinji’s attachment to Rei, who directs him toward Asuka?

That question brings us to a second paternal figure: the **anal father**. Unlike the stern father who issues prohibitions, this father is wholly permissive: he tells his child to enjoy.

In *Evangelion*, Misato Katsuragi occupies this role. Shinji moves into her home, Asuka later joins them, and Misato repeatedly nudges the two together. As Lacan once said, a woman, too, can be a Name-of-the-Father. The Name-of-the-Father is nothing more than a symbolic function or position, and anyone can occupy it. The structure comes first; particular individuals fill its functions.

Why place Misato beside Gendo? Because the permissive injunction to enjoy is not simply the opposite of prohibition. Misato is Shinji’s superior and Gendo’s subordinate; she carries NERV’s authority into the home. Her encouragement of intimacy with Asuka looks indulgent, yet it still tells Shinji what he ought to want. “No” and “Enjoy!” turn out to be two voices of the same order.

To understand that relation, we need one of Lacan’s most famous pairings: **Kant with Sade**. Kantian ethics asks us to set aside “pathological” motives—our private pleasures, pains, and interests—and act only on a principle that could hold universally. Morality does not depend on whether an action makes any particular person happy; its authority comes from the form of the law. The Marquis de Sade, whose name gave us the “S” in **S&M**, made cruelty and sexual transgression the subject of works such as *Philosophy in the Bedroom*. What could he possibly have to do with Kant?

Max Horkheimer and Theodor Adorno had already brought the two together in *Dialectic of Enlightenment*. They regarded Sade as a consequence of Kantian ethics: by raising human beings to the position of legislators for all things, Kant’s ethics freed the bourgeois subject from traditional restraints, and Sade was precisely such a subject. Lacan appears to have arrived at the pairing independently. Where Kant gives us a critique of pure reason, Lacan constructs something like a critique of pure desire.

Kant asks us to imagine a man who wants to sleep with someone waiting in a room. Put a gallows outside and tell him he may enter, but will be hanged as soon as he comes out. Kant expects him to master his appetite and walk away. Lacan finds this naïve. Desire does not always submit to rational calculation. A Sadean subject may enter despite the gallows—and may be drawn less by the person in the room than by the gallows itself, the fatal prohibition that turns the act into transgression.

[fig] Kant with Sade.

![Portraits of Kant and Sade arranged one above the other](attachments/roof-archive/cv1708099/04-kant-and-sade.jpg)

Georges Bataille had made a similar point before Lacan: we need institutions, but we also need excess. Only when an institution has imposed a prohibition can we enjoy breaking it. Put another way, the law’s merciless “No” creates the forbidden act whose transgression produces *jouissance*. Prohibition thus generates the very breach it condemns; in that sense, the law transgresses itself. Lacan goes further still, declaring law one of the gravest crimes humanity has committed.

Think of the familiar iceberg metaphor: a small visible portion of the psyche above the waterline and a much larger, hidden mass below.

![Photograph of an iceberg above and below the waterline](attachments/roof-archive/cv1708099/05-iceberg-model.jpg)

How does the paternal Other’s prohibition keep working once the father is no longer standing over us? The subject internalizes it as the superego. What first arrived as an external command becomes an inner voice that polices the ego. The ego suffers under its demands; the superego derives *jouissance* from making them ever harsher. This is why obedience does not put an end to punishment. The law can become a machine through which one part of the psyche torments another and enjoys the torment.

Now we can see why the Name-of-the-Father and the anal father are not cleanly opposed. At the end of this dialectical turn, they reveal themselves as the same paternal function. The prohibition “You must not” carries an obscene supplement: “Enjoy your obedience, your guilt, and your punishment.” Conversely, the permissive command “Enjoy!” quickly becomes another duty. The law’s authority depends on an enjoyment that also corrodes it.

Return to the Oedipal bargain. The subject gives up the imaginary phallus and receives a symbolic signifier for desire. As long as the father maintains the stern bearing of the Name-of-the-Father, the exchange can still look fair. Once it becomes clear that the father obtains illicit *jouissance* by tormenting and symbolically castrating the subject, however, the exchange begins to look **unequal** from the subject’s side. The father seems to have kept more and lost less; the subject seems to have given up a little more than he received. Lacan calls this surplus lost by the subject **objet petit a**, and the subject’s desire now aims precisely at that object.

The subject urgently challenges the father to complete the exchange and make it equal—to return the *objet petit a* he confiscated. The challenge takes the form of a question: **What, exactly, do you want from me?** What must I do before you will give back the abject remainder you took away? But the father does not know what this confiscated object is either. His inability to answer produces our third figure: the **humiliated father**.

What does that humiliation look like? Consider Gendo at the end of *The End of Evangelion*.

[fig] The humiliated father.

![Gendo Ikari lying beneath a long shadow in The End of Evangelion, as identified in the lecture and response](attachments/roof-archive/cv1708099/translations/en/06-humiliated-father.png?v=d29e6898)

The majestic Name-of-the-Father has already disclosed its obscene enjoyment; now that enjoyment destroys the majesty itself. Gendo is physically defeated, but, more importantly, he collapses as a name and a symbolic function. He admits what his authority had concealed: he was afraid to face Shinji. When Shinji’s “What do you want from me?” finally returns to him, Gendo has no answer.

Instead, he tries to pass the question on to Yui, the maternal figure beyond him—what we might write as *mOther*, placing the capital-O Other inside “mother.” But Lacan insists that **there is no Other of the Other**. No higher authority guarantees the father’s authority or keeps the final answer in reserve. The *mOther* occupies the place of Freud’s *das Ding*, the **Thing**: the imagined presence that could tell the subject what the Other really wants and bring desire to rest. That presence does not exist.

This is where anxiety enters. The question travels from the divided subject to the paternal Other, only to reveal that the Other is itself incomplete. The subject can find no final demand to obey, yet he cannot simply dismiss the demand. Freud and Lacan call the affect produced at this point **anxiety: an affect without an object**. The hoped-for object remains suspended within the unanswered question, leaving the subject exposed to lack itself. We invent an Other who could answer us because that lack is harder to bear.

This failure brings us to **Atè**, a limit Lacan draws from Sophocles’ tragedy *Antigone*. In *Antigone*, Atè is the name of a goddess; Lacan interprets it as a boundary. On one side of Atè, the subject glimpses the truth of desire: the object he ultimately wants and has ultimately lost. But once the subject crosses Atè and touches that object, the subject disappears, because the object exists only in the imagination and not in reality. This boundary between life and death leads us to the next anime in today’s talk, Makoto Shinkai’s *The Place Promised in Our Early Days*.

Before turning to Shinkai, let me add one more example. *Flip Flappers*, an anime whose main story pays tribute to *Evangelion* in many ways, also gives us an especially abject example of the humiliated father. Who humiliates him? A maternal figure, cast as the object capable of answering his question.

![Two heroines from Flip Flappers embrace as a large dark silhouette closes around them](attachments/roof-archive/cv1708099/07-flip-flappers.gif)

Back in *Evangelion*, giant Rei appears after the scene we just discussed and carries out the Human Instrumentality Project. She is **das Ding**, the imagined object believed capable of answering the question. But that object does not in fact exist. If the subject ever reached the point where “What, exactly, do you want from me?” received its answer, the subject’s desire would disappear, and so would the subject.

Lacan’s formula for fantasy makes this structure visible: $\bar S \lozenge a$, the barred subject on one side and *objet petit a* on the other, joined and separated by a lozenge. Fantasy connects the subject to the cause of desire while keeping the two apart. That distance is not an unfortunate obstacle. Only while *objet petit a* remains out of reach can a human being escape the animal inertia of directly realizing its own essence and become a subject. In the terms of this talk, Atè names the separating limit. Turn the lozenge into a straight path along which the subject simply reaches the object, and the subject disappears.

[fig] Atè as the limit between the barred subject and *objet petit a*.

![Formula illustrating the limit of Atè](attachments/roof-archive/cv1708099/08-ate-formula.jpg)

Now to *The Place Promised in Our Early Days*. Japan has been divided into northern and southern zones. Hiroki, the male protagonist, lives south of the Tsugaru Strait. Across the water to the north stands an enormous, mysterious tower. It is a phallic object of fascination—not the symbolic phallus that names desire, but an imaginary object that seems to promise its fulfillment. As a boy, Hiroki gazes at the tower and plans to build a small plane with Sayuri so that they can fly there together. The tower gives their shared longing a destination, while Sayuri becomes the person in whom Hiroki invests that promise. Then she falls into a deep sleep and is moved to a Tokyo hospital. Hiroki abandons the project and drifts through the years that follow.

In Tokyo, he begins to dream about Sayuri again. He learns that an unexplained memory disorder has kept her asleep in a hospital since the summer she turned fifteen and, without much narrative motivation, returns to the abandoned plane and his childhood dream of seeing that phallus with her. When war breaks out between the northern and southern zones, Hiroki seizes the outbreak of fighting as his chance to fly the sleeping Sayuri across the Tsugaru Strait. He crosses Atè and comes into contact with the imaginary phallus. Sayuri wakes after they reach the tower, and the story ends there.

![The tower and Hiroki’s aircraft approaching it in The Place Promised in Our Early Days](attachments/roof-archive/cv1708099/translations/en/09-clouds-beyond-scene-one.png?v=c69a4785)

![Hiroki reaches toward the sleeping Sayuri aboard their aircraft in The Place Promised in Our Early Days](attachments/roof-archive/cv1708099/translations/en/10-clouds-beyond-scene-two.png?v=227d31c2)

The film stops at the fairy-tale moment of awakening, exactly where “happily ever after” usually spares us the trouble of imagining life after the wish comes true. From a psychoanalytic perspective, the abrupt ending looks like a repression: the film reaches fulfillment and refuses to picture its consequences. I think Shinkai finally pictures those consequences in *5 Centimeters per Second*. That later film supplies the ending withheld here and introduces the final figure in my sequence—not another version of the father, but **the father’s absence**. Once the humiliated father has lost his authority, the subject left to manage desire alone becomes what I will call the **post-Oedipal subject**.

[fig] Giant Rei in *Evangelion*.

![Giant Rei in Evangelion, as identified in the lecture](attachments/roof-archive/cv1708099/11-white-rei.jpg)

Giant Rei in *Evangelion* makes the subject’s disappearance after crossing Atè spectacular.

*The Place Promised in Our Early Days* is subtler: it cuts away at the point of contact. *5 Centimeters per Second* carries us beyond that point. At the end, Takaki and Akari pass each other at a railway crossing; he turns and walks away, becoming a post-Oedipal subject.

The post-Oedipal subject has a melancholic structure. Freud explains that structure in “Mourning and Melancholia”: its defining symptom is that the subject treats an object already obtained as though it were already lost. Takaki encounters Akari but does not pursue her. He turns away and puts distance between himself and the object of his desire. Read this scene back into *The Place Promised in Our Early Days*, and the omitted aftermath becomes clear: after Hiroki and Sayuri reach the tower, they break up. Takaki knows that he cannot cross Atè and touch Akari.

[fig] The moment of turning away.

![Railway-crossing scene from 5 Centimeters per Second](attachments/roof-archive/cv1708099/12-railway-turning-scene.jpg)

Now put Kant and Sade beside the final scene between Asuka and Shinji in the original *Evangelion* films. The two face one another after the old paternal order has collapsed. In Kant and Sade, what truly attracts the pervert is the possibility of being hanged. By *Evangelion*’s end, however, the stern Name-of-the-Father has collapsed in humiliation and the gallows have burned down. Once the father who mediated between sexual objects disappears, the sexual relationship disappears as well.

Why, then, do I call Takaki relatively wise? If he actually pursued Akari, what would happen? Consider the life he leads and the ring on Akari’s finger: the reality that **the sexual relationship does not exist** would be exposed. Through his melancholic structure, Takaki avoids tragedy by putting distance between himself and the object of his desire.

As Žižek puts it, prohibition lets the subject avoid confronting nonexistence. *Das Ding*—the maternal Other who could answer every demand and satisfy every desire—does not exist, and that absence produces anxiety. Once the father intervenes and forbids desire for the maternal Other, the subject can return to ordinary psychic life. Complete satisfaction no longer seems inherently impossible; it seems possible but forbidden by someone. The father becomes a convenient obstacle, even a savior. He shields the subject from the Thing’s empty place.

But as the Name-of-the-Father passes through the anal father and becomes the humiliated father, that shelter collapses. What then? The superego manufactures a prohibition from within. The subject avoids the desired object and turns that avoidance into a symptom—a repeated rule that answers “What am I supposed to do?” even as it causes suffering. Takaki’s withdrawal supplies just such an answer. He cannot establish or sustain a relationship with Akari, so he makes distance itself a way of life.

Do I still need to explain what the post-Oedipal subject is? Think about how we become subjects who consciously try to lose weight. No individual father appears and orders us to manage our weight. Instead, the internalized superego issues its own commands: work out, diet, monitor your body, try harder. It torments the subject and obtains *jouissance* from the torment, while giving us something definite to do. This is the post-Oedipal mechanism in miniature.

That completes the main sequence of the talk. Let me add a broader claim. I see the passage from the Name-of-the-Father through the anal father and the humiliated father to the post-Oedipal subject as a kind of psychoanalytic historical materialism. Historically, the disciplined Enlightenment subject of Kant gradually gives way to the neoliberal, Deleuzian **body without organs**. This is also the development of capital—or, more precisely, of capital’s libidinal model.

In today’s globalized world, especially in Europe and North America, neoliberalism promises to remove every prohibition imposed by the Name-of-the-Father under the slogan “It is forbidden to forbid.” But it fails to reckon with the post-Oedipal subject that emerges afterward. So long as we remain within the libidinal economy that sustains capital, we have not been liberated and cannot be.

![Portrait of Jacques Lacan](attachments/roof-archive/cv1708099/13-jacques-lacan.jpg)

Lacan observes that a patient who seeks analysis because a symptom causes suffering also derives *jouissance* from the symptom. Once desire has lost its old coordinates, that symptom may be the only structure keeping a person going. If the father no longer supplies a prohibition, the superego will. For anyone committed to a progressive Enlightenment picture of human freedom, the result is bleak: the command to “be yourself” can become a particularly intimate form of blackmail. The self we build remains inseparable from the compulsions that hold it together.

Is there any way out? Let us return once more to the graph of desire. Even after the post-Oedipal subject has settled into a symptom, one route remains open.

[fig] An archival chalkboard sketch of Lacan’s registers; the annotations are too blurred to carry the argument.

![Blurry archival chalkboard sketch of circles and connecting arrows, with most annotations illegible](attachments/roof-archive/cv1708099/14-three-registers-diagram.jpg)

The paternal law belongs to the Symbolic; the subject’s mirror image belongs to the Imaginary. The path we have followed from anxiety to symptom also remains within the Imaginary, because the subject imagines that the desired object could restore a lost satisfaction. I now want to add a different line: the line of the **Real**. The Real must be strictly distinguished from reality. Where there is reality, there is no Real; where there is the Real, there is no reality.

The problem is how to escape the post-Oedipal circuit of inward prohibition and symptom without simply reinstalling the father. The subject first identified with the father; once the father was exposed and humiliated, desire was cast outward, beyond the paternal Other. The **drive** offers another direction. Rather than sending the subject after an imagined object that would finally satisfy desire, it leads toward a mark of what the big Other itself lacks: the missing signifier written $S(\bar A)$.

That missing signifier is where my psychoanalytic historical materialism locates an alternative to the present libidinal economy. Yet precisely because the signifier is missing, I cannot give this alternative a settled name or a ready-made program. I can only call it X, or borrow Lacan’s strange formula: **“Woman does not exist.”** X does not exist either—not as a stable signifier already contained within the existing symbolic order. “Woman” here names the position that reaches beyond the paternal signifier toward that absence. In this sense, the ultimate historical alternative is woman.

The formulas of sexuation explain this strange, literal formulation. We can leave the other quadrants aside today and concentrate on the lower half. On the left, the barred masculine subject, $\bar S$, reaches toward *objet petit a*, the cause of desire. We can read that line as Atè: the masculine subject crosses Atè and sees *a* but cannot actually touch it. On the right, *La* marks the feminine subject. She, too, crosses Atè and identifies with the father—no subject can escape the father—and with the phallus he supplies as the signifier of desire. But her path does not stop at the phallus. The feminine subject also reaches toward $S(\bar A)$, the signifier missing from the big Other. That second line is why “woman” can name the alternative I have just described.

[fig] The formulas of sexuation: always something new to see.

![Lacan’s formulas of sexuation](attachments/roof-archive/cv1708099/15-sexuation-formula.jpg)

I do not want to give this signifier missing from the big Other an elaborate interpretation. I will only point to what it shares with themes raised by radical thinkers of the twentieth century. Why could this missing signifier not be Badiou’s “passion for the Real”? Why could it not be the specter in Derrida’s *Specters of Marx*, the “void” in Althusser’s late manuscripts, or the historical catastrophe and corpses toward which Benjamin’s new angel turns in “Theses on the Philosophy of History”? Why could it not be messianic time, dialectics at a standstill? In messianic time, in change at a standstill, the missing signifier can free the subject from the pseudo-actions of hysteria and depression and bring it into contact with the Real that persists in stillness.

## Zhang’s Response

This talk has carried me farther from my own field than almost any talk I can remember. When that happens, an academic has one reliable trick: supply your own background music and bring the conversation back into your key. No one can beat you while your own soundtrack is playing. I learned a great deal from Fishboard. In my own encounters with psychoanalysis, I rarely build on this scale or sustain a line of thought across so many concepts and works. I admire both the ambition and the ability to keep the whole design in view.

The discussion of the humiliated father also made me notice something in *The End of Evangelion* for the first time: **the shadow behind Gendo Ikari** is extraordinarily dark. Gendo lies on the ground, spent, while the upright silhouette behind him retains the commanding posture he can no longer sustain. Perhaps that shadow is the flawless image of authority he has always tried to project. No actual person could coincide with it. At the end of his life, Gendo finally separates from the image—from the perfect shadow he could never become.

Now I am going to drag the discussion back onto my own turf—ha ha. Psychoanalysis is not the only theoretical resource for thinking about these anime. Judith Butler’s account of subjection also fits *Evangelion* and Makoto Shinkai’s films remarkably well.

[fig] Judith Butler.

![Portrait of Judith Butler](attachments/roof-archive/cv1708099/17-judith-butler.jpg)

Anyone commanded to obey faces a kind of blackmail: **obey or die**. In *Evangelion*’s opening, Gendo orders Shinji to pilot the Eva: do it or leave. The death being threatened is social rather than immediately physical. Shinji may remain alive, but he will be cast out of the community and denied the life he hoped to enter. Under those terms, obedience looks like the only real choice.

Butler’s crucial move is to locate agency within that coerced choice. External authority cannot supervise every moment or carry out its own orders through us. To become effective, it has to be internalized: the subject must identify with the command and reproduce it as an act of his own will. Once that happens, two possibilities emerge:

1. The subject can get into bed with external authority and become an authority figure in turn: its most loyal attack dog and lackey, voluntarily obeying orders and demanding obedience from others without any supervision. Such a toady can be even more fanatical about following instructions than the cold authority itself.
2. Because authority depends on the subject’s own strength and willingness, internalization also gives the subject a degree of leverage. He can comply unevenly, delay, redirect, refuse, or sabotage the command. Obedience never eliminates agency altogether.

Shinji repeatedly explores the second possibility. He soon realizes that he is often the only person capable of piloting the Eva and saving the world. Gendo and NERV need his cooperation. Shinji’s reluctance, disgust, refusal, and occasional defiance show him testing their dependence on him. Commander Ikari may appear to control everything, but Shinji retains a limited power to decide. That leverage gives his resistance practical force.

![Shinji Ikari piloting an Eva](attachments/roof-archive/cv1708099/18-shinji-in-eva.jpg)

Makoto Shinkai presents a more complicated case, and his popularity in Japan has an immediate social and historical context. I do not know whether you have seen this, but at the end of Shinkai videos on Bilibili I used to see the screen fill with *danmaku*—comments synchronized to the video and sent scrolling across the image—saying things like “So-and-so, I’m sorry” and “So-and-so, I like you.” (Editor’s note: Those comments are still there, lol.)

Japan today has a very low and still falling birth rate, so much of its respectable mainstream culture urges and cajoles people into having children, insisting that children are wonderful. ACG culture, by contrast, keeps its distance from mainstream ideology. In that sense, Shinkai’s *5 Centimeters per Second* is a work for the “Forever Alone” crowd. It meets the command to hurry up and have children with suspicion, detachment, even resistance: being single is fine too, so why have children?

![Takaki seated at a computer in an office in 5 Centimeters per Second](attachments/roof-archive/cv1708099/19-five-centimeters-scene.jpg)

Here again we find Butler’s bind. The state, the family, and the imagined community of “Japan” may speak with the voice of external authority, but they still depend on individuals to carry out the command. That dependence leaves room for refusal: I will not have children; I choose to remain single. The quiet legitimacy Shinkai grants this choice is one way his films resist a mainstream script. Butler’s theory gives us a way to recognize and understand this feature of *Evangelion* and Shinkai’s work.

The difficult question is how much emancipation such resistance can achieve. *5 Centimeters per Second* effectively asks, “What is so terrible about the Forever Alone ending?” Yet if we want not merely to withdraw but to change the world, what does remaining single accomplish? **Resistance is not liberation**, and its effects may be limited. Shinji’s refusals win him a voice and create opportunities to approach Rei on his own terms, but he is still forced back into the Eva. Resistance can alter the balance of power without transforming the world that produced it.
