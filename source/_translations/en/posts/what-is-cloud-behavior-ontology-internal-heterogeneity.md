## 0. Introduction

Over the past two or three years, labels such as 云观众 (*yún guānzhòng*, “*yun* viewer”) and 云玩家 (*yún wánjiā*, “*yun* player”) have become commonplace in Chinese online communities. The prefix 云 (*yún*, literally “cloud”) roughly marks an armchair participant: someone who talks like a viewer or player without having watched or played the work at issue. The terms occur most often in discussions of anime, comics, and games. A “*yun* viewer” of an anime, for instance, has usually never seen it—or never finished it—but still passes judgment and offers interpretations. Yet “*yun* X” can also describe behavior that makes no explicit claim. A “*yun* JoJo fan” might know almost nothing about *JoJo’s Bizarre Adventure* while eagerly repeating its memes and professing devotion to the series.

*Translator’s note:* English has no single productive label matching Chinese 云 in this sense. “Armchair critic” is the closest established functional equivalent, but the essay also covers fandom and nonverbal conduct. The pinyin *yun* is therefore retained; “cloud” would be misleading because *cloud gaming* already means playing games streamed from remote servers.

The pattern is hardly unique to Chinese fan culture. Comparable figures turn up across societies and periods. During China’s poetry boom of the 1980s, many members of our parents’ generation became “*yun* poets”—especially young men hoping a poem might help them court someone. In the West, the mere existence of Pierre Bayard’s *How to Talk About Books You Haven’t Read* suggests that there is no shortage of “*yun* readers.” Among humanities graduate students everywhere, a little “*yun* research” may be closer to the rule than the exception. Who has really read all the hundreds of works listed in a dissertation bibliography?

These labels classify people by something they do. We call someone a “*yun* X” because we take one of their acts to be *yun* behavior. To understand what the labels mean—and what people accomplish by using them—we should therefore begin with the behavior.

What, then, makes an act *yun* behavior? I will answer that question twice. The first answer is ontological: instead of merely collecting examples, can we identify the conditions that make such different acts instances of the same thing? The second is practical: what are people trying to do when they call an act *yun*? That second answer reveals a split within the concept. The label has a common structure, but people use two different—and not entirely compatible—standards to decide who has earned the right to comment.[^1]

## I. An Ontology of “Yun Behavior”

The image below—whose title hyphenates “Yun-Player”—compresses the familiar accusation into three beats: someone who has never played a game watches a video, decides it looked easy, and mocks people who struggle with it. “The Yun-Player Triple Combo” shows how the insult lands more quickly than any dictionary definition could. But one example cannot explain why the label sticks to one act and not another. This section works toward a general answer: the necessary and sufficient conditions for an act to count as *yun* behavior.

![The Yun-Player Triple Combo](attachments/roof-archive/cv8347885/translations/en/01-cloud-gamer-triple-meme.png "=100%")

### 1.1 Nonparticipation

Whether the target is a “*yun* viewer” of anime or a “*yun* player” of games, the first condition is nonparticipation. A *yun* viewer has not watched—or not finished—the anime in question; a *yun* player has not played—or not finished—the game. Nobody needs to know this for certain. The accusation often rests on an inference about whether the person really took part.

### 1.2 Comment-Like Conduct

Mere nonparticipation is not enough. We do not call everyone who has ignored an anime or game a “*yun* X.” The person must also do something that can be read as commentary: confidently passing judgment, as in the meme; pretending to have watched an anime before offering an opinion; or signaling approval or contempt by breaking a disc, reposting something on Weibo or Twitter, or conspicuously displaying a purchase. Such gestures invite interpretation, but none has a single, self-evident meaning.

These are not all literal comments, but each has what I will call *comment-like force*. Let $x$ stand for the act itself: its words, image, or gesture convey some content about a work. Later, the Relevance Rule will identify what the community thinks a person needed to do—watch, play, read, or otherwise engage with the work—before $x$ can claim standing as commentary. For now, the important point is that $x$ presents an attitude or judgment about the work. That is the second condition of *yun* behavior.

### 1.3 The Community as Public Context

Nonparticipation and comment-like conduct can both occur in private. The usual accusation, however, requires a public judgment. In the **typical attribution** of *yun* behavior, other people encounter the act and apply the label. Their response distinguishes the social phenomenon at issue here from a private secondhand opinion.[^2]

Suppose I have never watched *Glasslip* but know its reputation. One day, alone, I see the title and mutter, “This show is garbage.” Nobody hears me. The remark comes from hearsay, but it would not normally count as *yun* behavior in the sense at issue here. Say the same thing in a QQ group where everyone else has watched *Glasslip* and considers it a masterpiece, and the other members may call me a *yun* viewer.

Not every audience can make the charge stick. Imagine that I shout “*Glasslip* is garbage” in a university classroom. My classmates are normies whose knowledge of anime begins and ends with Hayao Miyazaki. I, meanwhile, know anime extremely well but happen to be an ill-tempered, ostracized, gloomy fat otaku. Out of spite, the class contradicts me, declares *Glasslip* a masterpiece, and calls me a “*yun* viewer.” Their chorus alone does not make my remark *yun* behavior. At least some people in the audience must have standing to apply the label.

Having watched *Glasslip* is not the only possible basis for that standing. Indeed, firsthand participation is neither necessary nor sufficient for someone to be recognized as a qualified judge of the label. Consider an extreme case. Three respected forum regulars have never watched *Glasslip*, but they have followed reliable accounts from people who have. They also agree with the broad consensus in major anime-review communities that the show is garbage. I join their discussion and, knowing nothing of what they have said but trusting the studio P.A.WORKS, announce that *Glasslip* is excellent. They immediately call me a “*yun* viewer.” In this hypothetical, their standing comes from their established place in the community and their familiarity with the testimony of people who watched the show—not simply from their agreement with the prevailing view. A community can, in other words, authorize nonparticipants to identify *yun* behavior.

Now reverse the distribution of experience. I still have not watched *Glasslip* and still call it excellent. The other three have watched it and consider it garbage. Yet I am a “veteran otaku” with more than twenty-two years of anime viewing behind me and considerable influence across several social platforms, while they are newcomers who began watching anime this year. If I hide the fact that I have not seen *Glasslip*, my authority may stop them from calling me a *yun* viewer. They may even begin to distrust their own judgment. This does not make me right. It shows that communities confer the standing to apply the label, and that social status can matter as much as direct experience. The person being labeled may also challenge the accuser’s standing rather than simply accept the charge.[^3]

There is no universal test for this standing; the qualifications change with the setting. I will use *qualified member* to mean someone whom a particular community recognizes as entitled to apply “*yun* X.” The term does not tell us whether that recognition is deserved. It simply identifies the people who, in that setting, can make the accusation stick.

### 1.4 Violating Basic Beliefs

We must next ask what prompts the accusation. Suppose I discuss an anime on a forum for self-styled anime elitists. Even if I have not watched it, I am unlikely to be called a “*yun* viewer” so long as I do not contradict certain beliefs the community takes for granted.

These *basic beliefs* are not merely whichever opinion of the work happens to be most popular. They are often beliefs about facts. If I say, “Miuna Shiodome, the heroine of *Glasslip*, is a well-developed character,” I will probably be taken for a *yun* viewer; if I say, “*Glasslip* is the greatest anime of all time,” I am more likely to be taken for a troll. The boundary between fact and evaluation is not clean, however, as a great deal of epistemology has shown. A community may treat an evaluative inference as basic too.

Here is how such a belief can take shape. Suppose a stock claim $p$ circulates mainly among people whom qualified members already regard as nonparticipants. Over time, those members learn to treat agreement with $p$ as evidence that a speaker has not watched the work. Its negation, $\neg p$, then hardens into a communal baseline: affirming $p$ triggers an inference of nonparticipation. That inference comes from the community’s experience of who repeats $p$, not from the logical opposition between $p$ and $\neg p$.

It is difficult to draw a fixed boundary around basic beliefs. A functional definition will do here: they are the beliefs whose violation gives qualified members reason to infer that the person has not undertaken the relevant activity.

### 1.5 The Relevance Rule

One question remains. If *yun* behavior requires nonparticipation, how do we decide what the person was supposed to have done? A comment on a game might require playing that game. A comment on an adaptation might be judged against knowledge of the adaptation, the original, or the franchise as a whole. The work alone cannot decide. The answer also depends on what firsthand engagement a particular community expects before someone makes that particular comment.

For an act $x$, a community $C$, an activity $A$, and the work $W_A$ encountered through that activity, I propose the following Relevance Rule:

**$(\mathrm{RR})$:** $A$ is relevant to $x$ in $C$ if and only if $x$ has comment-like force concerning $W_A$, and $C$ collectively regards participation in $A$ as necessary for $x$ to have standing as commentary on $W_A$.

The rule concerns standing, not physical possibility. Of course someone can utter “*Glasslip* is a masterpiece” without having seen it. But if seasoned members of an anime forum believe that only someone who has watched *Glasslip* has the right to make that judgment, then watching *Glasslip* is the activity relevant to the utterance under $\mathrm{RR}$.

The point of $\mathrm{RR}$ is to capture variations that “watch, appreciate, or play the work” cannot. Activities and works do not line up one to one. In January 2019, for example, the well-known actor Chen Kun used Weibo to recommend the theatrical film *Fate/stay night [Heaven’s Feel]* (HF), reposting an item about it. A netizen who knew nothing of Chen’s earlier public engagement with the franchise asked on Baidu Tieba, “Is this guy just shilling? Is he a *yun* viewer?” Respondents could point to a Weibo post from several years earlier in which Chen had recommended *Fate/Zero* (FZ).

That earlier recommendation does not prove that Chen watched HF—or even that his familiarity with FZ was firsthand. It serves a narrower purpose. For some members of the forum, it establishes enough of a public history with the *Fate* franchise to ward off an immediate charge of opportunistic shilling. In this setting, the accepted credential expands from direct experience of this particular film to a visible record of engagement with the series. When a public figure’s gesture reaches a niche fandom, that broader standard may make members think twice before reaching for the “*yun* X” label.

A more specialized fan community may go in the opposite direction. On a dedicated Tieba or forum, members might insist that anyone commenting on the 2006 anime adaptation of *Fate/stay night* must not only have watched it, but also have played the original game and perhaps know other works in the franchise. A superficial acquaintance with the series can itself invite the charge of being a *yun* viewer. Under $\mathrm{RR}$, then, the community decides which activity bears on a given act, and its standard may broaden or narrow from one context to another.[^4]

### 1.6 Conclusion

We can now state the account more compactly. Let $S$ be an agent and $x$ an act performed by $S$. The act $x$ counts as *yun* behavior if and only if:

$(1)$ $x$ occurs in a public context $C$ where qualified members can judge whether the label applies;

$(2)$ $x$ has comment-like force concerning a work $W_A$;

$(3)$ under $\mathrm{RR}$, the community $C$ treats an activity $A$ as relevant to the standing of $x$ as commentary on $W_A$;

$(4)$ $S$ has not participated in $A$; and

$(5)$ $x$ violates a basic belief $J$ collectively accepted by $C$.

Nobody consciously runs through this checklist before using the insult. It describes the social relations that make the category possible. Like other social kinds sustained through speech, *yun* behavior depends on conventions that can change from one community to another. The opening meme makes the accusation seem obvious; these five conditions show how much communal judgment that apparent obviousness conceals.

## II. Heterogeneity in Pragmatic Aims

There is another way to ask what *yun* behavior is: what does someone accomplish by applying the label? “*Yun* viewer,” “*yun* player,” and “*yun* fan” are all insults, but what exactly do they discredit?

In most cases, the immediate target is an act rather than a person’s identity. Among seasoned anime fans, it is my claim that “Miuna Shiodome, the heroine of *Glasslip*, is a well-developed character”—not a preexisting reputation for knowing little about anime—that gets me called a *yun* viewer. Someone else making the same mistake will probably be treated the same way. The label discredits the act by telling the community that this apparent commentary has no standing.

The label can also be used to attack the work indirectly. Consider “Only *yun* players like this game” or “Most people who talk about this game all day are *yun* players.” The speaker is rarely making a careful demographic claim. The point is that praise from *yun* players does not count: if nobody with a right to comment could love the game, then the game itself must be garbage. Whether aimed directly at an act or indirectly at a work, the label operates one level above the original comment. It is metacommentary—a judgment about which commentary deserves to count at all.

That metacommentary draws on a familiar intuition: a judgment should be grounded in experience of the thing judged. I will call this the experience-to-commentary inference, or **E2C**. Its strict form, **strong E2C**, holds that:

> Firsthand participation in the relevant activity is necessary, though not sufficient, for a comment-like act to have standing.

Mao Zedong put the intuition bluntly: “No investigation, no right to speak.” Lu You’s verse offers another version: “What one learns on paper will always feel shallow; to understand a thing fully, one must put it into practice.” E2C also touches on longstanding questions in epistemology about how experience gives us knowledge and justifies our beliefs. We need not settle those questions here. Within the framework above, strong E2C focuses on condition (4): participation does not guarantee a good comment, but nonparticipation is enough to disqualify one.

Actual usage does not enforce this strict rule consistently. Condition (5) already points to the problem. Someone may never have watched the anime or played the game, yet escape the *yun* label simply by saying what the community expects. A conventional remark from a nonparticipant is often accepted as commentary; firsthand experience suddenly ceases to be a requirement.

The Chinese internet practice called 复读 (*fùdú*, “repeating”) makes the inconsistency easy to see. Once a work becomes a topic, its earliest takes and stock phrases are copied from thread to thread, whether the prevailing judgment is positive or negative. Recent positive examples often involve Nintendo’s first-party games. From *The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild* to the then-popular *Animal Crossing: New Horizons*, every Nintendo release that breaks into the mainstream attracts waves of bandwagon praise, much of it from people who have never played the game. Trend-chasing promotional accounts are an obvious case.

The hostility surrounding *Girls’ Frontline*’s troubled third closed beta supplies a negative example. Many people who never entered the beta cited it as a reason to condemn the game; a wildly disproportionate number even claimed to be “veterans of the third beta.” Yet in both the Nintendo and *Girls’ Frontline* cases, nonparticipants who repeat the accepted line are unlikely to be accused of *yun* behavior. If I had commented under an *Animal Crossing* video on March 25, 2020, “Anyone who doesn’t buy *Animal Crossing* is out of the group,” readers would have understood “the group” as the imagined fandom in-group rather than necessarily a literal chat group. If I had written on Tieba in August 2016, “MICA Team’s third-beta fiasco disgusted me,” that comment, too, would have passed without scrutiny. Both lines were conventional and closely aligned with their communities’ basic beliefs. They are ordinary, tolerated repetitions by nonparticipants—not weak *yun* behavior, but counterexamples to any claim that communities apply strong E2C consistently.

We need not respond by severing the link between experience and standing altogether. Actual practice follows a second, weaker rule. Under **weak E2C**, a comment from someone without firsthand experience can borrow support from somebody else’s experience. The source may be named outright, or the support may travel through several intermediaries; stock repetition often relies on just such an implicit chain. This remains a form of E2C because the chain of support is still supposed to end with someone who participated. It is weaker because the present speaker need not have done so.

Borrowed experience does not automatically give a comment standing. The original participant must be considered reliable within the community; the report passed along must actually support the claim being made; and the claim must remain within the range that the community considers safe to repeat. That last restriction connects weak E2C to condition (5). A familiar line that fits the community’s basic beliefs can travel on borrowed authority. A novel or dissenting claim is far more likely to provoke a demand for direct experience.

This distinction separates ordinary repetition from weak *yun* behavior. The accepted *Animal Crossing* and *Girls’ Frontline* lines satisfy weak E2C in the eyes of their communities, even when nobody asks the speaker to name a source. They therefore fail condition (5) and do not count as *yun* behavior under the ontology above. If a nonparticipant cannot point, explicitly or implicitly, to a reliable chain of experience—or if the claim goes beyond what that chain can support—the exemption collapses and the label returns.

The two rules give the accusation two different meanings. Under the strong-E2C reading, the charge is simply that the person did not participate, so the act has no standing. Under the weak-E2C reading, the charge is more specific: (i) the person has not supplied a reliable source of experience, (ii) the experience invoked does not support the claim, or (iii) the claim violates a basic belief and the person cannot meet the resulting demand for firsthand participation.

I will call accusations grounded in the first rationale charges of **strong *yun* behavior**, and those grounded in the second charges of **weak *yun* behavior**. These phrases name two ways of justifying the same social label, not two sets of acts that can always be distinguished from the outside. A single act may attract both explanations. Ordinary accepted repetition belongs to neither category as actually applied: its acceptance is precisely what reveals that the community professes the strong rule while often operating by the weak one.

One might object that the weak form is merely an artifact of poor enforcement. If we checked the history behind every comment-like act, the argument goes, all nonparticipants would be exposed and only the strong standard would remain. A perfectly conscientious inspector would audit my entire life after seeing “Anyone who doesn’t buy *Animal Crossing* is out of the group” and call me a *yun* player upon discovering that I had never played it.

But that ideal audit describes a different practice from the one we are trying to understand. Communities decide whom to trust, which claims may circulate without a source, and when to demand proof of participation. Those repeated decisions create the category’s actual boundaries. A community that verified every speaker’s history would sort comments differently; it cannot serve as a supposedly cleaner version of the practice we actually observe. Routine nonverification is not noise that analysis can simply discard. It is one reason the strong and weak standards diverge, and why charges of weak *yun* behavior are so common in real communities.

## III. Conclusion: The Possibilities and Limits of *Yun* Behavior

The split between strong and weak *yun* behavior is not an ontological contradiction. The same five conditions can hold while people give different reasons for denying an act standing. Instead, the split raises an epistemological question about the metacommentary performed by the label: what kind of experience, testimony, or inference gives a person the right to speak?

In Chinese online communities, “*yun* viewer” and “*yun* player” are insults by default, and the acts they describe are dismissed just as readily. Yet if a *yun* viewer is simply someone who has not watched an anime but holds forth about it, almost everyone has been a “*yun* X” at some point. And if *yun* behavior includes all commentary offered without firsthand participation, actual communities reject it far less consistently than they imagine. They accept secondhand claims all the time, then use those claims as premises for further conclusions.

We should therefore ask which rationale we are invoking whenever we condemn *yun* behavior. A weak-form charge may be justified when a claim has no credible experiential source or goes beyond the source from which it borrows. The strong form—a blanket refusal to consider anything said without personal participation—is much harder to sustain. Decades of anime, comics, and games have produced far more works than any one person could experience. Even so, we routinely make reasonable judgments beyond our firsthand knowledge. I have played neither *Muv-Luv Alternative* nor *NEKOPARA Vol. 1*, but their Bangumi ratings give me grounds for thinking that the former is the better visual novel.

That permission is not unlimited. The rough restrictions proposed above—reliable sources, a defensible link between experience and claim, and attention to context—do not yet tell us where the boundary lies. A fuller account would have to engage with the philosophical debates postponed earlier: knowledge by acquaintance, group agency, epistemic externalism, and much else besides. Working through them may help us decide not only what *yun* behavior is, but also when commentary without firsthand experience deserves to be heard.

## Supplementary Notes

1. **Clarification of concepts.** “*Yun* behavior” names a type of behavior; a particular act is an instance of that type. For readability, the main text sometimes speaks of “attributing *yun* behavior to an agent.” More fully, it means “attributing to an agent’s act the property of being *yun* behavior.” The shorthand does not turn *yun* behavior into a permanent trait of the person.

   “*Yun* X” stands for concrete expressions such as “*yun* player” and “*yun* viewer.” In this essay, an agent $S$ is a *yun* X if and only if $S$ performs an act $x$ and $x$ is *yun* behavior. Saying that “$x$ has the property of being *yun* behavior” states the same definition in predicate form.

2. More recently, “*yun* X” has also become a harmless form of self-mockery. After watching someone else finish a game, people may say that they “*yun*-cleared” it—completed it vicariously. This self-deprecating usage ordinarily makes no comment-like judgment and falls outside the phenomenon discussed here. That is why section 1.3 limits its claim to **typical attribution**.

3. Two kinds of contextual disagreement deserve explicit mention. First, the person labeled a “*yun* X” may challenge the accuser’s right to use the term. Rival groups may also recognize different authorities and basic beliefs. The framework describes what allows a community to sustain an accusation; it does not guarantee that the accusation is correct or universally accepted.

   Second, different communities may expect different kinds of participation before accepting the same comment. One may treat watching an adaptation as enough; another may also require playing the original game. In formal terms, for any comment-like act $x$, let $R_x = \{a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots, a_n\}$ contain every activity that counts as relevant in at least one possible context. Let $A_1$ contain the activities treated as relevant to $x$ in context $C_1$, and $A_2$ those treated as relevant in context $C_2$. Then $A_1 \subseteq R_x$ and $A_2 \subseteq R_x$. If the contexts apply different standards, $A_1 \neq A_2$. Either set is a proper subset of $R_x$ only if its context excludes at least one potentially relevant activity; that need not be true in every case.

[^2]: Whether *yun* behavior requires a public context—or whether descriptions such as “*yun* viewer” and “*yun* player” must always be attributed by another person—may be disputed in light of recent usage. See Supplementary Note 2.

[^3]: Another important limiting case occurs when the person being labeled challenges the person applying the label. See Supplementary Note 3.
