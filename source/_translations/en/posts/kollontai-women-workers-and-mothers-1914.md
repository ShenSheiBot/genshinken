*Textual basis of this draft:* This selection from Kollontai’s *Работница-мать* (*Worker-Mother*, anthologized as “Working Woman and Mother”) follows Lab on Roof’s selection and arrangement. The authorial prose is newly translated from the [University of Toronto / Robarts Library Russian witness bearing a 1912 imprint](https://archive.org/details/rabotnitsamat00koll), not a verified copytext of the 1914 edition. The title retains Roof’s historical date label. A brief variant absent from this Russian witness is separately identified below.

## Mashenka, the Factory Director’s Wife

Mashenka is pregnant. In the household of the factory director himself, celebration mingles with concern. And no wonder: Mashenka is about to present her husband with an heir. There will be someone to inherit the wealth created by the hands of women and men workers… The doctor has ordered them to take great care of Mashenka. She must not tire herself or lift anything heavy. Let her eat whatever she likes. Fruit? Give her fruit. Fresh caviar? Bring her caviar.

Above all, Mashenka must have no worries, nothing to upset her. Then the child will be born strong and healthy, the birth will be easier, and Mashenka will preserve her own health. So they say in the factory director’s family. This is how pregnant women are treated in families whose purses are stuffed with banknotes and gold. And they do take care of Mashenka, the lady of the house.

“Don’t tire yourself, Mashenka.” “Mashenka, don’t move that armchair,” say the people around her.

“A pregnant woman, a mother, is sacred to us,” the sanctimonious hypocrites and Pharisees of the bourgeois camp assure us. But is that really so?

## Mashenka the Laundress

In the same building as the factory director’s wife, off the third courtyard, another Masha huddles in a corner behind a printed cotton curtain—a laundress, a day labourer. Masha the laundress is heavy with child, in her eighth month. But how her eyes would widen, how astonished she would be, if someone said: “Mashenka, you must not carry heavy loads. You must take care of yourself, for your own sake, for your child’s sake, for humanity’s sake. You are pregnant, which means that in society’s eyes you are sacred.” Masha would think the speaker mad, or playing a cruel joke. A working-class woman “sacred” when she is pregnant? Who ever heard of such a thing? Do not Mashenka the laundress and hundreds of thousands of other propertyless women, forced to sell the labour of their hands to factory owners, industrialists and employers, learn this for themselves? It is precisely when employers see that poverty has closed in, that there is no way out, that the women go out to earn their bread even when they can barely summon the strength—that is when they skin them twice over…

“What matters most for a pregnant woman is undisturbed sleep, good food, clean air and moderate exercise” (physical movement), the doctors teach. Once again Masha the laundress, together with hundreds of thousands of women wage workers, slaves of capital, would laugh in the speaker’s face. Moderate exercise? Clean air? Plenty of wholesome food? Undisturbed sleep? What working-class woman knows these blessings, available only to the ladies called Mashenka, only to the wives of the factory-owning gentlemen?

Early in the morning, while the darkness of night still struggles with the dawn and Mashenka the lady still dreams sweet dreams, Mashenka the laundress rises from her narrow bed and goes to the damp, dark washhouse. Her feet slip on the wet floor, yesterday’s puddles have not yet dried, and the musty, rotten smell of dirty washing strikes her in the face… It is not of her own free will that Masha trudges to the washhouse she loathes: behind her stands a relentless taskmaster—poverty. *(Chinese translator’s note: “Masha is an affectionate form of Mashenka.” English translator’s clarification: Masha is a familiar form of [Maria](https://gramota.ru/poisk?dicts%5B%5D=28&mode=slovari&query=%D0%BC%D0%B0%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%8F); Mashenka is its affectionate diminutive.)* Masha’s husband is a worker. His wages are small; they cannot feed two people. So Masha stands silently at the washtub, teeth clenched, until the very last day before she gives birth… Do not imagine that Masha the laundress has an “iron constitution,” as ladies like to say of women workers. Standing so long at the tub has covered her legs with swollen veins; her steps have grown slow and heavy… Bags have formed beneath her eyes, her hands are swollen, and at night—it is a long time since Masha the laundress has had any real sleep…

How often, carrying heavy baskets of wet washing, Masha the laundress leans against the wall to keep from falling: her head spins, her sight goes dark… How often she feels as though an enormous aching tooth were throbbing in her back, while she can scarcely move her legs, as though they were filled with lead… If only she could lie down for an hour, catch her breath… But is a wage worker “allowed” that? Such “pampering”! She is no lady! And pregnant Masha the laundress silently endures her life of hard labour. Only that pregnant woman is “sacred” who does not have the insatiable taskmaster—poverty—standing behind her.

## Childbirth

For Mashenka the lady, childbirth is an event. Celebration or not, the whole house is in an uproar. Doctors, midwives, nurses… The woman giving birth lies in a clean, soft bed. Flowers stand on the table. Her husband kisses her hand; postmen bring letters and telegrams. A priest holds a service of thanksgiving. The child is born healthy and strong. No wonder! How carefully they protected Mashenka the lady, how they cherished her!

Mashenka the laundress is giving birth too. In a corner, behind a printed cotton curtain, in a room crammed with strangers. Mashenka is suffering. She tries to muffle her groans with a pillow. Her neighbours are all working people; it would be wrong to rob them of their sleep, to take away their last chance of rest. Towards morning a birth attendant arrives. She washes and tends to the infant, then hurries off to another woman in labour. Mashenka lies there, now alone in the room. She looks at her little child. What a puny creature! So thin, so wrinkled… His eyes seem to reproach his mother, asking sadly, “Why did you bring me into the world?” Mashenka looks at him and weeps herself, quietly, without a sound…

## The Cross of Motherhood

For Mashenka the lady, motherhood is a joy and a celebration. The factory owner’s heir grows up in a bright, tidy nursery, watched over by trained nannies and supervised by a doctor. If Mashenka herself has too little milk, or does not wish to spoil her “figure,” a wet nurse will be found. Mashenka the lady amuses herself with the infant, then goes out visiting, shopping, to theatres and balls… There is someone to look after the baby… For Mashenka the lady, motherhood is a celebration, an amusement.

For the Mashenkas who work—the dyers, weavers, laundresses, rubber workers—for hundreds of thousands of working-class mothers, motherhood is a cross to bear. The factory whistle sounds, calling them to work. But the baby is crying, crying his heart out. How can she leave him? Whom can she entrust him to? The working mother puts some milk in a feeding bottle and leaves the child with an elderly neighbour or her own little daughter. She goes to work, but worry over the baby gnaws at her heart, gnaws and gnaws… His little sister, out of kindness and ignorance, will feed him porridge and push bread into his mouth.

Mashenka the lady’s baby grows lovelier every day. White as sugar, rosy as an apple, and sturdy. The factory worker’s, laundress’s, craftswoman’s child grows thinner every day. At night he kicks his legs, writhes and cries. The doctor comes and scolds: “Why didn’t you breastfeed him? Why did you take it into your heads to feed him all that rubbish? And you call yourselves mothers! Now you have only yourselves to blame if the child dies.” Hundreds of thousands of working mothers offer no defence. They stand with bowed heads, secretly wiping away their tears.

## How Could Things Be?

Let us imagine a society, a people, a state where there are no more ladies called Mashenka, and no laundresses called Mashenka either. There are no parasites, but there are no wage workers either. Everyone works, all alike, and in return society and the state care for them and make their lives easier. Just as the relatives of the wealthy Mashenkas care for them today, this society, like a large, close-knit family, will care for its weaker members: women and children. When Mashenka—neither a lady nor a woman worker, but simply a citizen—becomes pregnant, she will not have to fear what will happen to her or her child. Society, one large, close-knit family, will take care of everything.

A home surrounded by a garden and flowers will be at Mashenka’s disposal, a home arranged so that every pregnant woman, every woman giving birth, every nursing mother can live happily, healthily and comfortably… In this great family of society, the doctors’ concern will be: how can they not only preserve the health of mother and infant, but also ease the woman’s pains in childbirth? Science strides forward; here too science will help. Once the infant is stronger, the mother will return home, to her ordinary life, to take her share of work once more for the good of the great family that is society. But she will not have to suffer with worry over her baby. Here too society will come to her aid: children will grow up in kindergartens, children’s colonies,[†](#childrens-colonies) crèches and schools, looked after by experienced nannies. Whenever the mother wishes, she can have her children with her. When she has no time, she knows her child is in safe hands… Motherhood will no longer be a cross to bear. For every woman there will remain only the joy, only the great happiness of motherhood that today belongs exclusively to the ladies called Mashenka.

But is such a society not a fairy tale? Can it exist? The scientific study of peoples’ economies and of the history of society and the state shows that such a society must come into being, and will. However much the rich capitalists, factory owners, landowners and property owners resist, the “fairy tale” will come true and become reality. For that “reality” the working class is already fighting throughout the world. And although society is still far from becoming one close-knit family, although much struggle and sacrifice lie ahead, it remains true that workers in other countries have already achieved a great deal. Working men and women are also seeking, through laws and all manner of measures, to lighten the cross of motherhood for the woman worker.

## What Must Every Woman Worker Do?

How are all these demands to be won?[^kollontai-mothers-1] What must be done? Every working-class woman, every woman who reads this little book, must not remain indifferently on the sidelines, but support the working-class movement, which fights for all these demands and is wresting from the old world a new and better future—a future without the bitter tears of mothers, where the cross of motherhood becomes a woman’s greatest joy and pride. We need only tell ourselves: “There is strength in unity.” The more of us women workers enter the working-class movement, the greater our strength will be, and the sooner we shall win what we seek… At stake are our happiness and the lives and future of our children!

**English translator’s note (†):** “Children’s colonies” was a historical designation for supervised collective provision for children, often involving country or holiday stays for their health.
