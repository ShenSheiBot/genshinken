![A scene from the PLANETS critics’ roundtable on Kamen Rider BLACK SUN](/attachments/roof-archive/cv19777785/01-planets-black-sun-roundtable.png)

Uno: Generally speaking, *Ultraman* has more political allegory because the disasters it faces are more visible and larger in scale, and have to be dealt with politically. *Kamen Rider*, by comparison, is relatively apolitical: for many productions, getting some good-looking action performers to do a few fight scenes is enough to make them entertaining. Yet there is a strange historical necessity to bringing out a work like *BLACK SUN*, made for adult audiences, at this particular moment in 2022.

Between 1972 and 2022, Japan had plenty of other major news—the bursting of the economic bubble, Aum Shinrikyo, the great earthquake of March 11, 2011… But *Kamen Rider BLACK SUN* sets all those headline events aside, making its story’s timeline coincide with the history of the Japanese New Left’s defeat. That is impressive: it is saying that the essence of those fifty years is nothing other than the rout of the (New) Left (both in Japan and worldwide). But here’s the darkly funny thing: in Japan as it actually is, those with the power to change society are not the real-world Left, but “invincible people”/Xianzhong[^1] (“invincible people” is an internet expression coined by the founder of 2ch: having already lost everything, they have nothing left to lose, and therefore no attachments and no restraints—for example, Tetsuya Yamagami, who ambushed Shinzo Abe).

![Kamen Rider BLACK SUN poster featuring the two leads](/attachments/roof-archive/cv19777785/02-kamen-rider-black-sun-poster.jpg)

Uno: As a story that radically rewrites the history of Japan’s left-wing movements, the series actually makes very good use of the power of fantasy in *Kamen Rider*. Otherwise, how could they fight? They would have no chance of winning. The Japanese online right-wingers who fly into a rage at its allusions to Japan’s militarist past, and the people who just want a quiet life and angrily give it bad ratings because they don’t want politics in tokusatsu/*Kamen Rider*[^2], are simply idiots (baka). They lack even the most basic knowledge and cultural understanding. No need to pay them any attention. The irony, though, is that the series’ treatment of this issue—especially the ending after BLACK SUN’s death (just repeating that the struggle must continue?)—still lags behind Japanese reality (so are “invincible people”/Xianzhong the current meta?). Japan’s mainstream Left today consists of so-called liberal champagne socialists [to put it in terms of the series, some of the humans who side with the kaijin]. They like to say and do left-wing things to win approval and recognition online, but it does fuck-all. Yet these people occupy the same niche as the New Left fifty years ago—and the same goes the other way round. Only the wording has changed. So it’ll take some new moves, some new thinking (but what, exactly?), to get beyond the status quo.

It’s pretty damn impressive, and genuinely a good thing, that this series got made at this moment. Kazuya Shiraishi, an established director in Japanese cinema, took it on and, despite all the constraints, managed to tell the story he wanted to tell, expanding what the tokusatsu superhero genre can portray. What *BLACK SUN* has achieved will also let future tokusatsu productions do more. By the phrase I wrote down, “grasshopper man/kaijin in an intermediate form” [the unfinished form before transformation],[^3] I meant that the series still leaves something to be desired. The director could have pursued his own ideas more boldly—forget about Toei, and don’t worry about the pressure that comes with the existing *Kamen Rider* franchise and its fans. The series could have been better still. (He gave an example involving the two-stage transformation. It sounded as though he was saying that if Shiraishi had worked more independently, doing the transformation in one go rather than in two stages would have suited his approach better. I didn’t fully understand this part.)

[^1]: **English translator’s note:** “Invincible people” translates the Japanese *muteki no hito*. *Xianzhong*, derived from the name of the historical rebel Zhang Xianzhong, is Chinese internet slang associated with indiscriminate retaliatory violence. Their pairing here belongs to the Chinese adaptation; they are not synonymous terms. See [*Imidas* on *muteki no hito*](https://imidas.jp/ryuko/detail/N-101-2174.html) and [Chi Zhang’s study of the Xianzhong meme](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/18681026251364391).

[^2]: **English translator’s note:** *Tokusatsu* here names the Japanese live-action special-effects tradition, especially superhero and monster productions; see the [*Encyclopedia of Science Fiction*](https://sf-encyclopedia.com/entry/tokusatsu). *Kaijin* are the series’ humanoid creatures, not necessarily villains; the [BBFC’s series description](https://www.bbfc.co.uk/release/kamen-rider-black-sun-season-1-q29sbgvjdglvbjpwwc0xmdi2mze2) also uses the term. “The current meta” borrows gaming language for what is most effective under present conditions.

[^3]: **English translator’s note:** The bracketed gloss is the Chinese translator’s. In the series’ official terminology, the grasshopper-kaijin form is already a first-stage transformation; BLACK SUN is the second stage. See [Toei’s BLACK SUN entry](https://www.kamen-rider-official.com/zukan/forms/952).
