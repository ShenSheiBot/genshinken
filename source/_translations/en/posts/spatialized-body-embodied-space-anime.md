This essay won a prize in Rags Drum’s 2021 Eve Festival.

![A formation of giant robots in space](/attachments/roof-archive/cv14124702/figure-1.png)

> **Abstract:** This essay examines two recurring forms in anime: the spatialized body and embodied space. The first is a superhuman body—above all, the giant robot—that gives an individual a boundary, an interior, and a position in the world. The second is a social world imagined as a body or living system, as in *Cells at Work!* or Studio Ghibli’s animistic spaces. These forms have long coexisted in anime, carrying different and sometimes conflicting ideas about individuals and communities. They do not map neatly onto a single opposition. But they do help us see why anime so often sets extraordinary ideals against ordinary life, or abstract universality against concrete experience.

## I. To Pilot a Robot Is to Produce a Subject: The Spatialized Body

Let us begin with the “spatialized body.” In Italo Calvino’s *The Nonexistent Knight*, Agilulf is nothing but an empty suit of armor. Manga and anime offer kindred figures: Alphonse Elric in *Fullmetal Alchemist*, for instance, or Brook in *One Piece*. Flesh, with all its troublesome material qualities, has fallen away. What remains is a body in its most immediately legible form: a suit of armor, a skeleton. Yet a spirit or soul lives inside that material shell and directs its actions without being reducible to matter itself.

An empty shell driven from within by spirit or will: the image also describes the superhuman bodies so common in anime, giant robots above all.

As an extension of its pilot’s body, the giant robot is first of all a shell. It is not merely physical armor. It draws a conceptual line around the pilot, marking where this body ends and every other body begins. The robot therefore makes difference visible. It gives particularity a silhouette.

Robot-war stories make a point of distinguishing mass-produced models from customized and one-off machines. The latter appear in astonishing variety. Almost every machine introduced in the many *Gundam* side stories seems to be a special model—no wonder fans joke that the Earth Federation’s maintenance division must be hell. Mechanical designers often tailor a machine to its pilot, so these differences can express characterization. But a special machine is not automatically more vivid than a mass-produced one. What matters is the contrast itself: particular against general, “my machine” against everybody else’s. The difference assigned to the machine helps establish the pilot as a distinct individual.

Consider the Scopedog piloted by Chirico Cuvie, the protagonist of *Armored Trooper VOTOMS*. It is a plain, mass-produced machine, repeatedly battered and replaced. That very ordinariness comes to express Chirico’s tenacity: he survives every setback without needing a gaudy custom model. Its lack of distinction becomes distinctive. The joke reaches its purest form in Kou Uraki’s famous description of the GM Custom: its defining feature is that it has no defining feature.

Such boundaries give the individual a stable outline; the pilot is no longer merely one person in a crowd. But the shell does something else as well. By separating an inside from an outside, it produces an “inner subject.” Turn the opening image around: an entirely material shell is paired with an intangible, invisible soul. The result resembles mind-body dualism made visible. The machine takes over the work of the flesh-and-blood body. Across robot anime, machines suppress crowds, carry out repairs, and fight fires. The robot becomes a metonym for the human being as an acting, working body.

The pilot inside the machine can then appear to be pure will, issuing commands to a body that acts on the world. Classic robot anime repeatedly stage this split: the machines fight outside while the pilots argue inside their cockpits. At its limit, the pattern becomes the Newtype encounter in *Gundam*, when psychically attuned people meet almost as disembodied minds. Piloting a robot divides thought from the external world, leaving an active mechanical body outside and a thinking consciousness enclosed within.

The classic homunculus problem associated with Cartesian dualism imagines a tiny observer behind the eyes, receiving sensory signals and judging experience. It is remarkably close to the anime image of a person piloting a robot. What, then, is the cockpit? Here it is the imagined seat of the inner self, the protagonist’s interior space. *Brain Powerd* makes the implication almost literal by placing its cockpit where a womb would be in a human body. Entering the cockpit produces a subject: it redraws the distinction between self and world, subject and object. The pilot becomes an inward, incorporeal cogito; the robot becomes the outward body through which that cogito fights and acts.

## II. The Spatiality of the Robot Body: Inside and Outside

We can also ask how the giant robot organizes the space around it. For Bernard Stiegler, a technical object is not simply a tool but part of what makes human existence possible. We shape ourselves through sustained contact with technologies, from tools of production to cultural forms such as film and television. A technical object extends human existence by carrying culture, experience, and history beyond any one living body.

Many robot anime fold dense social, historical, and political relations into the manufactured object called the robot. When a pilot enters the cockpit, they enter those relations too. In the original *Mobile Suit Gundam*, Amuro climbs into the RX-78-2 designed by his father. In doing so, he ceases to be a civilian on a neutral space colony and becomes a soldier for the Earth Federation. His long journey aboard the refugee warship White Base carries him toward Earth, but it also draws him into the war and the unfinished business of his parents’ generation. He inherits more than a machine.

A technical object can preserve a way of perceiving space as well. In something like the Cartesian sense of “extension,” space in these works is not merely the abstract three-dimensional grid of physics. It gathers around the superhuman body that occupies it. Machines differ not only because their pilots do, but because they belong to particular terrains and military formations. The tropical approaches to Jaburo, the Earth Federation’s underground headquarters in *Gundam*, are associated with an extravagant assortment of Zeon’s amphibious mobile suits—the group fans nickname the “Four Sacred Beasts of Jaburo.” The Gouf, a close-combat machine introduced in desert warfare, turns that environment into a blunt, physical style of fighting.

The superhuman body, then, is constituted through difference. The contrasts among machines become coordinates for perceiving their world: this pilot and that pilot, this faction and that faction, this terrain and that terrain. Robot anime scatter innumerable superhuman bodies across their battlefields, each defined against the others around it. The body becomes spatial in two directions at once. Inwardly, the shell creates depth by enclosing a subject. Outwardly, it gives that subject a distinct position among many others. This is what I mean by the “spatialized body.”

This imaginative form tends to emphasize individual subjects at the expense of less easily personified forces such as institutions, social groups, and cultures. Robot-war stories may spend dozens of episodes building complicated political formations, only to compress the outcome into one enormous confrontation among machines. In *Gundam*, the Battle of A Baoa Qu serves as the final battle of the One Year War; in *Mobile Suit Zeta Gundam*, the Gryps Conflict likewise culminates in a concentrated, catastrophic showdown. Actual wars do not resolve so neatly. Military outcomes are entangled with political, economic, and social pressures that no duel between machines can contain.

That compression is precisely what makes these stories distinctive. They may depict whole political systems, societies, and cultures, yet ultimately fold those entanglements into powerful superhuman bodies. Everything ends in a struggle between them.

Combat between superhuman bodies is therefore not war in the ordinary sense, or not only war. To understand what else it is doing, we must turn to the other imaginative form: embodied space.

## III. Embodied Space: Living Space, or the Anti-Spatialized Body

Works such as *Cells at Work!* and *Howl’s Moving Castle* offer a different form: “embodied space.” In *Cells at Work!*, the conceit is literal. The characters inhabit a human body, and its regions correspond to organs and biological systems. *Howl’s Moving Castle* is not anatomically mapped in this way, but its wandering house still behaves like a living whole: its rooms, magical hearth, inhabitants, and shifting entrances depend on one another. In both cases, space is relatively self-contained and organized as a system. Its regions have distinct functions but remain connected, and those connections often produce hierarchy. In the bathhouse of *Spirited Away*, for example, workers labor in the lower levels while the proprietor, Yubaba, rules from her lavish rooms above. Physical elevation becomes social rank.

The point is not to assign every room an anatomical equivalent. It is that the parts are organically connected, like organs in a body. Embodied space is relational space. It emphasizes not an isolated individual but the ties among people whose work and lives sustain a common world. Where the spatialized body anchors space in a particular superhuman figure, embodied space locates it in the network of relations among characters. Those relations are often explicitly social. When a red blood cell wanders through the body in *Cells at Work!*, she also wanders through a landscape of workplaces and institutions.

Because they stress sociality, works of this kind tend to give unusual weight to jobs, routines, and everyday life. This does not mean that the spatialized body is free of social relations—Amuro enters the Gundam only to become entangled in them. The difference is one of emphasis. The robot concentrates those relations in the acting subject; embodied space makes the relations themselves visible. The original *The Super Dimension Fortress Macross*, a major hit from the closing years of the Shōwa era whose influence still reverberates, brings both forms together.

The VF-1 Valkyrie, a transforming fighter piloted by Hikaru Ichijo, is a spatialized body: his mechanical body in combat. The far larger SDF-1 Macross is ordinarily an inhabited ship, a mobile city carrying civilians through space. It is embodied space. Yet the ship can also rearrange itself into the form of a giant robot when it goes into battle. The series gives as much attention to daily life aboard this city-ship as to the war against the Zentradi. Alien infiltrators encounter Earth culture; Hikaru, the singer Lynn Minmay, and officer Misa Hayase move through an endlessly tangled love triangle. Such everyday relations are not incidental to the SDF-1. They are its substance.

More strikingly, the protagonists do not end the war simply by destroying enough enemy craft. “Culture,” represented by Minmay’s singing, breaks the Zentradi’s devotion to war. The series also makes the passage between its two spatial forms physical. While rescuing Minmay, Hikaru damages his VF-1A and enters a sealed, rearranged section of the transformed SDF-1. Stranded alone inside the ship’s vast interior, the two begin their romance. Hikaru leaves the spatialized body of his fighter and enters the embodied space of the city-ship, moving from solitary combat into a shared life.

The Zentradi have been denied culture and reduced to instruments of war. The human protagonists, by contrast, carry a whole society with them aboard the SDF-1. The arrangement seems to set the spatialized body against embodied space: abstract destructive force against settled communal life. In *Event*, Slavoj Žižek loosely paraphrases Heinrich Heine with the phrase “freedom, equality, and crab soup.” Crab soup stands for the ordinary pleasures without which ideals such as freedom and equality become empty impositions, indifferent to the people in whose name they are proclaimed. *Macross* initially seems to let crab soup defeat abstraction. Minmay’s singing is the antidote to the Zentradi’s war fever. Give up abstract ideals, return to everyday pleasures, and conflict will melt away.

But is that really all the series is doing?

## IV. Spatialized Bodies and Embodied Spaces: Borders, Violence, and Law

Even as *Macross* advances that proposition, it gives us reason to doubt it. Return to the transforming ship. Outside, the SDF-1 rearranges its body so that it can fight the Zentradi. Inside, the same violent rearrangement brings people into new proximity and changes their relationships. The television series repeats the device, most notably when another transformation gives Hikaru and Misa time together. Extraordinary combat does not merely threaten everyday life here. By reshaping the ship’s interior, it creates occasions for intimacy.

Everyday life may therefore rest on extraordinary force rather than simply opposing it. *Patlabor 2: The Movie* similarly places metropolitan prosperity alongside a ghostly military confrontation, exposing the violence that the peaceful city keeps just beyond ordinary sight. The comforts of life in wealthy centers can likewise depend on extractive labor and coercion pushed onto distant regions. We can imagine people calmly pursuing their routines inside a giant body while the body that shelters them remains locked in ceaseless combat.

The combat is the same kind of struggle waged by the giant robots discussed earlier; what changes is where the story locates it. In one arrangement, the world has shattered into competing bodies and nowhere offers stable shelter. In the other, the story draws a border around a community, places ordinary life inside, and moves conflict to the edge. That border is not neutral. It decides who belongs to the protected everyday world, who remains outside it, and what force may be used in the name of preserving it. This is the bridge from spatial form to law’s violence: these battles do not concern statutes in any literal sense, but they dramatize the force that establishes an order, draws its boundaries, and defends them.

Walter Benjamin’s “Critique of Violence” distinguishes lawmaking violence from law-preserving violence: force founds an order, then returns to preserve it. Robot anime often stage their final battles as clashes of ideas because the machines carry political projects as well as weapons. The Battle of A Baoa Qu, for example, is not itself a constitutional convention. Yet as the last great confrontation of the One Year War, it determines whether Zeon’s attempt to overturn the Earth Federation’s order can survive. The duel condenses a much larger political struggle; victory grants one side the power to define the peace that follows. In this limited but important sense, the final battle becomes a struggle over who may found a new order and whose order will be preserved.

Think of the Turn A Gundam, a world-destroying machine concealed inside a sacred statue until history calls it forth again. Or consider the conclusion of *Linebarrels of Iron*, where the protagonist merges with his machine and becomes the world’s hidden guardian—both a source of authority and the force that protects it. The giant robot makes human agency appear more than human. Spatialization helps explain why. The machine replaces the vulnerable human body as the thing that acts, stripping action of hunger, fatigue, pain, and all the compromises of lived experience. The pilot can then appear as pure intention inside the cockpit, while the mechanical shell imposes that intention on the world. This is the robot’s aura of sublimity, even divinity: an idea seems to act without getting its hands dirty, although the machine outside is doing violence on its behalf.

For the law-preserving side of embodied space, consider *Cells at Work! CODE BLACK*. The cells inhabit a body in severely compromised health. Cast as exploited workers, they must labor constantly to keep it alive, pushing themselves toward death from overwork. At the literal level of the story, the causal sequence is clear: the host body’s condition creates the cells’ terrible working conditions. The cells cannot reach the host’s social environment or change the habits that have damaged his health.

The allegory works differently. The diseased body contains a miniature workplace, while the host’s unseen world contains the social conditions—overwork, stress, and neglect—that have helped make the body sick. The cells’ overtime does not literally cause the host’s illness. Rather, the manga projects a destructive labor culture into the body and makes its consequences appear to the cells as an external biological emergency. The displacement matters. Because the workers can neither see nor alter the system producing the crisis, “work harder” appears to be their only possible response. An emergency at the border of their communal world dictates the rules within it. At the climax of the manga’s first chapter, the protagonist does not come to believe those rules are just; he accepts that the crisis leaves him nothing else to do. Embodied space here naturalizes an existing order by making its causes inaccessible to the people who must preserve it.

This pattern recurs across decades: Earth’s unified government faces the Zentradi in *Macross*; the Earth Federation faces the Principality of Zeon in *Gundam*; Ultraman defends ordinary human settlements from kaiju. These are not identical political conflicts, but they share a spatial arrangement. A protected social world occupies one side, while a superhuman or monstrous body arrives from beyond its border as the agent of destruction or change. In this essay’s terms, embodied space tends to align with everyday life under an established order, while the spatialized body tends to align with the extraordinary force that threatens to remake it.

Think again of the first episode of *Mobile Suit Gundam*, in which Zeon mobile suits invade and shatter the inhabited space of a colony. *Mobile Suit Gundam 0080: War in the Pocket* carries this logic into a child’s small world. Al watches two pilots—Bernie in a Zeon Zaku and Chris in a Federation Gundam—fight a mortal battle without knowing each other’s identities or their connections to him. Their machines produce a brief, terrible sublime before his eyes while destroying the ordinary life he hoped war would enliven. In that sense, one might even call *0080* *sekaikei*, a mode in which intimate personal drama connects directly to the fate of the world. Across these works, everyday routines are more often shown on the Earth-aligned side, while Zeon appears through grand political rituals such as Garma’s state funeral.

The Principality of Zeon remains uncanny because it scrambles the moral alignment. It emerges from the space colonies’ anticolonial struggle against rule from Earth, yet its answer is often to reverse the hierarchy and place Spacenoids—people born in space—above Earthnoids. The order Zeon seeks to establish through war begins to resemble the order it claims to overthrow.

This is why identifying with either side is not enough. The spatialized body and embodied space are not moral camps, and the conceptual axes developed here do not perfectly coincide. Spatialized bodies tend to privilege the individual, the extraordinary, and lawmaking force; embodied spaces tend to privilege community, everyday life, and law-preserving force. But *Macross* has already shown that one object can become both, while *CODE BLACK* shows that communal life can be brutally abstract in its own way. The two forms can therefore coexist within the same political imagination. One turns an idea into a purified force capable of remaking the world. The other treats the existing world as an organic whole whose rules must be preserved. Together they offer a cramped choice: an “abstract universality” that sacrifices or excludes concrete lives, or a concrete everyday life deprived of any universal claim and any power to transform itself.

Here the mind-body or subject-object dualism from the first section reaches its most disastrous form. The desire to change the world becomes pure thought with no ground beneath its feet, detached from practice and hostile to inconvenient circumstances: spirit without flesh. Concrete life, meanwhile, retreats into cynicism, rejecting every larger claim because so many such claims have proved empty: flesh without spirit. Once the opposition is posed this way, transformation and ordinary life can only betray each other.

But that opposition need not be the final word. What I have called “concrete universality” is not a bodiless idea imposed from above, nor is it a name for everyday life exactly as it already exists. It is a universal claim that arises from a particular relation, injury, or need and remains answerable to the lives from which it grew. The point is not to abolish extraordinary action, but to refuse the fantasy that action becomes purer as it sheds its concrete body.

*Macross* offers only a partial image of this alternative, but it is enough to clarify the direction of the argument. Minmay’s song matters not because “culture” floats above the war as a superior abstraction. It matters because a specific practice from the ship’s everyday life—one voice, one audience, one shared pleasure—crosses the border that had separated human community from Zentradi enemy. At the same time, the SDF-1’s transformations show that ordinary life is not an untouched refuge from violence; intimacy develops inside a structure built for combat. Neither embodied space nor the spatialized body supplies the answer on its own. The possibility lies in the passage between them: when a concrete life generates a claim that reaches beyond its existing community, and when transformative force remains accountable to the bodies and relationships it would change.

This essay cannot point to a world in which that reconciliation has already been achieved. Its more modest conclusion is a criterion for reading these imagined worlds. We should look neither for a perfect community sealed against conflict nor for a sublime machine that will impose justice from outside. We should look within ordinary life for the moments that make a larger claim possible—and ask whether the power released by those moments preserves their concrete source or abandons it. That is where a world-changing idea might acquire a body, and where a body might cease to be only a border.
