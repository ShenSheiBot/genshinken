## 0. Why Basara Sings at the Enemy

Even in a franchise as cheerfully extravagant as *Macross*, *Macross 7* (hereafter *M7*) is a glorious oddity. A newcomer whose first encounter with it is the theatrical compilation *Macross FB7: Listen to My Song!* may reasonably wonder what on earth they have just watched. The film's cheap, choppy construction accounts for some of the confusion. Much of it comes straight from *M7*.

Music repeatedly crosses into warfare in *Macross*, but each series makes that relationship intelligible in its own way. In *The Super Dimension Fortress Macross*, Lynn Minmay's songs overwhelm the Zentradi because they are encountering human culture for the first time. *Macross Delta* introduces Freyja Wion and Walküre early on as performers with an organized tactical role.

![Freyja Wion smiling in the glow of a sunset](attachments/roof-archive/cv3057441/02-macross7-scene.jpg)

Freyja Wion in *Macross Delta*, where the performers' tactical role is part of the premise from the start.

In *Macross Frontier*, the plot gradually connects Sheryl Nome and Ranka Lee's voices to their effects on the Vajra. However fanciful the premise, the story eventually tells us how the effect works within its world.

![Sheryl Nome and Ranka Lee performing against a field of stars](attachments/roof-archive/cv3057441/03-fire-bomber.jpg)

Sheryl Nome and Ranka Lee in *Macross Frontier*, another entry that makes the singers' effect on an alien species part of its plot.

Basara Nekki presents a different problem. In the first episode of *M7*, a man in tinted glasses and a tank top flies a Valkyrie into battle and belts out “Totsugeki Love Heart.” Neither side knows what to make of him. At this point, almost nobody believes that music can affect the Protodeviln at all.

The series does not leave that practical question unanswered. By its second half, Basara's allies have observed what song can do, organized Sound Force, and begun using music as part of their defense. What remains elusive is Basara's own reason for taking his songs into combat. His desire is plain enough: he wants everyone to listen, enemies included. But he never turns that desire into a neat origin story, and he refuses the military's conclusion that a song whose effects are useful must therefore be a weapon. The plot's explanation of what singing does is not an explanation of why Basara sings.

What endures is the spectacle of Basara saving the galaxy by refusing to stop. The *Super Robot Wars* crossover strategy games turn the conceit into an exceptionally powerful support ability: he can transform a battle by singing rather than attacking. In *M7* itself, though, his grandest claims are addressed as readily to mountains and galaxies as to soldiers: “Mountain! Galaxy! Listen to my song!”

A work this divisive, ambiguous, and fiercely loved might seem destined to support an industry of exegesis. It can certainly bear the weight. Yet in the informal Chinese-language posts and conversations I happened to encounter while developing this essay—not a survey, and not a representative sample—people were much more likely to celebrate the songs, the characters, and the rush of the finale than to hunt for a hidden key. That contrast is what interests me here.

My title echoes Susan Sontag's “Against Interpretation,” but I am not proposing a ban on interpretation. Interpretation remains one rewarding way to understand art. My target is narrower: the demand for depth, the assumption that an artwork's real value must lie beneath its immediate effects and that criticism earns its keep by digging that value out.

The distinctions matter. Asking how song affects the Protodeviln is a question about fictional cause and effect. Asking what Basara wants, or why he became the person he is, is a question about character. Asking what his singing stands for is an act of interpretation. *M7* eventually answers enough of the first question to carry its plot. It makes Basara's immediate desire unmistakable while leaving its origins largely unstated. And it remains open to the third kind of inquiry without making that inquiry the price of emotional admission. The point is not that stories can dispense with causality or character. It is that an explanation, a motive, and a hidden meaning are different things—and none automatically accounts for the hold a work has on us.

## 1. How Interpretation Produces Depth

Anime fandom abounds in works that seem to ask for decoding. Consider the final scene of *The End of Evangelion*, in which Shinji tries to strangle Asuka. Fans have proposed reading after reading of that moment, along with “complete guides” to *Evangelion*'s world, inventories of its religious symbols, and histories connecting the series to otaku culture. Or consider *Serial Experiments Lain*, whose broken chronology, recurring images, and unstable realities invite viewers to reconstruct the story and name its themes. My own response to *Lain* runs almost the other way: the more fractured its narration becomes, the less useful it feels to force the series into a single decipherable account. That response, of course, is already an interpretation.

![Key art for Serial Experiments Lain, with Lain listening through earphones](attachments/roof-archive/cv3057441/05-kemono-friends.jpg)

The fractured realities of *Serial Experiments Lain* make reconstruction tempting even when they frustrate any final solution.

The classic interpretive move is simple: what looks like A is really B, or A stands for B. A cross-shaped flash after an Angel's destruction is not merely an explosion but a Christian symbol. On the surface, “The Seven Days of Fire,” an episode of the *Patlabor* original video animation series, is a comic dispute among rival groups of maintenance workers. One Chinese-language reading places their factionalism beside conflicts within Japan's postwar left, then turns that political parallel into a comment on labor in the anime industry.[^macross-2-1] In each case, interpretation directs attention beyond what is immediately present to another subject, structure, or history that promises to reveal what the work is “really” about. Depth waits at the end of the journey.

![Two fighter aircraft crossing above a cloud layer](attachments/roof-archive/cv3057441/04-interpretation.jpg)

A familiar combat image can remain spectacle, help reconstruct a fictional world, or become a sign of something beyond the plot.

That journey can take several forms. Symbols and metaphors offer the most obvious case: the critic matches image to meaning like a codebreaker. A fractured plot demands another kind of work. The viewer assembles clues, notices relationships, and reconstructs a story the series never states outright. A shift in tone can trigger the same impulse. If an image feels conspicuously out of place, viewers may take the mismatch as a promise that another layer is waiting to be found.

The first season of *Kemono Friends* supplied a charming example. Fans matched the abandoned amusement parks in its ending sequence to real parks that had closed, researched the locations, and combed the show for more clues. Some Chinese-speaking fans jokingly called this amateur scholarship “Friends studies.” Location hunting and close comparison changed how they watched: what had first looked like a modest children's show now seemed to contain unexpectedly careful world-building, and even small variations in dialogue began to attract scrutiny.[^macross-2-2]

![Serval turning toward the viewer in the savanna of Kemono Friends](attachments/roof-archive/cv3057441/06-anime-character.jpg)

Serval in *Kemono Friends*, whose bright surface became the object of elaborate fan investigation.

The most ambitious interpretations place a work inside an intellectual or historical frame. They ask what an anime says about violence, identity, technology, or modernity, whether or not its creators consciously planted a thesis. Such readings draw on more than the work itself: the Second World War, the collapse of Japan's asset bubble, or the panic around otaku culture after Tsutomu Miyazaki's arrest. Contemporary media made Miyazaki's collection of manga and videotapes part of its explanation for his crimes, helping turn a serial-killer case into an indictment of a whole subculture. Each framework reveals different relationships among the same scenes.

None of this is inherently suspect. Some creators deliberately build with symbols, mysteries, and competing ideas. Interpretation can itself be creative: a good reading does not merely announce a work's secret but offers an argument that other viewers can test and contest. Several readings may coexist, though that does not make all of them equally persuasive.

And there is almost always something to read. A political or philosophical account of *Cannon Fodder*, the final segment of *Memories*, might be illuminating.

![The regimented, militarized city of Cannon Fodder](attachments/roof-archive/cv3057441/07-anime-scene.jpg)

The city in *Cannon Fodder*, a film whose military order readily invites political interpretation.

With enough ingenuity, however, a critic could perform the same operation on a production failure. *My Sister, My Writer* contains a notoriously off-model face that viewers nicknamed “the sphinx” for its stiff, distorted proportions. Perhaps the image punctures the fantasy of the perfectly designed anime girl. Perhaps the warped anatomy is a deliberate homage to Egyptian art. Perhaps it lays bare the way the anime, manga, and game industries fashion idealized girls for male consumers. These are jokes, but only just. The machinery of interpretation will run on a mistake as readily as on a deliberate symbol. The infamously misshapen cabbage in *Brighter Than the Dawning Blue* offers the same temptation in vegetable form.

Duchamp's *Fountain* helped make it possible to treat almost anything as art. In a similar spirit, contemporary criticism can make almost any anime look deep. That is why we cannot escape the demand for depth by searching for perfectly “transparent” content. Sontag admired art whose content seemed simply to be there rather than waiting to be excavated, and called for an “erotics of art” attentive to sensuous presence. Critics and fans, however, now have ways to find significance in the plainest image, the cheapest genre formula, or a cabbage drawn badly enough to become a meme.

If everything can be interpreted, the alternative cannot be to find the one object that defeats interpretation. We should ask a different question: must every attachment be converted into an account of hidden meaning? That question leads back to *Macross 7*.

## 2. When Interpretation Becomes Optional

In the limited Chinese-language fan conversations and posts I encountered, *M7* inspired few attempts at comprehensive interpretation. That tells us about my sample, not about everything written on the series. Sampling may be the whole explanation. A less charitable possibility is that the show's uneven production discouraged sustained attention: a mistake can always be made to mean something, but most viewers are content to laugh at a bad drawing and move on. Timing may also have mattered. *Neon Genesis Evangelion* began near the end of *M7*'s run and later became an obvious focus for discussions of anime that rewarded decoding. It may have drawn attention away from its contemporaries, though my casual sample cannot establish that history.

What matters to my argument is therefore not a supposed absence of *M7* criticism, but the positive language I kept encountering. “Totsugeki Love Heart,” “Power to the Dream,” and “Try Again” make the pulse quicken. Basara's baffling choices strike a chord even when a viewer cannot say why. The character known only as the Flower Girl spends the series trying and failing to hand Basara a bouquet; when she finally reaches him in the last episode, the delight is immediate. These are ordinary, personal responses. They make no demand that anyone else feel the same way.

Language can describe the shape of a feeling, but it cannot put precisely the same sensation into another person's body. Criticism often bridges that gap by turning attachment into a public argument: a scene matters because it completes a symbol, reveals a hidden structure, or addresses a major theme. Those arguments can be valuable. But a response does not become worthless because it cannot be generalized. *M7* gives us a chance to let attachment remain attachment instead of treating interpretation as its necessary defense.

That does not mean the series lacks depth. The Flower Girl readily lends herself to symbolism. Her repeated failure to reach Basara, followed by her success in the finale, forms a small-scale counterpart to the long struggle for Basara's songs to reach the galaxy. His largely unexplained past invites viewers to reconstruct a hidden biography from scattered hints. The recurring *Macross* fantasy of changing an enemy through song raises larger questions: can art interrupt violence? Can an aesthetic experience cross the boundary between peoples, or even species? Because Basara refuses to call music a weapon even after it acquires strategic value, *M7* can also be read as a defense of art that exceeds its usefulness.

![Basara Nekki lying in the grass with his guitar across his body](attachments/roof-archive/cv3057441/08-macross7-battle.jpg)

Much of Basara's past remains offscreen, but his desire to make the world listen is never obscure.

Symbol, hidden story, grand theme: *M7* supports all three. Yet none quite accounts for the center of its appeal. The parallel between the Flower Girl and Basara can move us before we formulate it as a parallel. An essay on music and violence may begin with close observation or intellectual curiosity, but for an admirer it may also begin with the memory of being carried away by a song. When the entire Macross 7 fleet joins the final chorus of “Totsugeki Love Heart,” the desire to decode the scene does not disappear; for a moment, it simply gives way to the desire to sing along.

The series earns that response through repetition. Again and again, Basara goes where people are fighting and sings at them. His allies first dismiss him, then discover the practical effects of his music, then try to deploy those effects, and finally join his chorus. Their account is causally correct: singing changes the battle. Basara rejects it only as an account of his intention. From his point of view, a listener's military usefulness does not determine what a song is for.

![Basara Nekki standing before his red Valkyrie](attachments/roof-archive/cv3057441/09-macross7.jpg)

Basara and his red Valkyrie: the image joins rock-star bravado to military hardware without making one reducible to the other.

Basara does have a motive. He wants his song to reach everyone, including those shooting at him. What the series withholds is a full psychological explanation for that desire: why he came to want it so fiercely, why battle became his chosen venue, why he trusts music with such unreasonable certainty. Those gaps may invite interpretation, but they are not themselves hidden meanings. Nor does the late confirmation of music's effects unlock Basara as a person. It merely explains why the fleet comes to rely on something he was already doing for reasons of his own.

This is not a defense of incoherence. Fiction needs enough cause and motive for its action to be intelligible. *M7* supplies both, but distributes them strangely. The in-world mechanism carries the battle plot; Basara's repeated insistence makes his immediate desire clear; neither gives us a master key to the feeling produced when everybody joins in. Plausibility and backstory can enrich a story. They are not identical to depth, and they are not the only sources of pleasure.

The troubled reception of 2018's *DARLING in the FRANXX* (*DITF*) shows the other side of the bargain. The series presented genuine mysteries: children raised apart from adult life, a sealed plantation society, cryptic identities, and visual motifs that appeared to promise eventual answers. In the Chinese-language forum discussions I followed during its broadcast, small clues quickly became the basis for large predictions. One circulating joke imagined speculation beginning with an ordinary bicycle and ending with a planet-killing weapon. It was not a plot summary but a picture of interpretive inflation—the absurd distance a theory could travel from a minor cue to a cosmic revelation.

The series supplied the mysteries; viewers enlarged them into promises; the ending supplied a sudden cosmic scale without satisfying every expectation built along the way. Those are three different contributions to the disappointment. Interpretation did not cause the show's narrative problems, and disappointed viewers were not wrong simply because they had interpreted it. But once anticipated depth becomes the main source of excitement, an ending must answer not only for what happens on screen but for the structure of expectation the work and its audience have built together.

![The title card for Strelizia, one of the Franxx mecha](attachments/roof-archive/cv3057441/10-macross7-character.jpg)

Strelizia in *DARLING in the FRANXX*, a series whose suggestive designs became fuel for escalating theories.

Sontag's “transparent” art is not empty art. It is art present enough to be felt before it is decoded. Anime needs room for that kind of encounter—an **erotics of anime**, if you like. Some of us may want beautiful girls and giant robots, or perhaps giant beautiful-girl robots. Others will want something else entirely. The point is not to make one corner of otaku taste universal. It is to stop requiring pleasure to produce a certificate of profundity before criticism will take it seriously. Depth remains one valuable way of judging art. *Macross 7* shows that we can also decline the demand for it.

## 3. What the Audience Brings Along

Once private attachment enters criticism, a harder question follows: where does it come from? Not from the work alone. Every viewer arrives with memories, loyalties, and knowledge already in tow. That is why a Chinese online argument over *Dear Basketball* matters to an essay about *Macross 7*.

When *Dear Basketball* won Best Animated Short Film at the 2018 Academy Awards, some anime fans and basketball fans in two Chinese-language discussion threads disagreed about what the prize should recognize.[^macross-2-3] The skeptical position was not that context-dependent art is illegitimate. It was that an award specifically for an animated short should chiefly reward what a film achieves through animation. From that perspective, Kobe Bryant's fame and the public emotion surrounding his retirement should not outweigh the relative achievements of the nominees as animated films.

The reply begins from a different question: what makes this particular film moving? Bryant wrote and narrated *Dear Basketball* from his retirement letter, and much of its force for basketball fans came from years spent following his career. A viewer without that history could admire the drawing and filmmaking yet feel that other viewers had brought the strongest emotion with them. That does not make their emotion false, and it does not prove the film deserved the award. It only shows that an audience's prior attachment can become part of the experience.

The border between what lies “inside” and “outside” a work is difficult to police. Fire Bomber's songs were written for *M7* and performed within its story, so they seem to belong securely to the anime. Suppose another anime uses a famous preexisting song to brilliant effect. Its choice, timing, and placement are part of the filmmakers' work, while the song also carries memories formed elsewhere. Those associations are no more internal to the anime than a viewer's knowledge of Bryant's career. A work is not a sealed container. It arranges materials and histories that each member of the audience may meet with a different store of knowledge.

*Shin Godzilla* makes the point clearer. In the corner of Chinese-language fandom I encountered, anime and tokusatsu—live-action special-effects drama—had overlapping audiences, so Hideaki Anno's film offered a natural comparison. A response to it may depend on knowledge of Godzilla's long association with nuclear catastrophe and on familiarity with the political and bureaucratic life the film satirizes. One viewer may find its procedural meetings deadening; another may recognize their painful precision.

![Shin Godzilla's black head and dorsal plates against a red field](attachments/roof-archive/cv3057441/11-shin-godzilla.jpg)

The stark silhouette of *Shin Godzilla* arrives already burdened with the monster's cultural history.

Nationality does not fix this difference. Cultural knowledge can be learned, and no national audience responds with one mind. Knowledge does not guarantee attachment either. A critic can explain every allusion in *Shin Godzilla* without making a reader feel its urgency, just as a perfect account of Bryant's career cannot make someone care about basketball. Fandom, cultural literacy, and emotional investment overlap, but they are not the same.

The comparison does not show that the participants in the Oscar dispute were hypocrites, nor does it decide which short should have won. It separates two questions that the dispute brought together. What may legitimately contribute to an encounter with a film? And what should a comparative award chiefly reward? Prior devotion to Bryant can be part of a genuine response to *Dear Basketball* even if someone also believes the animation category should privilege achievement in animation. Cultural knowledge can deepen *Shin Godzilla* without becoming mandatory homework. The answers need not cancel one another.

Perspective matters, not because viewers are trapped inside identities, but because no encounter with art begins from nothing. Criticism can recover context, test interpretations, and help us notice what we missed. It cannot make a response compulsory. No reading, however exact, can reproduce years of following Bryant or the particular thrill of hearing a Fire Bomber song at the right moment in one's life.

To resist the demand for depth is not to choose form over content, reject intellectual criticism, or deny that artworks carry history. It is to stop treating hidden meaning as the only respectable explanation for why art matters. Interpretation grows from evidence, critical frameworks, and personal investment; at its best, it makes those elements available to other people. It need not become an alibi for pleasure.

*Macross 7* makes that limit wonderfully audible. The story eventually explains why the singing changes a battle. It never converts Basara's urge to sing into a military purpose, and it does not need a secret beneath that urge to make the final chorus matter. Before the mechanism, there is a singer shouting at the galaxy. Before the grand theme, there is the ridiculous conviction that the galaxy might listen. Criticism loses nothing by beginning with the person who wants to sing back—and, sometimes, by letting the song be enough.
