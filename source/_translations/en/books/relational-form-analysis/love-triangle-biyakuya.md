## Introduction

As viewers, many of us know the impulse: a film or series ends, so we replay parts of it, open a blank document, and try to put our thoughts about an anime, comic, or game (together, ACG), a film, or a novel into words. Once the introduction is out of the way and we turn to the plot, however, a difficult question arises. How can we explain each character's personality and role convincingly and consistently? Characters are the soul of a work and largely determine its direction. Without reading them closely, we cannot fill the gaps that a story may leave open, and our interpretation loses force.

Critics emphasize different things when they analyze characters in ACG works. One common approach reconstructs a character's **personal history**, looking for clues in lived experience. Another asks what function the character performs within a particular narrative structure. The first is an exercise in **empathy**: it tries to give the character human depth, although the critic's own projections may enter the account. The second treats the character as **a device serving the author's—or the market's—narrative aims**, abstracting away their humanity and assigning them a generalized **database** identity: a bundle of familiar, reusable character traits that can be recognized apart from any one story. Comparing the two approaches, however, can obscure three important points. First, an author created the work; a character remains an imagined Other. Yet fictional growth still speaks to the growth of real people, so the two cannot be completely separated. Second, works differ too much for any single rigid method to suit them all. Third, one aspect of character connects people inside the story with one another and connects those characters with viewers outside it. It draws us into the fictional situation while supplying the background that makes a character intelligible. That aspect—and the subject of this essay—is **relationship**.

To define **relationship**, we must distinguish it from **personal history** and **structure**. A personal history naturally includes events and the intricate relationships within them, but it begins with one character's development and follows that development through time. In practice, such an essay often proceeds chronologically, with a section devoted to each character. A relational approach instead folds time into space. It sets chronology aside for a moment and treats relationships as positions in a field in order to uncover what Simmel calls “social synthesis.” Rather than merely recording two people's separate experiences, for example, it can examine the roles each occupies in their romance. Yet the “social synthesis” of an ACG work is neither just a structural product of the culture industry nor cold material for analysis. Relationships have emotional richness and an internal logic of their own. Unless we understand the people within them, we cannot grasp what makes a character feel real—or what the story does to us.

Relational analysis tries to supplement personal-history criticism, which can remain narrowly biographical, and database analysis, which can reduce a character to a narrative function. By connecting situations in ACG works with situations in real life, it offers another way to read fictional characters. **The two installments of this essay examine, respectively, relationships among characters within a work and relationships between a work and its audience.**

## I. Toward Relations Among Characters—Triads and Their Variations

If relational analysis begins from the idea that ACG works transform and sublimate real life, then formal accounts of real social life can be adapted to fictional relationships. Simmel's formal sociology offers one suggestive model. He abstracts basic social forms from concrete life, distinguishing his approach both from accounts that begin with individual action and from those that begin with large-scale social functions. His aim is to express society almost as a geometry.

Within this framework, Simmel identifies the most basic unit of social analysis: **the triad**. It can be understood as the beginning of society. Philosophies of the subject begin with the individual and explore the inner self, but the individual alone cannot account for the loss of autonomy that comes with living among others. A dyad does introduce interaction between subjects, yet its two members have only one another. Their bond remains private and particular, without the features that would allow it to stand for social life more broadly; in that sense, it is a romantic illusion. Simmel's formal point is that the arrival of a third person changes the dyad qualitatively. Each member is now answerable to two others and must adjust one bond in light of the other, rather than giving either relationship complete priority. Families show how this works: excessive intimacy between a child and either parent can test the parents' emotional compact, while some distance helps preserve all three positions.

The psychoanalytic step that follows is my extension of Simmel, not part of his taxonomy. I read the incompleteness of each bond through **desire as lack**: because no one relationship can wholly satisfy or absorb a member, desire remains open and the third position continues to matter. On this reading, lack is not a universal law derived from the family example; it is one way to explain the tension that keeps the fictional triangles below in motion. Simmel himself classifies third parties as mediators, arbitrators, beneficiaries (*tertius gaudens*), and those who divide and rule (*divide et impera*). I will not carry that taxonomy into ACG. Instead, I want to propose a different model for three-person relations in fiction.

### (1) The Love-Triangle Model—A Special Form of the Triad

Love triangles in ACG works, especially school romances, are a perennial topic among viewers, **but the limited set of gender-marketed romances discussed here organizes them in different ways**. The male-oriented heterosexual examples often revolve around the bonds between one male protagonist and two heroines. A second male rival does not automatically make a plot *netorare*, or NTR. In fan usage, NTR describes a protagonist losing a lover or desired partner to someone else, with sexual or emotional betrayal and displacement at the center; it overlaps with a rival-male plot only when that experience is central. The one-man, two-woman arrangement offers a voyeuristic audience position: it invites viewers to identify with the protagonist, share his pleasure, and treat the heroines as his objects of desire. Viewers who accept that invitation may form shipping camps—factions supporting competing pairings—around the rival heroines. At the same time, those bonds obstruct emotional progress, keeping the plot in suspension until the end approaches. Then a symbolic marker from the past—an object or event—returns at the climax and finally compels the protagonist to choose. **In this formula, the love triangle is therefore a peculiar kind of triad. Its ending often turns the relation among three people into a new triangle of “the couple – society (the world).” The plot stops there, leaving us with the empty promise that “the prince and princess lived happily ever after.”**

![Three schoolgirls are separated across broad steps beside the water: one stands at left, one walks in the center, and one crouches at right](attachments/roof-archive/cv1660015/02-three-girls-outdoors.jpg)

**I want to begin with a love-triangle formula that some writers know extremely well.** Male protagonists in these examples differ in personality, but many refuse to accept the limit imposed by choosing. The essay uses **perverse structure** loosely for that psychoanalytic pattern, not for sexual deviance or a clinical diagnosis; likewise, **melancholic** and **depressive** describe literary temperaments that turn frustration inward, not diagnosed illnesses. In practice, these protagonists rarely relinquish either object of desire, whether they play the passive avoider or the proactive nice guy. Hachiman Hikigaya in *My Youth Romantic Comedy Is Wrong, As I Expected* represents the first temperament. He pretends that “youth” is already dead and **gives up his own agency and desire—figuratively “castrating” himself—to preserve harmony around him**. Here castration means accepting or staging a loss of agency, not physical harm. This both blocks his narcissistic fantasy and prolongs his emotional investment in Yui Yuigahama and Yukino Yukinoshita. Haruki Kitahara in *White Album 2* represents the second temperament. He lavishes the same care on both women and, through apparently decisive action, immerses himself in the pleasure of the relationship itself. The discussion here refers to the original visual novel: Setsuna Ogiso's sudden confession after the school festival becomes a traumatic kernel, throwing Haruki's emerging feelings for Kazusa Touma into disarray, and the player experiences that conflict through his repeated hesitation.

[fig] Setsuna Ogiso (left) and Kazusa Touma (right) in *White Album 2*. Their face-to-face standoff gives the rivalry between Haruki's two objects of desire a relation of its own.

![Two women face each other on a snowy city street at night](attachments/roof-archive/cv1660015/03-two-girls-at-night.jpg)

The two heroines are even more revealing. In the male-oriented examples discussed here, women are often drawn as objects of desire. Setting aside **database analyses of *moe* traits**—the reusable visual and personality cues that invite affection for a character—relational form tends to place one heroine **near** the male protagonist and the other **far** from him, creating an uneven **psychological distance**. I will call the nearby heroine the “**girl-next-door type**” and the distant heroine the “**mysterious type**.” The girl next door seems naturally approachable and worth protecting. Structurally, she belongs to the ordinary here and now and rarely crosses the bounds of everyday life. The mysterious heroine, by contrast, acquires sublimity through distance. An aura of cool pride or composure surrounds her; because she remains elusive, desire cannot be satisfied, and she may bring danger into the story. **In this model, contrasts in the two heroines' ways of life and social backgrounds make the triangle unstable.**

This taxonomy also resembles an openly sexist binary in everyday commentary about actresses. A petite, charming woman with an innocent appearance becomes the girl next door, someone supposedly fit to be a **wife**; a talented, sexy, enigmatic actress becomes the imagined **mistress**. The Chinese television drama *The Happy Life of the Boss* depicts both. Mei Hao, who takes a job at a foot-massage parlor after her family falls apart, is the girl-next-door type; the career woman Xin Wen is the mysterious type. The “boss,” played by Fan Wei, gravely objectifies the two women circling him and calls Mei Hao “the shoe that fits better,” a metaphor that exposes both male control and marital compromise. In the school romances considered here, the fantasy of “pure love” softens that chauvinist rhetoric without removing the structure. The male protagonist, his childhood friend, and the school beauty form a familiar triangle; when the plot begins as *boy meets girl*, the childhood friend is often the one sacrificed.

[fig] Yukino (left), Yui (center), and Hachiman (right) in *My Youth Romantic Comedy Is Wrong, As I Expected*. The lineup does not literally put Hachiman between the heroines; the triangle lies in their competing attachments.

![Yukino Yukinoshita stands at left, Yui Yuigahama at center, and Hachiman Hikigaya at right in My Youth Romantic Comedy Is Wrong, As I Expected](attachments/roof-archive/cv1660015/04-oregairu-trio.jpg)

For the girl-next-door type, **psychological proximity is the key. Because the protagonist takes her familiar presence for granted, she may slip from his conscious attention.** She need not be gentle; she may even have markedly unconventional traits, appearing *denpa*—a fan term for someone eccentric and detached from ordinary social cues—or almost expressionless. In these examples, however, she occupies a marginal position, or becomes an object of scrutiny, within a larger social group. That indirect placement highlights her special bond with the protagonist. Her family's material circumstances may also resemble his. She may belong to a family whose members depend on one another to get by, or to an intact household with a caring mother and humorous father. Either arrangement gives her an ordinary, intimate love that makes her feel close to the protagonist. Yui Yuigahama plays this role in *My Youth Romantic Comedy Is Wrong, As I Expected*. Although cheerful, helpful, and popular with boys, she remains somewhat peripheral in Yumiko Miura's group and finds the labor of reading the room exhausting. Her own nuclear family is warm and harmonious. Noe Isurugi, the “chicken girl” in *true tears*, is likewise marginalized for her *denpa* qualities, while her close bond with her brother gives her a source of care. The same pattern applies to Muxin in *Gaokao.Love.100Days*. These family circumstances and social positions mark each character as “ordinary” within the model.

![A pink-haired schoolgirl raises both hands beside a dark-haired schoolgirl with glasses and folded arms](attachments/roof-archive/cv1660015/05-two-schoolgirls.jpg)

By contrast, **the mysterious type remains at a greater psychological distance. She startles the protagonist like a striking work of art, becoming the object of his desiring gaze and, often, his image of the sublime.** In the examples here, such characters tend to be exceptionally talented or academically accomplished. Their cool pride or composure gives them another kind of marginality: excellence makes them inaccessible. They are also often affluent but subject to unexpected pressures at home. A stern parent may stand in for what Lacanian theory calls the **Big Other**: the social authority whose rules and expectations the subject has internalized. Alternatively, a broken family may force the heroine to become self-reliant and care for others too early. This background leaves a deliberate opening in the seemingly untouchable heroine's emotional life, allowing the protagonist to intervene. Yukino Yukinoshita in *My Youth Romantic Comedy Is Wrong, As I Expected*, Hiromi Yuasa in *true tears*, and Xiaohan in *Gaokao.Love.100Days* all fit this pattern.

The heroine-centered love triangles considered here—one woman and two men—depart somewhat from that male-centered model. The heroine is **not automatically required by the formula to renounce her own agency or desire**, so her personality has more room to vary. She may **masquerade**—perform the femininity expected of an object of male desire—and take the role of girl next door or mysterious woman, as Hagu does in *Honey and Clover*. Or she may make the men her objects of desire and sort them into types, occupying the contradictory positions of independent spectator and embodied participant at once. The examples suggest more room for a heroine's desire and aesthetic judgment, though this small corpus cannot establish a general historical shift in women's autonomy. These heroines may have sly private motives and genuine flaws. They are by no means uniformly gentle, yet they are seldom staged primarily as an eye-catching **spectacle** for the viewer's gaze. Even so, some heroine-centered romances reproduce familiar heterosexual dynamics and give romance a passive, expectant form. The heroine rushes about and fumbles through love, and that very awkwardness draws the attention of her objects of desire.

[fig] An unidentified trio from the original publication. Its composition places the woman physically between two men, literalizing the heroine-centered triangle discussed here.

![A woman stands between two men against a red background dotted with stars](attachments/roof-archive/cv1660015/06-three-characters-red-background.jpg)

In the named heroine-centered examples, the two men can likewise be divided by psychological distance. The nearby man is the “boy-next-door type.” He tends to be active and outgoing: noisy, flippant with others, and completely at ease in everyday banter with the heroine. Yet he follows the conventions of that banter while burying his desire for her, disclosing it only indirectly to the reader. His appeal lies in the pain concealed by the jokes. The “mysterious boy,” by comparison, is more idealized. Some have no defining trauma; they are mysterious simply for mystery's sake. He may be a bookish young man, a teacher, a rational geek, or an imperious CEO—commanding within his field without sacrificing elegance. Beside the boy next door's occasional vulnerability, the mysterious man looks exceptionally capable and assertive, yet becomes strangely sexless at decisive moments. In *Daytime Shooting Star*, Suzume Yosano's seatmate Daiki Mamura and her first love, the teacher Satsuki Shishio, occupy these two positions. In *Shortcake Cake*, Riku Mizuhara seems flippant but is steadfast in love, as loyal as a dog, while the cultivated bearing of the bookish Chiaki Kasadera makes him mysterious early in the manga. I nevertheless find Ten Serizawa's transformation from alert, clever heroine to a girl completely consumed by love faintly unsettling—especially when she decides to improve her appearance so that she can be worthy of Riku.

[fig] Ten Serizawa appears in the foreground of this *Shortcake Cake* cover, with Riku Mizuhara and Chiaki Kasadera behind her. The composition turns the essay's two competing male types into a visible triangle.

![Japanese cover of Shortcake Cake showing Ten Serizawa in front of Riku Mizuhara and Chiaki Kasadera](attachments/roof-archive/cv1660015/07-school-romance-manga-cover.jpg)

The heterosexual love triangles discussed here are thus both anchored and restrained by their central protagonist. They treat romantic love as exclusive and often quasi-proprietary. Because the two heroines—or two male leads—love the same person, their own relationship risks rupture. Yet the central protagonist and the rivals' existing social ties require jealousy to remain unspoken. Anyone tempted to attack a rival must ask, “What will the person I love think of me?” They may also fear destroying a friendship they once genuinely shared. Whatever its outcome, the final confrontation dissolves the three-way relationship. The loser returns to the place of the “Other”: the excluded outsider beyond the surviving couple. To postpone that trauma, the rivals maintain a delicate arrangement of compensation and gratification, performing harmony in the name of “preserving a beautiful relationship.” They may remain locked in a psychological contest for a long time, accumulating impulses to let go, hate, reconcile, and give thanks. One may betray their own desire and withdraw for the protagonist's sake, saying, “As long as they're happy”—a self-punishing move that this essay reads in melancholic terms. At other moments, the beloved's kindness makes that same rival feel uniquely valued. This intimacy between rivals can also support a queer counter-reading that viewers aligned with the central protagonist may miss. The most genre-disrupting conclusion would not pair the protagonist with either desired object at all; it would remove the protagonist and allow the two rivals to love each other.

As I said at the beginning of this section, a love-triangle plot is driven by the changing contest among its three members, and that relation dissolves at the end. The protagonist finally faces reality and accepts their feelings. In this formula, one object of desire must leave, returning to the undifferentiated mass of people outside the couple. The result is a division between **“the world of two” and “the real world.”** **In another class of stories, however, that division is only the beginning.**

### (2) The Byakuyakō (Fugitive-Lovers) Model—The General Form of the Triad

We can now turn to a more general relational form. Once two people become intimate early in a story, they must confront the imbalance between their bond and a cruel reality. The central question for both is how to live in society. This returns us to the underlying point: **a triad need not join three concrete people; it can join three positions.** Those positions are occupied not simply by biological individuals but by people in symbolic roles.

Some works have only two central protagonists, bound by an extraordinary intimacy as lovers or sworn companions. The third party is not one specific intruder but politics, society, a prevailing atmosphere, or the entire world. This third term tests the pair and drives the plot. The model readily produces emotional devastation, but it also brings the protagonists' charisma into full view. They are rarely “everyday people”: recognizable individuals whose words and behavior are distinctive without being extraordinary. Instead, some degree of psychological distortion, an unusual, repressive, or broken family, and a horrifying experience such as kidnapping or murder become defining traits. “Fugitive lovers” or “Romeo and Juliet” would be more general names, but I prefer the **Byakuyakō model**, after Keigo Higashino's novel *Journey Under the Midnight Sun* (*Byakuyakō*). For this essay, the compact Japanese title serves as shorthand for the relationship embodied by Ryo Kirihara's meticulousness and Yukiho's cruelty. Stories of this type push the contradiction between “two people” and “society” to its limit. They wrap individual heroism in tragedy, offering vicarious satisfaction in place of banal everyday life. *Journey Under the Midnight Sun* and *Kurai Heya* (*The Dark Room*) exemplify this cruel fugitive-lovers narrative.

[fig] An unidentified silhouette illustration from the original publication. By reducing the pair to linked figures against an empty field, it renders society as an absent but surrounding third term.

![Silhouettes of a boy and girl holding hands against a pale background](attachments/roof-archive/cv1660015/08-two-silhouettes.jpg)

Why do such stories move us? Baudelaire supplies the aesthetic starting point: literature can give figures at society's margins a defiant dignity and make their longing for redemption or revolt formally compelling. I extend that observation to the Byakuyakō model through **abjection**. The surrounding social order first produces the pair as part of its world, then casts them out as a contaminating remainder. Their intimacy can therefore appear to resist the same order that excludes them. Giorgio Agamben's *homo sacer* names a narrower legal-political extreme: a person left exposed to power while denied ordinary protection. Not every fictional outsider occupies that position; the analogy applies only when the story's third term makes the pair both vulnerable to social power and unable to claim its protection. What becomes aesthetically attractive, then, is not marginalization itself but the pair's refusal to let exclusion define their bond.

[fig] An unidentified pair from the original publication. The sheltering pose visualizes the refuge that Byakuyakō narratives locate inside an embattled two-person bond.

![A masked white-haired boy shelters a black-haired girl against a blue background](attachments/roof-archive/cv1660015/09-paired-characters-blue.jpg)

When such meanings reach the screen or popular fiction, of course, resistance is finally absorbed into fantasy. Yet these works preserve the same relational form. I want to explain how partners in the Byakuyakō model can move an audience while facing the Big Other's threats and temptations. The works considered here often contrast routine, socially mediated contact with an ordeal that only the pair shares. That contrast is a narrative arrangement, not proof that remote or commodity-mediated relationships are inherently thinner than face-to-face intimacy. Within the arrangement, enduring hardship together becomes a scarce privilege, and the old heroic spirit retains an aesthetic charge by crossing the limits of ordinary life. The protagonists' exceptional histories and the suffering imposed by the third term invite viewers aligned with the pair to treat endurance as proof of true love. Kitsch fantasy or not, that invitation helps explain how these stories solicit attachment to fictional characters.

[fig] Rachel Gardner and Zack in *Angels of Death*. Their mutual dependence inside a hostile facility is a particularly stark version of the Byakuyakō model's couple-against-the-world structure.

![Rachel Gardner and Zack from Angels of Death lean against one another on a red background](attachments/roof-archive/cv1660015/10-paired-characters-red.jpg)

Within the ACG examples considered here, school dramas and psychologically brutal thrillers realize the Byakuyakō model with different degrees of intensity, but the form remains similar. In a school drama, the third term may take the form of what *Rascal Does Not Dream of Bunny Girl Senpai* calls **Adolescence Syndrome**: a supernatural manifestation of a teenager's social or emotional crisis. In a thriller, a murder or kidnapping case can occupy the same structural position. Works such as *Usotsuki Mii-kun to Kowareta Maa-chan* (often rendered as *Lying Mii-kun and Broken Maa-chan*) and *CARNIVAL* blur the line between those genres. **These examples seldom require an elaborate symbolic order—the wider network of social laws, institutions, and norms. They also rarely show the couple struggling directly against politics.** School bullying, kidnapping, crime, and vaguely defined world-scale organizations common to *sekai-kei*—stories that magnify adolescent intimacy into stakes for the world while leaving institutions hazy—more often occupy the third position. I suspect that withholding political context helps these particular stories concentrate on psychological complexity and obsession.

[fig] Mai Sakurajima (left) and Sakuta Azusagawa (right) in *Rascal Does Not Dream of Bunny Girl Senpai*. The Japanese boxes give brief character profiles, including their names, while the smaller dialogue is incidental. No claim here depends on that wording: the image only orients the example of Adolescence Syndrome.

![Mai Sakurajima and Sakuta Azusagawa from Rascal Does Not Dream of Bunny Girl Senpai, with Japanese character-profile text](attachments/roof-archive/cv1660015/11-school-romance-manga-panel.jpg)

Whether the story is an emotionally brutal abuse narrative or a serious drama about existence, the couple in this model is often confined to a narrow time and place: a rooftop, warehouse, or sealed basement; a secret that you and I know but no one else does. The family is already one of the most explicit forms of symbolic order that such stories will acknowledge. This suggests a resistance to social analysis: **the work seems able to treat its attractive protagonists with gravity only by making the third term hazy.** How, then, can the appeal of the Byakuyakō model compensate for its lack of political imagination? The answer is difficult, but the cracks in some writers' depictions of the third term make the problem visible.

Romeo Tanaka's scripts, strictly speaking, have a sociological tension. Just when we are immersed in the unruly fantasy of “chūnibyō kids falling in love,” *AURA: Koga Maryuin's Last War* and *Yume Miru Kusuri: A Drug That Makes You Dream* land like blows to the head. Romeo's stories about bullying and chūnibyō—the adolescent fantasy that one possesses secret powers or a grand identity—do not address the symbolic order as a whole. But by introducing a concrete mediator, the bully, they also introduce contradictions from actual social relationships, creating **discordance**. *AURA* builds its Byakuyakō relation on Ichirō Satō and Ryōko Satō's shared chūnibyō sensibility. Yet Ichirō has already been bullied and mocked for his fantasies, while Ryōko is being bullied in the present. Symbolic order collides violently with chūnibyō's narcissistic fantasy. The rooftop resolution is highly romanticized, but it ends with Ryōko hoping to “be an ordinary girl”; the shared fantasy has not protected her from social injury. **From inside Ichirō and Ryōko's shared performance, the chūnibyō fantasy seems self-sufficient: it can cast them as heroes, assign evil to villains, and explain conflict on its own terms. Bullying cannot be absorbed into that private mythology without exposing the fantasy as a response to ordinary humiliation. That unassimilable pressure is what I mean here by the Lacanian Real; it is not identical with everyday reality as a whole.** Romeo puts that social pressure openly on display, hardly the safest strategy for a light-novel author. *AURA* neither keeps its Byakuyakō relation at the level of school violence, as *A Silent Voice* does, nor confines it to romantic fantasy, as *Love, Chunibyo & Other Delusions!* does. Its triad arises neither from ordinary conflict among characters nor from competition between romantic rivals, but from a clash between the pair's sustaining fantasy and the social injury that fantasy cannot explain away. It is too serious for pure chūnibyō fantasy and too extravagant for straightforward social criticism. Similar tensions recur across Romeo's work: even his earnest depictions of bleak social terrain remain mixed with incongruous moe elements. Why the Byakuyakō model so often ignores or muffles the social causes of its triad will require further thought.

**This unresolved third term also explains my earlier interest in a queer ending to the love triangle. If the two female rivals turn toward one another after the central protagonist drops out, the story need not simply replace one sealed pure-love couple with another. What I would call a “hard-edged yuri” version would carry the absent protagonist and the social pressures that structured the original triangle into the new dyad as an active third term. This is not a claim about yuri as a genre; it is a test implied by the model: does the new couple confront the forces around it, or erase them to preserve the fantasy of a self-sufficient love?**

## Conclusion

This essay has proposed a basic way to connect character relationships in ACG with social relationships. Rather than reconstructing personal histories or reducing characters to functions in a story, it focuses on the forms their relationships take. Drawing on Simmel's triad, I have examined two forms that recur in the ACG examples above: the love triangle and the Byakuyakō model. The love triangle is a special case because one member must leave at the end, turning the original field into “the couple – society.” When a new story begins from that arrangement, it produces what I call the Byakuyakō model. In the Japanese works discussed here, this model often removes or weakens politics and other forms of social mediation until a triad looks like a dyad. Explaining why will require analysis from other angles.
