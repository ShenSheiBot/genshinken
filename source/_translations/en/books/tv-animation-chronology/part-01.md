> **Translator’s note:** *An Accessible Chronology of Japanese TV Animation* was published in four installments in October–November 2018. References to “now,” current broadcasts, and recent events retain that standpoint.

If this series is going to begin,

it has to begin at the very beginning.

There is no particular agenda behind it, really: just a survey of Japanese TV animation from its birth to the present. I hope that, along the way, you will find a few things you like.

![Decorative gold stars](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-001.png)

Major events:

1953: The precursors of TV animation begin to appear, modeled on television commercials.

1958: Japan’s first color animated feature, *The White Snake*, is released.

7 March 1959 (Shōwa 34): The live-action *Astro Boy* begins broadcasting.

1962: Tatsunoko is founded.

In the meantime, Mushi Production is established in 1961 and begins adopting “limited animation” from the United States.

> **Supplement:** Unlike full animation, limited animation works with restricted time, personnel, detail, and numbers of frames, controlling backgrounds and character movement within the frame to reduce the time and money required.

1963: With the broadcast of *Astro Boy*, the first great animation boom begins. Newcomers to the industry and new productions multiply, and animation becomes a fixture of children’s conversations.

> **Supplement:** The black-and-white 1963 *Astro Boy* is not the 1980 version Chinese viewers usually remember from childhood.

1963: Studio Zero is founded.

1963: Japan’s first original TV animated series, *Ken, the Wild Boy*, begins broadcasting.

1964: Tokyo Movie is founded.

1965: Japan’s first color TV animated series, *Jungle Emperor Leo*, begins broadcasting.

Keywords for this period: Osamu Tezuka; Mushi Production; snack manufacturers; black-and-white television; the first experiments; *Astro Boy*.

14 July 1958: The first TV animation, *Mole’s Adventure*, airs. At fifty-three seconds long, it marks television animation’s entrance onto the stage of history.

![Television news image showing the title of Mole’s Adventure](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-002.jpg)

The footage used here comes from a television news report made when material from the film was discovered in 2013.

15 January 1960: *New Animation: Three Tales* begins broadcasting. Made up of three animated shorts totaling thirty minutes, it is the first television program in Japanese history filmed using chroma key.

(In other words, the first supplier of blue-screen meme footage.)

![A child’s face from Three Tales](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-003.jpg)

1961: *Instant History* begins its run of shorts, Japan’s first TV animation series. True to its name, each episode lasts one minute: the oldest “instant-noodle anime,” over before your noodles are ready.

![Still from Instant History](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-004.jpg)

Budget and production-schedule constraints mean that no other animated series airs in 1962.

1 January 1963: *Astro Boy* begins broadcasting, 193 episodes in all (including seventeen reruns). Japan’s first true regularly broadcast half-hour TV animation series, and its first homegrown robot anime.

![Astro Boy title image](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-005.jpg)

7 April 1963: *Galaxy Boy Troop* airs, 92 episodes. A puppet animation based on an original work by Osamu Tezuka, its premise even influences the later immortal classic *Space Battleship Yamato*. Interestingly, its premiere date, 7 April, is also the date on which the battleship Yamato sank.

![Puppets from Galaxy Boy Troop](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-006.jpg)

4 September 1963: *Sennin Buraku* (“Village of Immortals”) airs, 23 episodes. Japan’s earliest late-night animation. Its source manga holds the Japanese record for the longest serial by a single artist, at 2,861 installments.

![Still from Sennin Buraku](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-007.jpg)

4 October 1963: The Japanese–American puppet animation *The Adventures of Pinocchio* airs. From 20 December, it is broadcast together with another puppet series, *Shisukon Ōji*.

![Puppet Pinocchio on a stage](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-008.jpg)

20 October 1963: *Tetsujin 28* airs, 84 episodes. Three months after those eighty-four finish, a second, thirteen-episode run begins, bringing the total to 97. The earliest giant-robot animation in Japanese history, it has a profound influence on later mecha anime. (Go Nagai: He means me.)

![Tetsujin 28 in a city street](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-009.jpg)

7 November 1963: *8 Man* airs, 56 episodes.

An ordinary adaptation of an SF manga, nothing special in itself, it has the misfortune to become a casualty of the series of industry fiascos that follows.

![8 Man running](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-010.jpg)

25 November 1963: *Ken, the Wild Boy* airs, 86 episodes. Toei’s first TV animation production, and Japan’s first original TV animated series.

![Ken, the Wild Boy, moving through the trees](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-011.jpg)

20 December 1963: *Shisukon Ōji* (“Prince Cisco”) airs, 15 episodes. A puppet animation, and the first animation to use tie-in marketing. (The source manga was a collaboration between Fujiko Fujio and a snack company, about a little hero who transforms by eating snacks.)

![Shisukon Ōji manga covers](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-012.jpg)

21 January 1964: *Zero Fighter Hayato* airs, 38 episodes. Short of money, this animation uses special-effects filming techniques. It consumes most of Mushi Production’s and Toho’s energies at the time, nearly ruining another work, *Big X*. Out of filial respect for Daddy, it changes the “enemy country’s” name, and sets off the great wave of war manga in the sixties.

![An aircraft from Zero Fighter Hayato](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-013.jpg)

7 June 1964: *A Little Ninja, Fujimaru* airs. Originally called *Ishimaru of the Wind*, it changes its name to Fujimaru to suck up to sponsor Fujisawa Pharmaceutical (now Astellas Pharma): “Fuji” uses the same character as in Fujisawa.

![Still from A Little Ninja, Fujimaru](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-014.jpg)

3 August 1964: *Big X* airs. This poor victim of *Zero Fighter Hayato* makes a stark contrast in animation quality with the concurrently airing *Astro Boy*. Even most of its film ends up lost as a result; at present, only a total of 59 episodes can be confirmed.

![Characters from Big X](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-015.jpg)

3 January 1965: *New Treasure Island* airs, one episode. The manga on which it is based was Osamu Tezuka’s debut long-form work. Intended as a long-running animated serial, it apparently ends up a one-off because of questions over authorship rights and the allocation of copyright.[^treasure]

[fig] *New Treasure Island* (nope).

![New Treasure Island music-video title card, with the band silhouetted on steps](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-016.jpg)

![An animal character looking through a telescope in New Treasure Island](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-017.jpg)

7 January 1965: *Super Jetter, the Boy from the Future* airs. An original animation TBS produces after learning a painful lesson from the overseas-rights problems with *8 Man*—a tragedy brought on by not understanding English when signing the contract.

![Super Jetter opening credits](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-018.jpg)

1 February 1965: *Space Patrol Hopper* airs, 44 episodes. Partway through, it is renamed *Patrol Hopper: Uchūkko Jun* (“Space Kid Jun”).

![Still from Space Patrol Hopper](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-019.jpg)

4 April 1965: *Dolphin Prince* airs, three episodes. Japan’s first color animation broadcast on television, but its experimental nature means it does not continue as a series. Together with *Go! Marine Kid*, it later lays the groundwork for 1969’s *Marine Boy*.

[fig] The opening sequence of *Marine Boy*.

![Marine Boy opening sequence](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-020.jpg)

8 April 1965: *PiPi the Alien* airs, 52 episodes. Japan’s first production to combine live-action and animated performers.

![PiPi the Alien credits over a live-action background](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-021.jpg)

4 May 1965: *Space Boy Soran* airs, 96 episodes. Not much to say about the work itself; it has a small part in the W3 affair coming up later.

![Space Boy Soran opening credits](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-022.jpg)

8 May 1965: *Space Ace* airs, 52 episodes.

![Space Ace title image](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-023.jpg)

31 May 1965: *Kaitō Pride* (“Phantom Thief Pride”) airs (date uncertain), 23 episodes. Originally black-and-white; the images here are from the recolored export version, which was also renamed *Dr. Zen*.

![Color still from Dr. Zen, the export version of Kaitō Pride](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-024.jpg)

3 June 1965: *Prince Planet* airs. Like *Tetsujin 28*, another victim of the Glico opening song: repeated cries of “Glico!” are shoehorned into the lyrics. Imagine watching *Boonie Bears* when someone suddenly turns up and screams “Pleasant Goat!” for half a minute.

![Prince Planet flying](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-025.jpg)

6 June 1965: *W3* (*Wonder Three*, also known as *The Amazing Three*) airs, 52 episodes. The W here is read “Wonder”; this is Mushi Production’s third work. Nothing remarkable about the work itself. During the manga’s serialization, Mushi Production could not stand the overlap between its own *Number 7* and rival productions such as *Rainbow Sentai Robin* and *Space Boy Soran*. A key employee left just then, leading Osamu Tezuka to suspect collusion between the sponsors and the publisher. He moved *W3* from *Weekly Shōnen Magazine* to *Weekly Shōnen Sunday*, costing the former a huge number of readers. Its publisher, Kodansha, then brought heavy pressure to bear on Tezuka and Mushi Production, indirectly leading to the studio’s bankruptcy. This is the famous W3 affair.[^w3]

![W3 title and credits](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-026.jpg)

29 August 1965: *Obake no Q-tarō* (“Q-tarō the Ghost”) airs, 96 episodes. It sweeps away the ratings slump caused by an oversupply of SF animation, producing a ratings miracle for everyday-life animation. Its later theme song, “Oba-Q Ondo,” sells six million copies, a sales record no one has managed to beat to this day. It pioneers both anime ondo dance songs and the voice-actor singer, in the person of Machiko Soga. The earlier opening, “Obake no Q-tarō,” is also a member of the million-seller club.

![Obake no Q-tarō opening credits](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-027.jpg)

6 October 1965: *Jungle Emperor Leo* airs, 52 episodes. The first domestically produced Japanese color TV animation to broadcast a complete run. Its influence is such that people are still finding fault with *The Lion King* over it today.

![Leo running in Jungle Emperor Leo](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-028.jpg)

1 November 1965: *Hustle Punch* airs, 26 episodes.

![Character and song lyrics from Hustle Punch](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-029.jpg)

14 December 1965: *Tatakae! Osper* (“Fight! Osper”) airs, 53 episodes.

![Osper standing on a rock](attachments/wechat/hnI6Pa5irWyetGd339z5AQ/body-030.jpg)

On the whole, animation in this period is still taking its first steps in black-and-white. Most works germinate slowly in the shadow of monopoly and snack and pharmaceutical sponsors. Animators are still feeling their way, exploring and observing, moving forward under the burden of the name “television manga.” SF is all the rage, laying solid foundations for the science-fiction animation boom to come.

[^treasure]: **Translator’s note:** These are two different works sharing a title. The 1965 television animation adapts Stevenson with animal characters, not Tezuka’s debut manga. See [Tezuka Productions’ account](https://tezukaosamu.net/en/anime/52.html).

[^w3]: **Translator’s note:** The publishing dispute is documented; the bankruptcy connection asserted here is a separate causal claim. [The Tezuka archive’s reconstruction](https://tezukaosamu.net/jp/mushi/201011/column.html) recounts the dispute, including conflicting recollections, but does not establish that connection.
