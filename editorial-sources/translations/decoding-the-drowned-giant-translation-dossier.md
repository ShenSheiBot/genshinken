## Discourse map

- Objective: argue that the giant in Ballard's “The Drowned Giant” is most productively read not as a simple ecological symbol but as an image of ancient greatness, classical wisdom, and the remembered limits of modern humanity.
- Movement: Ballard's biography and “inner space” poetics; comparison with *Moby-Dick* and *The Metamorphosis* to isolate memory and forgetting as the decisive clue; construction of the giant/dwarf metaphor; confirmation through Swift, Plato, and the Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns.
- Major turns and qualifications: ecological readings are not denied but treated as insufficient; the comparison with *Gulliver's Travels* does not license a full political-regime allegory; the giant is great yet physically vulnerable; forgetting is presented as something produced by modern dismemberment and reinterpretation rather than mere decay.
- Pronoun or omitted-subject dependencies: “they” in the spectator passages consistently refers to the modern onlookers/dwarfs unless a sentence explicitly shifts to police or scholars; the narrator Steven remains the exception who remembers.
- Recurring wording: giant / dwarf; ancients / moderns; greatness; soul; memory / forgetting; dismemberment; distortion / misreading; science; modernity; the classical world.
- Ending function: the essay closes by importing Bloom's giant-and-dwarf image to restate its warning against complacently assuming intimacy with greatness.

## Voice card

- Genre and register: interpretive criticism with a political-philosophy frame; argumentative but explicitly speculative in places.
- Sentence rhythm: long analytical paragraphs punctuated by short rhetorical questions and repeated “if … then …” interpretive moves.
- First-person and reader address: frequent inclusive “we” used to guide inference; claims are often softened with “perhaps,” “seems,” and “we have reason to think.”
- Technical density: medium-high; literary comparison, intellectual history, Plato, Swift, modernity, science, and allegory overlap.
- Polemic and uncertainty: the essay is skeptical of scientism and modern self-satisfaction but repeatedly marks the danger of overinterpretation. Preserve both the polemic and the hedging.
- English treatment: readable critical prose with the Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns as the stable historical term; do not strengthen speculative claims into source facts.
- Japanese treatment: contemporary critical-essay である register; use the established Japanese Ballard title 『溺れた巨人』 and avoid syntactic calques from Chinese.

## Source discrepancies and quotation routing

- Footnote 4 in the Roof source cites James Naughtie's 2008 *Miracles of Life* interview, while the quoted Enlightenment wording in the body is attested in Zulfikar Abbany's *The Age* interview of 1 November 2003. The public translations preserve the historical footnote record rather than silently rewriting it; the target prose is phrased from the verified 2003 interview without reproducing an extended copyrighted passage.
- The 1969 Storm interview page supports Ballard's “inner space” formulation and the emphasis on the point where mind and external reality meet. The target prose therefore treats that claim as verified rather than as a back-translation from Chinese.
- The 1974 Louit interview archive supports the classification of “The Drowned Giant” as fantasy/allegory. Again, the target editions paraphrase the verified idea rather than inventing a line-by-line original quotation.
- Footnotes 5–12 and 15 point to a 1978 printed Ballard collection and exact pages. An authoritative full text matching those pages was not established in this pass. English therefore does not turn the Chinese renderings back into purported Ballard quotations. It uses indirect discourse for the cited wording while preserving the source's page citations. Japanese likewise translates the essay's claims directly from the Roof Chinese rather than pretending to reproduce the published Japanese translation by Asakura Hisashi.
- Tokyo Sogensha verifies the established Japanese title 『溺れた巨人』 and credits 浅倉久志 as translator of the 1971 collection. That published Japanese translation is bibliographic evidence only and is not copied or treated as the base text for quoted passages.
- The exact English source behind the long final Bloom passage was not located. The English edition converts it to attributed indirect paraphrase to avoid ghost back-translation into its known source language. The Japanese edition translates the Roof Chinese passage as a relay quotation; this relationship remains internal evidence rather than a reader-facing process note.
- The Roof source repeatedly writes “格利佛” alongside “格列佛”; both targets normalize the work identity to *Gulliver's Travels* / 『ガリヴァー旅行記』 without altering the Chinese canonical.
- The Roof source contains several obvious local wording repetitions and slips (for example “丧失了巨人的知识丧失巨人的知识”). The target editions render the recoverable intended proposition once; no public cleanup note is inserted.
