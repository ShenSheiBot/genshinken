## Discourse map

- Objective: use *Flavors of Youth* to argue that modern romantic relations and recognizable “everyday landscapes” depend on material, spatial, institutional, and symbolic conditions rather than existing as timeless universals.
- First movement: modern “romance” becomes socially possible when schools, parks, inns, shared student identity, and modern institutions create legitimate spaces in which young men and women can form a recognized relationship.
- Film bridge: Li Haoling's Shanghai story binds first love to shikumen housing and the intimate neighborhood relations that disappeared as residents moved into newer apartments.
- Second movement: following Kojin Karatani's “discovery of landscape,” the essay argues that everyday landscapes are produced through signification and can feed back into lived reality.
- Comparative turn: Japan's relatively standardized physical and social environments make a shared symbolic repertoire of youth easier to reproduce, while China's regional, generational, infrastructural, educational, and class differences frustrate a single nationally resonant image of youth.
- Ending function: what sounds like criticism of the film becomes a wider question about whether Chinese cultural producers and audiences can discover and reproduce their own plural everyday landscapes rather than merely viewing imported “Japanese taste.”
